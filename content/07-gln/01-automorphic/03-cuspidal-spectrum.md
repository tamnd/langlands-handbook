## The cuspidal spectrum

This section decomposes the space of cusp forms with a fixed central character into irreducible representations of $GL_n(\mathbb{A})$, and it writes each of them as a restricted tensor product of local representations. The result is the notion of a cuspidal automorphic representation, the main object of the volume. For $GL_2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ the example is the representation $\pi_\Delta$ generated by $\varphi_\Delta$. Its component at $\infty$ is the discrete series of weight 12, and its component at each prime is an unramified principal series.

### The space of square integrable cusp forms

Let $\omega$ be a unitary character of $F^\times \backslash \mathbb{A}^\times$. Write $X = Z(\mathbb{A}) GL_n(F) \backslash GL_n(\mathbb{A})$, a space of finite volume by Theorem 1.1.7.

**Definition 1.3.1 (the cuspidal spectrum).** Let $L^2(\omega)$ be the space of measurable functions $\varphi$ on $GL_n(F) \backslash GL_n(\mathbb{A})$ with $\varphi(zg) = \omega(z) \varphi(g)$ for $z \in Z(\mathbb{A})$ and $\int_X |\varphi|^2 < \infty$, modulo the functions that vanish almost everywhere. The *cuspidal subspace* $L^2_0(\omega)$ is the subspace of the $\varphi$ with $\varphi_P = 0$ almost everywhere for each proper parabolic subgroup $P$ defined over $F$. The group $GL_n(\mathbb{A})$ acts on both spaces by right translation, $(R(h)\varphi)(g) = \varphi(gh)$.

Lean: not stated yet

The constant term of an $L^2$ function is defined almost everywhere by Fubini's theorem, since $U_P(F) \backslash U_P(\mathbb{A})$ is compact. The representation $R$ is unitary, and $L^2_0(\omega)$ is a closed invariant subspace. By Proposition 1.2.7, every cusp form with central character $\omega$ lies in $L^2_0(\omega)$.

**Theorem 1.3.2 (discreteness of the cuspidal spectrum).** The representation $R$ on $L^2_0(\omega)$ is a Hilbert direct sum of irreducible unitary representations, and each irreducible representation occurs with finite multiplicity.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof sketch.* The theorem is due to Gelfand, Graev and Piatetski-Shapiro, and to Langlands in general. Let $f$ be a smooth function on $GL_n(\mathbb{A})$ with $f(zg) = \overline{\omega(z)} f(g)$ that has compact support modulo $Z(\mathbb{A})$. The operator $R(f) \varphi(x) = \int_{Z(\mathbb{A}) \backslash GL_n(\mathbb{A})} f(y) \varphi(xy) \, dy$ on $L^2(\omega)$ is an integral operator with the kernel
$$K_f(x, y) = \sum_{\gamma \in Z(F) \backslash GL_n(F)} f(x^{-1} \gamma y).$$
On $L^2_0(\omega)$ one may replace $K_f$ by the kernel minus its constant terms in $y$. The argument of the rapid decay proposition of Section 1.2 shows that this modified kernel is bounded on $\mathfrak{S} \times \mathfrak{S}$. As $X$ has finite volume, $R(f)$ is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on $L^2_0(\omega)$, so it is compact. Take self-adjoint such $f_m$ whose supports shrink to $Z(\mathbb{A})$, so that $R(f_m) \to 1$ strongly. The spectral theorem for the compact operators $R(f_m)$ shows that each closed invariant subspace $V \ne 0$ contains an irreducible subspace, and Zorn's lemma gives the decomposition. An irreducible $\pi$ of multiplicity $m$ gives each eigenvalue $\lambda \ne 0$ of $\pi(f_m)$ a multiplicity at least $m$ in $R(f_m)$, so $m$ is finite. $\square$

The orthogonal complement of $L^2_0(\omega)$ in $L^2(\omega)$ is described by Eisenstein series. Langlands proved that it is the sum of the residual spectrum, which is discrete, and of a continuous spectrum, which is a direct integral of Eisenstein series. For $n = 2$ the residual spectrum consists of the functions $\chi(\det g)$ with $\chi^2 = \omega$. For $GL_n$ Moeglin and Waldspurger proved that the residual spectrum consists of the Speh representations, which are built from cuspidal representations of $GL_m$ for divisors $m$ of $n$. Chapter 2 uses one of them, the constant function, in Example 2.1.3.

### Cuspidal automorphic representations

**Definition 1.3.3 (cuspidal automorphic representation).** A *cuspidal automorphic representation* of $GL_n(\mathbb{A})$ with unitary central character is an irreducible representation $\pi$ of $GL_n(\mathbb{A})$ that occurs in $L^2_0(\omega)$ for some $\omega$. In general, a cuspidal automorphic representation is a twist $\pi \otimes |\det|^s$, $s \in \mathbb{C}$, of one with unitary central character. We often identify $\pi$ with the space of its smooth $K$-finite vectors, a subspace of $\mathcal{A}_0$ that is a $(\mathfrak{g}, K_\infty) \times GL_n(\mathbb{A}_f)$-module.

Every cusp form in $\mathcal{A}_0$ with central character $\omega$ is a finite sum of vectors in cuspidal automorphic representations. Indeed, its translates by $K$ span a space of finite dimension in $L^2_0(\omega)$, and its $Z(\mathfrak{g})$-finiteness bounds the infinitesimal characters that occur. The cuspidal automorphic representations of $GL_1$ are the unitary Hecke characters, since $L^2_0(\omega) = L^2(\omega)$ is the line spanned by $\omega$ itself for $n = 1$.

An irreducible smooth representation $\pi_v$ of $GL_n(F_v)$ at a finite place is *admissible* if its space of vectors fixed by each compact open subgroup has finite dimension. It is *unramified* if it has a nonzero vector fixed by $K_v = GL_n(\mathcal{O}_v)$. Section 1.4 shows that this vector is unique up to a scalar. At an infinite place the analogue is an irreducible admissible $(\mathfrak{g}_v, K_v)$-module.

**Theorem 1.3.4 (tensor product theorem).** Let $\pi$ be a cuspidal automorphic representation of $GL_n(\mathbb{A})$. For each place $v$ there is an irreducible admissible representation $\pi_v$ of $GL_n(F_v)$, a $(\mathfrak{g}_v, K_v)$-module at the infinite places, with these properties: $\pi_v$ is unramified for almost all $v$, and for a choice of nonzero $K_v$-fixed vectors $e_v \in \pi_v$ at these places,
$$\pi \cong \bigotimes_v{}' \, \pi_v,$$
the restricted tensor product with respect to the vectors $e_v$. The $\pi_v$ are determined by $\pi$ up to isomorphism.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof sketch.* This is the theorem of Flath, in the form of Bump, Chapter 3. First, $\pi$ is admissible: for a compact open $U$ and an irreducible representation $\tau$ of $K_\infty$, the $\tau$-isotypic part of $\pi^U$ has finite dimension, by a theorem of Harish-Chandra on the forms with a fixed level, $K_\infty$-type and $Z(\mathfrak{g})$-eigencharacter. Next, for a finite set $S$ of places, the space $\pi^{K^S}$ of the vectors fixed by $\prod_{v \notin S} K_v$ is an irreducible module of $\bigotimes_{v \in S} \mathcal{H}_v \otimes \bigotimes_{v \notin S} \mathcal{H}(G_v, K_v)$, where $\mathcal{H}_v$ is the full Hecke algebra of $G_v$. The spherical algebras are commutative, by Section 1.4, so they act by characters. An irreducible admissible module of a tensor product of two algebras with approximate units is a tensor product of irreducible admissible modules of the factors. Letting $S$ grow gives the restricted tensor product, and the same lemma gives the uniqueness. $\square$

**Proposition 1.3.5 (unramified components).** Let $\varphi \in \pi$ be a nonzero cusp form that is right invariant under $K(\mathfrak{n})$. Then $\pi_v$ is unramified at each finite place $v \nmid \mathfrak{n}$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The vector $\varphi$ is fixed by $K_v$ for $v \nmid \mathfrak{n}$. Write $\varphi$ as a finite sum of pure tensors $\otimes_v x_v$. The projection to $\pi_v$ along the other factors, followed by the averaging operator $\pi_v(1_{K_v})$, sends $\varphi$ to a nonzero vector, since $\pi(1_{K_v}) \varphi = \varphi$. So $\pi_v^{K_v} \ne 0$. $\square$

**Example 1.3.6 (the representation of a newform).** Let $f \in S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ be a newform and let $\pi_f$ be the representation of $GL_2(\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q})$ generated by $\varphi_f$ of Example 1.2.3. It is irreducible, a fact that Volume 5 proves with multiplicity one and the newform theory. Its component $\pi_{f, \infty}$ is the discrete series of weight $k$. For $p \nmid N$, $\pi_{f, p}$ is unramified, and Section 1.4 computes it from $a_p(f)$. For $p \| N$, $\pi_{f,p}$ is an unramified twist of the Steinberg representation. Thus $\pi_\Delta = \pi_{\Delta, \infty} \otimes \bigotimes_p' \pi_{\Delta, p}$ with every $\pi_{\Delta, p}$ unramified.

**Remark 1.3.7 (automorphic classes in the Lean project).** The Lean project has no cuspidal automorphic representations. It uses the classes `Langlands.NumberFieldGL.AutomorphicClass K n` of nonzero cuspidal Hecke eigenforms for the relation of equal eigenvalues at almost all places. By strong multiplicity one, Theorem 1.5.6, these classes are the cuspidal automorphic representations. The Lean code does not prove this, see Remark 1.5.11.
