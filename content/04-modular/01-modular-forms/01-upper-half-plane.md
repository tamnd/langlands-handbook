## The upper half plane and the modular group

A modular form is a function on the upper half plane $\mathbb{H}$ that is almost invariant under a subgroup of finite index of $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$. This section studies the action of $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ on $\mathbb{H}$. The main result is that the set $\mathcal{D}$ of points $\tau$ with $|\mathrm{Re}\, \tau| \le 1/2$ and $|\tau| \ge 1$ is a fundamental domain, and that the matrices $S$ and $T$ generate the group. Then we define the congruence subgroups $\Gamma_0(N)$, $\Gamma_1(N)$ and $\Gamma(N)$, compute their indices and define their cusps. The proofs of Theorem 1.1.4 are those of Serre, *A course in arithmetic*, Chapter VII, Section 1.

### The action of $GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$

Let $\mathbb{H} = \{\tau \in \mathbb{C} : \mathrm{Im}\, \tau > 0\}$. Let $GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ be the group of real $2 \times 2$ matrices with positive determinant.

**Definition 1.1.1 (the action on $\mathbb{H}$).** For $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ and $\tau \in \mathbb{H}$ let
$$\gamma \tau = \frac{a \tau + b}{c \tau + d}, \qquad j(\gamma, \tau) = c \tau + d.$$
The function $j$ is the *factor of automorphy*.

The number $c\tau + d$ is not 0, because $c$ and $d$ are real and not both 0, and $\tau$ is not real.

**Lemma 1.1.2 (the action).** Let $\gamma, \gamma' \in GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ and $\tau \in \mathbb{H}$.
1. $\mathrm{Im}(\gamma \tau) = \det(\gamma) \, \mathrm{Im}(\tau) / |j(\gamma, \tau)|^2$. In particular $\gamma \tau \in \mathbb{H}$.
2. $(\gamma \gamma') \tau = \gamma(\gamma' \tau)$ and $j(\gamma \gamma', \tau) = j(\gamma, \gamma' \tau) \, j(\gamma', \tau)$.
3. A scalar matrix $r I$ with $r > 0$ acts trivially, and $j(rI, \tau) = r$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* For part 1, multiply the numerator and the denominator of $\gamma \tau$ by $\overline{c \tau + d}$. The numerator becomes $ac |\tau|^2 + bd + ad \tau + bc \bar\tau$, and its imaginary part is $(ad - bc) \mathrm{Im}\, \tau$. For part 2, write $\gamma' \tau = u / v$ with $(u, v)^t = \gamma' (\tau, 1)^t$, so that $v = j(\gamma', \tau)$. Then $\gamma (u, v)^t = (\gamma \gamma')(\tau, 1)^t$. The second coordinate of the left side is $v \, j(\gamma, u/v)$, and that of the right side is $j(\gamma \gamma', \tau)$. The quotient of the two coordinates of the left side is $\gamma(\gamma' \tau)$, and that of the right side is $(\gamma \gamma') \tau$. Part 3 is clear. $\square$

So $GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ acts on $\mathbb{H}$, and the kernel of the action is the group of positive scalars. The group $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ acts through $PSL_2(\mathbb{R}) = SL_2(\mathbb{R}) / \{\pm I\}$. The relation in part 2 is the *cocycle relation*. It is the reason why the slash operator of Section 1.2 is an action.

### The modular group and its fundamental domain

**Definition 1.1.3 (the modular group).** The *modular group* is $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$. Let
$$S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
so that $S\tau = -1/\tau$ and $T\tau = \tau + 1$. Let $\rho = e^{2\pi i/3}$. The *standard fundamental domain* is
$$\mathcal{D} = \{\tau \in \mathbb{H} : |\mathrm{Re}\, \tau| \le 1/2, \ |\tau| \ge 1\}.$$

The set $\mathcal{D}$ is a closed hyperbolic triangle with the vertices $\rho$, $\rho + 1 = -\bar\rho$ and $\infty$. Its interior angles are $\pi/3$ at $\rho$ and at $-\bar\rho$, and 0 at $\infty$. The point $i$ is on its boundary, in the middle of the arc of the unit circle. One has $S^2 = -I$ and $(ST)^3 = -I$, so $S$ has order 2 and $ST$ has order 3 in $PSL_2(\mathbb{Z})$.

**Theorem 1.1.4 (the fundamental domain).** Let $\Gamma = SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$.
1. For each $\tau \in \mathbb{H}$ there is $\gamma \in \Gamma$ with $\gamma \tau \in \mathcal{D}$.
2. Let $\tau, \tau' \in \mathcal{D}$ be distinct with $\tau' = \gamma \tau$ for some $\gamma \in \Gamma$. Then $\tau$ and $\tau'$ are on the boundary of $\mathcal{D}$. Either $\mathrm{Re}\, \tau = \pm 1/2$ and $\tau' = \tau \mp 1$, or $|\tau| = 1$ and $\tau' = -1/\tau$.
3. Let $\tau \in \mathcal{D}$. The stabilizer of $\tau$ in $PSL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is trivial, except in three cases. It has order 2 and is generated by $S$ if $\tau = i$. It has order 3 and is generated by $ST$ if $\tau = \rho$. It has order 3 and is generated by $TS$ if $\tau = -\bar\rho$.
4. The group $\Gamma$ is generated by $S$ and $T$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Let $\Gamma'$ be the subgroup generated by $S$ and $T$. For part 1 we prove more: there is $\gamma \in \Gamma'$ with $\gamma \tau \in \mathcal{D}$. Let $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in \Gamma'$. By Lemma 1.1.2, $\mathrm{Im}(\gamma \tau) = \mathrm{Im}(\tau) / |c \tau + d|^2$. The numbers $c\tau + d$ with $c, d \in \mathbb{Z}$ form a lattice in $\mathbb{C}$, so only finitely many pairs $(c, d)$ give $|c\tau + d| \le 1$. So the set of the values $\mathrm{Im}(\gamma \tau)$ that are at least $\mathrm{Im}\, \tau$ is finite, and there is $\gamma \in \Gamma'$ with $\mathrm{Im}(\gamma \tau)$ maximal. Choose $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $|\mathrm{Re}(T^n \gamma \tau)| \le 1/2$, and let $\tau_1 = T^n \gamma \tau$. Then $\mathrm{Im}\, \tau_1 = \mathrm{Im}(\gamma \tau)$ is maximal. If $|\tau_1| < 1$, then $\mathrm{Im}(S \tau_1) = \mathrm{Im}(\tau_1) / |\tau_1|^2 > \mathrm{Im}\, \tau_1$, which contradicts the maximality. So $|\tau_1| \ge 1$ and $\tau_1 \in \mathcal{D}$.

For parts 2 and 3, let $\tau \in \mathcal{D}$ and $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in \Gamma$ with $\gamma \tau \in \mathcal{D}$ and $\gamma \ne \pm I$. Exchanging $\tau$ and $\gamma \tau$, and $\gamma$ and $\gamma^{-1}$, we may assume that $\mathrm{Im}(\gamma \tau) \ge \mathrm{Im}(\tau)$, so $|c\tau + d| \le 1$. Each point of $\mathcal{D}$ has imaginary part at least $\sqrt 3 / 2$. So $|c| \sqrt 3 / 2 \le |c| \, \mathrm{Im}\, \tau \le |c\tau + d| \le 1$, and $c \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$. Replacing $\gamma$ by $-\gamma$, we may assume $c \in \{0, 1\}$.

If $c = 0$, then $d = \pm 1$, and $\gamma \tau = \tau \pm b$ with $b \ne 0$. Both points have real part in $[-1/2, 1/2]$, so $b = \pm 1$ and $\mathrm{Re}\, \tau = \mp 1/2$. This is the first case of part 2, and $\gamma$ does not fix $\tau$.

If $c = 1$, then $|\tau + d| \le 1$. If $d = 0$, then $|\tau| \le 1$, so $|\tau| = 1$. Then $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} a & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\gamma \tau = a - 1/\tau = a - \bar\tau$. The point $-\bar\tau$ is in $\mathcal{D}$. So $a = 0$, unless $\mathrm{Re}\, \tau = \pm 1/2$, that is, $\tau \in \{\rho, -\bar\rho\}$. For $a = 0$ we get $\gamma = S$ and $\gamma \tau = -1/\tau$, the second case of part 2; it fixes $\tau$ only for $\tau = i$. For $\tau = \rho$ the other possible value is $a = -1$, and then $\gamma \tau = -1 - \bar\rho = \rho$, so $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = (ST)^2$ fixes $\rho$. For $\tau = -\bar\rho$ the other value is $a = 1$, and $\gamma = TS$ fixes $-\bar\rho$. If $d \ne 0$, then $|\tau + d| \le 1$ with $|\mathrm{Re}\, \tau| \le 1/2$ and $|\tau| \ge 1$ forces $d = \pm 1$. For $d = 1$ we get $|\tau|^2 + 2 \mathrm{Re}\, \tau + 1 \le 1$, so $1 \le |\tau|^2 \le -2 \mathrm{Re}\, \tau \le 1$, and $\tau = \rho$. Then $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} a & a - 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\gamma \rho = a - 1/(\rho + 1) = a + \rho$, because $\rho(\rho + 1) = -1$. So $a \in \{0, 1\}$. For $a = 0$ we get $\gamma = ST$, which fixes $\rho$. For $a = 1$ we get $\gamma \rho = \rho + 1 = -1/\rho$, which is both cases of part 2. The case $d = -1$ is symmetric, with $\tau = -\bar\rho$.

In all cases $\tau$ and $\gamma \tau$ are on the boundary of $\mathcal{D}$ when they are distinct, and they are related as part 2 says. The list of the elements that fix a point proves part 3, because $ST$ has order 3 in $PSL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ and $(ST)^2$ is its inverse up to sign, and similarly for $TS$.

For part 4, let $\tau_0 = 2i$. It is in the interior of $\mathcal{D}$, so by parts 2 and 3 it is equivalent to no other point of $\mathcal{D}$, and its stabilizer in $PSL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is trivial. Let $\gamma \in \Gamma$. By the proof of part 1 there is $\gamma' \in \Gamma'$ with $\gamma' \gamma \tau_0 \in \mathcal{D}$. Then $\gamma' \gamma \tau_0 = \tau_0$, so $\gamma' \gamma = \pm I$. Since $-I = S^2 \in \Gamma'$, we get $\gamma \in \Gamma'$. $\square$

**Remark 1.1.5 (the fundamental domain in Mathlib).** Mathlib defines $\mathcal{D}$ as `ModularGroup.fd` and its interior as `ModularGroup.fdo`, in the file `Mathlib/NumberTheory/Modular.lean`. Part 1 of Theorem 1.1.4 is `ModularGroup.exists_smul_mem_fd`, and the statement that two equivalent points of the interior are equal is `ModularGroup.eq_smul_self_of_mem_fdo_mem_fdo`. The proofs there follow the same argument as above. The Lean statements on Maass forms of Section 2.6 use `ModularGroup.fd` to define square integrable functions on $\Gamma \backslash \mathbb{H}$.

The proof shows more than the theorem. The points of $\mathcal{D}$ are inequivalent except for the identifications on the boundary given in part 2. So the quotient $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) \backslash \mathbb{H}$ is obtained from $\mathcal{D}$ by gluing the two vertical sides by $T$ and the two halves of the arc by $S$. With the point at infinity added, it is a sphere.

### Congruence subgroups

**Definition 1.1.6 (congruence subgroups).** Let $N \ge 1$. Let
$$\Gamma(N) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) : \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \bmod N \right\},$$
$$\Gamma_1(N) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) : \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 1 & * \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \bmod N \right\},$$
$$\Gamma_0(N) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) : c \equiv 0 \bmod N \right\}.$$
A *congruence subgroup* is a subgroup of $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ that contains $\Gamma(N)$ for some $N$. The least such $N$ is its *level*.

One has $\Gamma(N) \subset \Gamma_1(N) \subset \Gamma_0(N) \subset SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$, and $\Gamma_0(1) = \Gamma_1(1) = \Gamma(1) = SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$. The group $\Gamma(N)$ is the kernel of the reduction map $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) \to SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$, so it is normal. For $\gamma \in \Gamma_0(N)$ one has $ad \equiv 1 \bmod N$, so $d$ is a unit modulo $N$. The map $\Gamma_0(N) \to (\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$, $\gamma \mapsto d \bmod N$, is a homomorphism, because the lower right entry of a product of two upper triangular matrices modulo $N$ is the product of the lower right entries. Its kernel is $\Gamma_1(N)$, so $\Gamma_1(N)$ is normal in $\Gamma_0(N)$. The matrix $-I$ is in $\Gamma_0(N)$ for each $N$, and it is in $\Gamma_1(N)$ only for $N \le 2$.

**Lemma 1.1.7 (lifting).** Let $N \ge 1$.
1. Let $v, w \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ be primitive vectors, that is, vectors whose coordinates are coprime. If $v \equiv w \bmod N$, then there is $\gamma \in \Gamma(N)$ with $\gamma v = w$.
2. The reduction map $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) \to SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$ is surjective.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* For part 1, a primitive vector $(x, y)^t$ is the first column of a matrix in $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$, because $xu - yt = 1$ has a solution. So there are $g, h \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ with $g e_1 = v$ and $h e_1 = w$, where $e_1 = (1, 0)^t$. Then $h^{-1} g e_1 = h^{-1} v \equiv h^{-1} w = e_1 \bmod N$. So the first column of $h^{-1} g$ is $e_1$ modulo $N$, and since the determinant is 1, $h^{-1} g \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \bmod N$ for some $x \in \mathbb{Z}$. Let $u = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\gamma = h u g^{-1}$. Then $\gamma v = h u g^{-1} v = h u e_1 = h e_1 = w$. Also $h^{-1} g \equiv u \bmod N$, so $u g^{-1} \equiv h^{-1}$ and $\gamma = h u g^{-1} \equiv I \bmod N$.

For part 2, let $\bar g \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$, and lift it to an integer matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with $ad - bc \equiv 1 \bmod N$. We may take $c \ne 0$, adding $N$ to $c$ if needed. The numbers $c$, $d$ and $N$ have no common prime factor. We first change $d$ modulo $N$ so that $\gcd(c, d) = 1$. For each prime $q \mid c$ with $q \mid N$, the prime $q$ does not divide $d + tN$ for any $t$. For each prime $q \mid c$ with $q \nmid N$, choose $t$ modulo $q$ with $d + tN \not\equiv 0 \bmod q$. By the Chinese remainder theorem there is one $t$ for all these primes, and $d + tN$ is coprime to $c$. Now $ad - bc = 1 + mN$ for some $m \in \mathbb{Z}$. Choose $u, v \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $ud - vc = -m$. Then $(a + uN) d - (b + vN) c = 1$, and the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a + uN & b + vN \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ is in $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ and lifts $\bar g$. $\square$

**Proposition 1.1.8 (the indices).** Let $N \ge 1$. Then
$$[SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) : \Gamma(N)] = N^3 \prod_{p \mid N} \left(1 - \frac{1}{p^2}\right), \qquad [\Gamma_0(N) : \Gamma_1(N)] = \varphi(N), \qquad [\Gamma_1(N) : \Gamma(N)] = N,$$
$$[SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) : \Gamma_0(N)] = N \prod_{p \mid N} \left(1 + \frac{1}{p}\right).$$

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* By Lemma 1.1.7, $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) / \Gamma(N) \cong SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$. By the Chinese remainder theorem, $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$ is the product of the groups $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/p^e\mathbb{Z})$ for $p^e \parallel N$. The group $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})$ has $p(p^2 - 1)$ elements: there are $p^2 - 1$ choices for the nonzero first column, and then $p$ choices for the second column. The reduction map $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/p^e\mathbb{Z}) \to SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})$ is surjective by Lemma 1.1.7. Its kernel is the set of matrices $I + pX$ with $\det(I + pX) = 1$. Modulo $p^e$ the condition is $\mathrm{tr}(X) + p \det(X) \equiv 0 \bmod p^{e-1}$, and for each choice of three entries of $X$ modulo $p^{e-1}$ there is exactly one fourth entry. So the kernel has $p^{3(e-1)}$ elements, and $|SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/p^e\mathbb{Z})| = p^{3e}(1 - p^{-2})$. This gives the first formula. The homomorphism $\Gamma_0(N) \to (\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$, $\gamma \mapsto d$, is surjective, because by Lemma 1.1.7 each matrix $\mathrm{diag}(d^{-1}, d)$ of $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$ lifts. Its kernel is $\Gamma_1(N)$, which gives the second formula. The homomorphism $\Gamma_1(N) \to \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$, $\gamma \mapsto b \bmod N$, is surjective because $T^b \in \Gamma_1(N)$, and its kernel is $\Gamma(N)$. This gives the third formula. The last formula follows from the other three and $\varphi(N) = N \prod_{p \mid N} (1 - 1/p)$. $\square$

For a prime $p$ we get $[SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) : \Gamma_0(p)] = p + 1$. A system of representatives of $\Gamma_0(p) \backslash SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is $I$ and $S T^j$ for $0 \le j < p$. Indeed, the right cosets of $\Gamma_0(p)$ correspond to the points of $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)$ through $\gamma \mapsto$ the class of the bottom row $(c : d)$, and the bottom rows of $I$ and $ST^j$ are $(0 : 1)$ and $(1 : j)$.

### Cusps

The group $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ acts on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}) = \mathbb{Q} \cup \{\infty\}$ by the same formula, with $\gamma \infty = a/c$ and $\gamma(-d/c) = \infty$. The action is transitive, because $a/c$ in lowest terms is $\gamma \infty$ for a matrix $\gamma$ with first column $(a, c)^t$.

**Definition 1.1.9 (cusps and widths).** Let $\Gamma$ be a congruence subgroup. A *cusp* of $\Gamma$ is an orbit of $\Gamma$ on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$. Let $s = \alpha \infty$ with $\alpha \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$. The *width* of $s$ is the least $h \ge 1$ with $\alpha T^h \alpha^{-1} \in \pm \Gamma$.

The width does not depend on the choice of $\alpha$ or of $s$ in its orbit. Indeed, the stabilizer of $\infty$ in $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is $\pm \langle T \rangle$, so the stabilizer of $s$ in $\pm\Gamma$ is $\alpha (\pm \langle T^h \rangle) \alpha^{-1}$. Since $\Gamma \supset \Gamma(N)$ and $T^N \in \Gamma(N)$, the width divides $N$. The width of $\infty$ is 1 for $\Gamma_0(N)$ and $\Gamma_1(N)$, because $T$ is in these groups, and it is $N$ for $\Gamma(N)$. This is the convention of Volume 0, Section 3.2.

**Example 1.1.10 (the cusps of $\Gamma_0(p)$).** Let $p$ be a prime. Then $\Gamma_0(p)$ has two cusps, $\infty$ of width 1 and $0$ of width $p$. Let $s = a/c$ with $\gcd(a, c) = 1$, and with $s = \infty$ for $c = 0$. Each $\gamma \in \Gamma_0(p)$ sends $\infty$ to $a'/c'$ with $p \mid c'$, and sends $0$ to $b'/d'$ with $p \nmid d'$, where $(a', c')$ and $(b', d')$ are its columns. If $p \mid c$, a matrix of $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ with first column $(a, c)^t$ is in $\Gamma_0(p)$ and sends $\infty$ to $s$. If $p \nmid c$, the solutions of $xc - ya = 1$ are $(x_0 + ta, y_0 + tc)$ for $t \in \mathbb{Z}$, and since $p \nmid c$ we can choose $t$ with $p \mid y_0 + tc$. Then $\begin{pmatrix} x & a \\ y & c \end{pmatrix} \in \Gamma_0(p)$ sends $0$ to $s$. So the orbits of $\infty$ and $0$ are the sets $\{a/c : p \mid c\}$ and $\{a/c : p \nmid c\}$, and they are disjoint. The stabilizer of $0$ in $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is $\pm \{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ h & 1 \end{pmatrix} \} = \pm S \langle T^{-1} \rangle S^{-1}$, and such a matrix is in $\Gamma_0(p)$ exactly when $p \mid h$. So the width of $0$ is $p$. The sum of the widths is $p + 1 = [SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) : \Gamma_0(p)]$.

The last observation holds in general: the sum of the widths of the cusps of $\Gamma$ is the index of $\pm\Gamma$ in $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$. The number of cusps of $\Gamma_0(N)$ is $\sum_{d \mid N} \varphi(\gcd(d, N/d))$, and the cusps are represented by fractions $a/d$ with $d \mid N$. We do not need these facts. Their proofs are in Diamond and Shurman, Chapter 3. For $N \ge 5$ the group $\Gamma_1(N)$ has $\frac12 \sum_{d \mid N} \varphi(d) \varphi(N/d)$ cusps; see the same chapter.

**Remark 1.1.11 (the quotient as a curve).** For a congruence subgroup $\Gamma$, the quotient $Y(\Gamma) = \Gamma \backslash \mathbb{H}$ is a Riemann surface, and adding the finitely many cusps gives a compact Riemann surface $X(\Gamma)$. For $\Gamma_0(N)$ and $\Gamma_1(N)$ these are the modular curves $X_0(N)$ and $X_1(N)$. They are algebraic curves defined over $\mathbb{Q}$, and they are moduli spaces of elliptic curves with extra structure. This chapter uses only the fundamental domain of $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ and the finite index of $\Gamma$. The geometry of the modular curves is the subject of a later chapter, and Section 2.4 uses the Jacobian of $X_1(N)$.
