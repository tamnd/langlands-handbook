## Reciprocity for GL(1)

### The local theorem

Let $K$ be a nonarchimedean local field, that is, a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ or a field $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$. Let $\mathcal{O}_K$ be its ring of integers, $\varpi$ a uniformizer, and $k$ the residue field, with $q$ elements. Fix an algebraic closure $\bar K$ of $K$. Let $G_K = \mathrm{Gal}(\bar K / K)$, and let $W_K \subset G_K$ be the Weil group of Volume 3, Chapter 1. Its abelianization $W_K^{ab}$ is the quotient of $W_K$ by the closure of its commutator subgroup. An element of $W_K$ is a *geometric Frobenius* if it acts on the residue field of $\bar K$ as $x \mapsto x^{1/q}$.

**Theorem 2.1.1 (local reciprocity).** There is a unique isomorphism of topological groups $\mathrm{rec}_K : K^\times \to W_K^{ab}$ with the following two properties.
1. For each uniformizer $\varpi$ of $K$, the element $\mathrm{rec}_K(\varpi)$ is the image of a geometric Frobenius.
2. For each finite abelian extension $L$ of $K$ in $\bar K$, the composite map $K^\times \to W_K^{ab} \to \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$ is surjective, and its kernel is the norm group $N_{L/K}(L^\times)$.

The theorem is the main theorem of local class field theory. Volume 3 proves it. Most texts on class field theory, such as Serre's chapter [Ser67], state it with the *arithmetic* normalization, in which a uniformizer goes to an arithmetic Frobenius $x \mapsto x^q$. The map $x \mapsto \mathrm{rec}_K(x^{-1})$ turns one normalization into the other. The handbook uses the geometric normalization of Deligne everywhere, as in [Tat79].

The finite form of the theorem is a statement about one extension at a time. It is weaker, because it says nothing about the Frobenius and it does not say that the maps for different extensions agree.

**Theorem 2.1.2 (finite abelian local reciprocity).** Let $L$ be a finite abelian extension of $K$ in $\bar K$. There is a surjective homomorphism $K^\times \to \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$ whose kernel is $N_{L/K}(L^\times)$. In particular $K^\times / N_{L/K}(L^\times) \cong \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$.

**Theorem 2.1.3 (local existence).** Let $U$ be an open subgroup of finite index of $K^\times$. There is a finite abelian extension $L$ of $K$ in $\bar K$ with $N_{L/K}(L^\times) = U$.

The field $L$ is unique, because the map $L \mapsto N_{L/K}(L^\times)$ is injective on finite abelian extensions. If $K$ has characteristic 0, each subgroup of finite index of $K^\times$ is open. If $K$ has characteristic $p$, this fails, and the theorem needs the word open.

**Example 2.1.4 ($K = \mathbb{Q}_p$).** Each $x \in \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$ is $x = p^n u$ with $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $u \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$. The maximal abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is generated by the roots of unity. The element $\mathrm{rec}(x)$ acts on a root of unity $\zeta$ of order $p^m$ by $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^u$. It acts on a root of unity $\zeta$ of order $m$ prime to $p$ by $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^a$, where $a p^n \equiv 1 \pmod m$. Thus $\mathrm{rec}(p)$ fixes the roots of unity of $p$-power order and acts as a geometric Frobenius on the unramified extensions. The units act trivially on the unramified extensions. In the arithmetic normalization the unit $u$ acts on $\zeta$ of order $p^m$ by $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{u^{-1}}$, which is the formula of Lubin-Tate theory.

**Remark 2.1.5 (the correspondence for $GL_1$).** A continuous character of $W_K$ is trivial on the closure of the commutator subgroup. Thus $\chi \mapsto \chi \circ \mathrm{rec}_K$ is a bijection from the continuous characters $W_K \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ to the continuous characters $K^\times \to \mathbb{C}^\times$. A continuous character of $K^\times$ is trivial on an open subgroup, so the continuous characters of $K^\times = GL_1(K)$ are its irreducible smooth representations. The characters of $W_K$ are its representations of dimension 1. Thus the bijection is the local Langlands correspondence for $GL_1$. The third section states its generalization to $GL_n$.

### The global theorem

Let $F$ be a number field, $\mathbb{A}_F$ its ring of adeles, and $C_F = \mathbb{A}_F^\times / F^\times$ its idele class group. For a finite place $v$ of $F$, the completion $F_v$ is a local field, and $F_v^\times$ maps to $C_F$: an element $x$ goes to the class of the idele with component $x$ at $v$ and $1$ at the other places. Let $L$ be a finite abelian extension of $F$, and let $v$ be a finite place of $F$ that is unramified in $L$. All the places of $L$ over $v$ have the same arithmetic Frobenius in $\mathrm{Gal}(L/F)$, because the group is abelian. Call it $\mathrm{Frob}_v$.

**Theorem 2.1.6 (global reciprocity).** Let $L$ be a finite abelian extension of $F$ in $\bar F$. There is a continuous surjective homomorphism $\mathrm{art}_{L/F} : C_F \to \mathrm{Gal}(L/F)$ such that, for each finite place $v$ of $F$ unramified in $L$ and each uniformizer $\varpi_v$ of $F_v$, the image of $\varpi_v$ is $\mathrm{Frob}_v^{-1}$.

The kernel of $\mathrm{art}_{L/F}$ is the image of the norm map $C_L \to C_F$. This part of the classical theorem is not in the Lean statement. The minus sign in the exponent is the geometric normalization again.

**Theorem 2.1.7 (global existence).** Let $U$ be an open subgroup of finite index of $C_F$. There is a finite abelian extension $L$ of $F$ in $\bar F$ and a map $\mathrm{art}_{L/F}$ as in the last theorem whose kernel is $U$.

For $F = \mathbb{Q}$ the extensions are explicit.

**Theorem 2.1.8 (Kronecker-Weber).** Each finite abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ is contained in a cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ for some $n \ge 1$.

The global theorems identify the characters of finite order of $C_F$ with the continuous characters of $\mathrm{Gal}(\bar F / F)$. Let $\chi$ be such a Galois character and let $\chi_H = \chi \circ \mathrm{art}$ be the Hecke character. At a place $v$ where $\chi$ is unramified, $\chi_H(\varpi_v) = \chi(\mathrm{Frob}_v^{-1})$. Thus the Hecke L-function of $\chi_H$ is the Artin L-function of $\chi$ whose Euler factors are $(1 - \chi(\Phi_v) N(v)^{-s})^{-1}$, where $\Phi_v = \mathrm{Frob}_v^{-1}$ is a geometric Frobenius. With the arithmetic Frobenius in the Euler factors, it is the Artin L-function of $\chi^{-1}$. Hecke L-functions have an analytic continuation and a functional equation, by the work of Hecke and by Tate's thesis. Thus class field theory gives these properties to the Artin L-functions of characters. This transfer of analytic properties from the automorphic side to the Galois side is the pattern of the whole program.
