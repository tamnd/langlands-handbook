## Modularity and GL(2)

### Two sequences of numbers

Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$, given by a minimal Weierstrass equation, and let $N$ be its conductor. For a prime $p \nmid N$ the curve has good reduction at $p$, and
$$a_p(E) = p + 1 - \# E(\mathbb{F}_p).$$
For a prime $p \mid N$ the number $a_p(E)$ is $1$, $-1$ or $0$, as the reduction at $p$ is split multiplicative, nonsplit multiplicative or additive. Hasse proved that $|a_p(E)| \le 2 \sqrt p$ for $p \nmid N$. The L-function of $E$ is the Euler product
$$L(E, s) = \prod_{p \mid N} (1 - a_p(E) p^{-s})^{-1} \prod_{p \nmid N} (1 - a_p(E) p^{-s} + p^{1 - 2s})^{-1} = \sum_{n \ge 1} a_n(E) n^{-s}.$$
By the bound of Hasse, it converges absolutely for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 3/2$.

Let $f$ be a cusp form of weight 2 on $\Gamma_0(N)$. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is in $\Gamma_0(N)$, so $f(\tau + 1) = f(\tau)$. Thus the cusp $\infty$ has width 1, and $f$ has a $q$-expansion
$$f(\tau) = \sum_{n \ge 1} a_n(f) q^n, \qquad q = e^{2 \pi i \tau}.$$
If $f$ is a normalized eigenform of the Hecke operators, then $a_1(f) = 1$ and $a_p(f)$ is the eigenvalue of $T_p$ for $p \nmid N$. Thus each prime $p$ has two numbers attached: $a_p(E)$ counts points, and $a_p(f)$ is an eigenvalue. The modularity theorem says that for each $E$ the first sequence is the second one for some $f$.

### The modularity theorem

**Theorem 2.2.1 (modularity of elliptic curves over Q).** Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$. There are an integer $N \ge 1$ and a cusp form $f$ of weight 2 on $\Gamma_0(N)$ such that $a_n(f) = a_n(E)$ for all $n \ge 1$.

Wiles and Taylor-Wiles proved the semistable case [Wil95]. Breuil, Conrad, Diamond and Taylor completed the proof [BCDT01]. One can take $N$ to be the conductor of $E$ and $f$ a normalized newform. This refinement uses the theorem of Carayol on the conductor of the Galois representation of a newform. The Lean statement asks only for some $N$, which is the form that the proofs give first.

**Theorem 2.2.2 (modularity of semistable elliptic curves).** Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ with no prime of additive reduction. Then $E$ satisfies the conclusion of the last theorem.

With the theorem of Ribet on the level of modular Galois representations, the semistable case implies Fermat's last theorem. The curve of Frey of a counterexample to Fermat is semistable.

**Example 2.2.3 (the curve $X_0(11)$).** Let $E$ be the curve $y^2 + y = x^3 - x^2 - 10x - 20$. Its discriminant is $-11^5$, its conductor is $11$, and its reduction at 11 is split multiplicative. The point $(5, 5)$ is a rational point of order 5. The cusp form is
$$f = q \prod_{n \ge 1} (1 - q^n)^2 (1 - q^{11 n})^2 = q - 2q^2 - q^3 + 2q^4 + q^5 + 2q^6 - 2q^7 - 2q^9 - 2q^{10} + q^{11} - 2q^{12} + 4q^{13} + \cdots,$$
the unique normalized cusp form of weight 2 on $\Gamma_0(11)$. The table compares the two sides for the first primes.

| $p$ | 2 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 11 | 13 | 17 | 19 | 23 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\# E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ | 5 | 5 | 5 | 10 | | 10 | 20 | 20 | 25 |
| $a_p(E)$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $-2$ | $1$ | $4$ | $-2$ | $0$ | $-1$ |
| $a_p(f)$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $-2$ | $1$ | $4$ | $-2$ | $0$ | $-1$ |

Each number $\# E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ in the table is divisible by 5. For a prime $p \ne 5, 11$, the reduction modulo $p$ is injective on the rational points of finite order prime to $p$, so the point of order 5 gives a point of order 5 in $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$. This curve is the modular curve $X_0(11)$ itself. For it, the equality of the last two rows follows from the congruence relation of Eichler and Shimura, which is much older than the work of Wiles.

### The consequences

**Theorem 2.2.4 (the Hasse-Weil conjecture for elliptic curves over Q).** Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ of conductor $N$, and let $\Lambda(E, s) = N^{s/2} (2\pi)^{-s} \Gamma(s) L(E, s)$ for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 3/2$. Then $\Lambda(E, s)$ extends to an entire function on $\mathbb{C}$, and there is $\varepsilon \in \{1, -1\}$ with $\Lambda(E, 2 - s) = \varepsilon \Lambda(E, s)$.

The theorem follows from the modularity theorem and from the theory of Hecke for the newform of level $N$. This is the transfer of analytic properties that the first section describes for $GL_1$: the automorphic side has the functional equation, and modularity moves it to the arithmetic side.

**Theorem 2.2.5 (Ramanujan-Petersson for holomorphic forms).** Let $f$ be a normalized Hecke eigenform of weight $k \ge 1$ on $\Gamma_0(N)$, and let $p$ be a prime with $p \nmid N$. Then $|a_p(f)| \le 2 p^{(k-1)/2}$.

Node: LGV8B6TVWZ · Lean: `Langlands.RamanujanPeterssonHolomorphic`

Deligne proved the theorem for $k \ge 2$, as a consequence of his proof of the Weil conjectures [Del74]. For odd $k$ the space of cusp forms on $\Gamma_0(N)$ is zero, because $-I \in \Gamma_0(N)$. The bound for forms of weight 1 with a character is a theorem of Deligne and Serre. For $k = 2$ and rational coefficients it is the bound of Hasse for the curve of the form. The form $\Delta = q \prod_{n \ge 1} (1 - q^n)^{24} = \sum_{n \ge 1} \tau(n) q^n$ of weight 12 and level 1 gives the original conjecture of Ramanujan.

**Corollary 2.2.6 (the conjecture of Ramanujan for $\tau$).** The statement of the last theorem, with the fact that $\Delta$ is a normalized Hecke eigenform, implies that $|\tau(p)| \le 2 p^{11/2}$ for each prime $p$.

The conclusion is the statement of the bound in the formal-conjectures repository of Google DeepMind, so the corollary also shows that the statement of the project implies that one.

**Theorem 2.2.7 (Sato-Tate).** Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ of conductor $N$ without complex multiplication. For $p \nmid N$, let $\theta_p \in [0, \pi]$ be the angle with $a_p(E) = 2 \sqrt p \cos \theta_p$. Then for $0 \le \alpha \le \beta \le \pi$,
$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\# \{ p \le x : p \nmid N, \ \alpha \le \theta_p \le \beta \}}{\# \{ p \le x \}} = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_\alpha^\beta \sin^2 \theta \, d\theta.$$

Clozel, Harris, Shepherd-Barron and Taylor proved the theorem for curves with multiplicative reduction at some prime. Barnet-Lamb, Geraghty, Harris and Taylor proved it in general [BLGHT11]. The proof uses the automorphy of all the symmetric powers $\mathrm{Sym}^n$ of the Galois representation of $E$, up to a finite extension of the base field.

### The view from GL(2)

Let $\ell$ be a prime, let $T_\ell(E)$ be the Tate module of $E$, and let $V_\ell(E) = T_\ell(E) \otimes \mathbb{Q}_\ell$. The Galois group $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ acts on $V_\ell(E)$, which gives a representation $\rho_{E, \ell} : G_{\mathbb{Q}} \to GL_2(\mathbb{Q}_\ell)$. For $p \nmid N \ell$ the representation is unramified at $p$, and the characteristic polynomial of an arithmetic Frobenius at $p$ is $X^2 - a_p(E) X + p$. The geometric Frobenius on the dual space $H^1_{et}(E_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}, \mathbb{Q}_\ell)$ has the same characteristic polynomial.

A newform $f$ of weight 2 generates a cuspidal automorphic representation $\pi_f$ of $GL_2(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})$. Thus the modularity theorem is a case of the reciprocity of the program: the Galois representation $\rho_{E, \ell}$ of dimension 2 corresponds to the automorphic representation $\pi_f$ of $GL_2$, and the L-functions agree. With the unitary normalization of $\pi_f$, the agreement reads $L(E, s) = L(\pi_f, s - 1/2)$. Volume 5 explains $\pi_f$, and Volume 11 explains the proof of modularity.
