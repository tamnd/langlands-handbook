## Algebraic integers

In this chapter $F$ is a number field of degree $n = [F : \mathbb{Q}]$. This section defines the ring of integers of $F$ and proves that it is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $n$. It also defines the norm, the trace and the discriminant. We follow [Neu99], Chapter I, Section 2.

### Integral elements

Let $A \subset B$ be rings. An element $b \in B$ is *integral* over $A$ if it is a root of a monic polynomial with coefficients in $A$. The ring $B$ is *integral* over $A$ if each element of $B$ is integral over $A$.

**Proposition 1.1.1 (integral elements form a ring).** Let $A \subset B$ be rings. An element $b \in B$ is integral over $A$ if and only if the ring $A[b]$ is a finitely generated $A$-module. The elements of $B$ that are integral over $A$ form a subring of $B$, the *integral closure* of $A$ in $B$. If $C$ is integral over $B$ and $B$ is integral over $A$, then $C$ is integral over $A$.

*Proof.* If $b^m + a_{m-1} b^{m-1} + \dots + a_0 = 0$ with $a_i \in A$, then $A[b]$ is generated by $1, b, \dots, b^{m-1}$. Conversely, let $A[b]$ be generated by $w_1, \dots, w_r$ as an $A$-module. Write $b w_i = \sum_j a_{ij} w_j$ with $a_{ij} \in A$. Let $M$ be the matrix $b I - (a_{ij})$. Then $M$ kills the vector $(w_1, \dots, w_r)$, so $\det(M)$ kills each $w_i$, and in particular $\det(M) \cdot 1 = 0$. The expansion of $\det(M)$ is a monic polynomial in $b$ with coefficients in $A$. Thus $b$ is integral.

Let $b, c \in B$ be integral over $A$. Then $A[b, c]$ is generated by the products $b^i c^j$ with $i$ and $j$ bounded, so it is a finitely generated $A$-module. The argument of the first part, applied to the module $A[b, c]$ and the elements $b \pm c$ and $bc$, shows that these elements are integral. The proof of transitivity is similar: an element of $C$ is a root of a monic polynomial with finitely many coefficients $b_0, \dots, b_{m-1} \in B$, and the ring $A[b_0, \dots, b_{m-1}, c]$ is a finitely generated $A$-module. $\square$

A domain $A$ is *integrally closed* if it is equal to its integral closure in its field of fractions. A unique factorization domain is integrally closed. Indeed, let $x = a/b$ be a root of $X^m + c_{m-1} X^{m-1} + \dots + c_0$, with $a$ and $b$ coprime. Then $a^m$ is divisible by $b$, so $b$ is a unit. In particular $\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed.

**Definition 1.1.2 (ring of integers).** An *algebraic integer* is a complex number that is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$. The *ring of integers* $\mathcal{O}_F$ of a number field $F$ is the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $F$.

Lean: `NumberField.RingOfIntegers`, from Mathlib

By Proposition 1.1.1, $\mathcal{O}_F$ is a ring, and $\mathcal{O}_F \cap \mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Z}$ because $\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed. Each $x \in F$ is a root of a polynomial $a_m X^m + \dots + a_0$ with $a_i \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $a_m \ne 0$. Then $a_m x$ is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients, so $a_m x \in \mathcal{O}_F$. Thus $F$ is the field of fractions of $\mathcal{O}_F$. By the transitivity in Proposition 1.1.1, $\mathcal{O}_F$ is integrally closed.

### Embeddings, norm and trace

The extension $F / \mathbb{Q}$ is separable, so there are exactly $n$ field embeddings $\sigma : F \to \mathbb{C}$. An embedding is *real* if $\sigma(F) \subset \mathbb{R}$, and *complex* otherwise. The complex embeddings come in pairs $\sigma, \bar\sigma$, where $\bar\sigma$ is $\sigma$ followed by complex conjugation. Let $r_1$ be the number of real embeddings and $r_2$ the number of pairs of complex embeddings. Then $n = r_1 + 2 r_2$.

For $x \in F$ let $m_x : F \to F$ be the $\mathbb{Q}$-linear map $y \mapsto xy$. The *trace* and the *norm* of $x$ are
$$\mathrm{Tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(x) = \mathrm{tr}(m_x) = \sum_\sigma \sigma(x), \qquad N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(x) = \det(m_x) = \prod_\sigma \sigma(x).$$
The second equality in each formula holds because the characteristic polynomial of $m_x$ is $\prod_\sigma (X - \sigma(x))$. The trace is $\mathbb{Q}$-linear, and the norm is multiplicative. The same definitions give $\mathrm{Tr}_{L/F}$ and $N_{L/F}$ for a finite extension $L / F$, with the sums and products over the $F$-embeddings of $L$.

**Lemma 1.1.3 (norm and trace of integers).** If $x \in \mathcal{O}_F$, then $\mathrm{Tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(x)$ and $N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(x)$ are in $\mathbb{Z}$. An element $x \in \mathcal{O}_F$ is a unit of $\mathcal{O}_F$ if and only if $N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(x) = \pm 1$.

*Proof.* Each conjugate $\sigma(x)$ is a root of the same monic integer polynomial as $x$, so it is an algebraic integer. The coefficients of $\prod_\sigma (X - \sigma(x))$ are sums of products of the $\sigma(x)$. Thus they are algebraic integers, and they are rational, so they are in $\mathbb{Z}$. If $xy = 1$ with $y \in \mathcal{O}_F$, then $N(x) N(y) = 1$ with $N(x), N(y) \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $N(x) = \pm 1$. Conversely, if $N(x) = \pm 1$, then $x^{-1} = \pm \prod_{\sigma \ne \mathrm{id}} \sigma(x)$ is an algebraic integer that lies in $F$, so it is in $\mathcal{O}_F$. Here we view $F$ as a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$ by one of its embeddings. $\square$

### The discriminant

**Definition 1.1.4 (discriminant of a basis).** Let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ be a basis of $F$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and let $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_n$ be the embeddings of $F$. The *discriminant* of the basis is
$$d(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n) = \det\big(\mathrm{Tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_i \alpha_j)\big)_{i,j} = \det\big(\sigma_i(\alpha_j)\big)_{i,j}^2.$$

The two expressions are equal because $\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha_i \alpha_j) = \sum_k \sigma_k(\alpha_i) \sigma_k(\alpha_j)$, so the matrix of traces is $S^t S$ with $S = (\sigma_k(\alpha_j))_{k,j}$. The discriminant is not zero, because the trace form $(x, y) \mapsto \mathrm{Tr}(xy)$ of a separable extension is nondegenerate. For a basis $1, \theta, \dots, \theta^{n-1}$ with conjugates $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n$ of $\theta$, the matrix $S$ is a Vandermonde matrix, and
$$d(1, \theta, \dots, \theta^{n-1}) = \prod_{i < j} (\theta_i - \theta_j)^2 = (-1)^{n(n-1)/2} N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(f'(\theta)),$$
where $f$ is the minimal polynomial of $\theta$.

**Theorem 1.1.5 (integral bases).** The ring $\mathcal{O}_F$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $n$. A basis of $\mathcal{O}_F$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is called an *integral basis* of $F$.

Lean: `NumberField.RingOfIntegers.basis`, from Mathlib

*Proof.* Choose a basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ of $F$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. Multiply each $\alpha_i$ by a nonzero integer, so that $\alpha_i \in \mathcal{O}_F$. Let $\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*$ be the dual basis for the trace form, so that $\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha_i \alpha_j^*) = \delta_{ij}$. Let $x \in \mathcal{O}_F$ and write $x = \sum_j c_j \alpha_j^*$ with $c_j \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $c_i = \mathrm{Tr}(x \alpha_i)$, and this is an integer by Lemma 1.1.3. Thus
$$\mathbb{Z} \alpha_1 + \dots + \mathbb{Z} \alpha_n \subset \mathcal{O}_F \subset \mathbb{Z} \alpha_1^* + \dots + \mathbb{Z} \alpha_n^*.$$
A subgroup of a free abelian group of rank $n$ is free of rank at most $n$. Thus $\mathcal{O}_F$ is free, and its rank is $n$ because it contains a free group of rank $n$. $\square$

Let $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_n$ and $\omega'_1, \dots, \omega'_n$ be two integral bases. The matrix that expresses one in terms of the other is in $GL_n(\mathbb{Z})$, so its determinant is $\pm 1$. The discriminant changes by the square of this determinant. Thus the following definition does not depend on the choice.

**Definition 1.1.6 (discriminant of a number field).** The *discriminant* of $F$ is $d_F = d(\omega_1, \dots, \omega_n)$ for an integral basis $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_n$. It is a nonzero integer.

Lean: `NumberField.discr`, from Mathlib

The same argument gives a useful test. Let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n \in \mathcal{O}_F$ be a basis of $F$, and let $M = \sum \mathbb{Z} \alpha_i$. Then
$$d(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n) = [\mathcal{O}_F : M]^2 \, d_F.$$
Indeed the matrix of the $\alpha_i$ in an integral basis has determinant $\pm [\mathcal{O}_F : M]$, by the theory of elementary divisors. In particular, if $d(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$ is squarefree, then $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ is an integral basis.

**Remark 1.1.7 (the sign of the discriminant).** The sign of $d_F$ is $(-1)^{r_2}$. Let $D = \det(\sigma_i(\omega_j))$. Complex conjugation fixes the real embeddings and exchanges the two embeddings of each complex pair. Thus $\bar D$ is $D$ with $r_2$ pairs of rows exchanged, and $\bar D = (-1)^{r_2} D$. So $D$ is real if $r_2$ is even and purely imaginary if $r_2$ is odd, and $d_F = D^2$ has the sign $(-1)^{r_2}$.

### Quadratic fields

**Example 1.1.8 (the integers of a quadratic field).** Let $d \ne 0, 1$ be a squarefree integer and $F = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$. Then
$$\mathcal{O}_F = \mathbb{Z}[\omega], \qquad \omega = \begin{cases} \sqrt d & \text{if } d \equiv 2, 3 \pmod 4, \\ (1 + \sqrt d)/2 & \text{if } d \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \end{cases}$$
and $d_F = 4d$ in the first case and $d_F = d$ in the second case. To see this, let $x = a + b \sqrt d$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $b \ne 0$. Then $x$ is integral if and only if its trace $2a$ and its norm $a^2 - d b^2$ are integers. If $2a$ and $a^2 - d b^2$ are integers, then $4 d b^2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $2b \in \mathbb{Z}$ because $d$ is squarefree. Write $a = a'/2$ and $b = b'/2$ with $a', b' \in \mathbb{Z}$. The condition is $a'^2 \equiv d b'^2 \pmod 4$. If $d \equiv 2, 3 \pmod 4$, this forces $a'$ and $b'$ to be even. If $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, it forces $a' \equiv b' \pmod 2$. This gives $\mathcal{O}_F$. The discriminant is $d(1, \omega) = (\omega - \bar\omega)^2$, which is $4d$ or $d$.

The integers $d_F$ that occur here are the *fundamental discriminants*. Thus $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ has discriminant $-4$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$ has discriminant $-3$, and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 5)$ has discriminant $5$. The ring of integers of a cyclotomic field is computed in Section 1.5.
