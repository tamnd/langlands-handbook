## Dedekind domains and ideals

The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is the ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$, by Example 1.1.8. In this ring
$$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}),$$
and the four factors are irreducible and pairwise not associate. Thus the factorization of elements is not unique. This section proves that the factorization of ideals is unique in every ring of integers. We follow [Neu99], Chapter I, Section 3.

### Dedekind domains

**Definition 1.2.1 (Dedekind domain).** A *Dedekind domain* is a noetherian integral domain that is integrally closed and in which every nonzero prime ideal is maximal.

Lean: `IsDedekindDomain`, from Mathlib

A principal ideal domain is a Dedekind domain. The next theorem gives the main examples.

**Theorem 1.2.2 (rings of integers are Dedekind).** The ring $\mathcal{O}_F$ is a Dedekind domain. For each nonzero ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $\mathcal{O}_F$, the quotient $\mathcal{O}_F / \mathfrak{a}$ is finite.

Lean: Mathlib has the instance of `IsDedekindDomain` for `NumberField.RingOfIntegers`

*Proof.* By Theorem 1.1.5, $\mathcal{O}_F \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$ as a group. Each ideal is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, so it is finitely generated, and $\mathcal{O}_F$ is noetherian. The ring is integrally closed by Section 1.1. Let $\mathfrak{a}$ be a nonzero ideal and $\alpha \in \mathfrak{a}$ nonzero. Then $m = N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)$ is a nonzero integer, and $m = \alpha \cdot \prod_{\sigma \ne \mathrm{id}} \sigma(\alpha)$ lies in $\mathfrak{a}$, because the second factor is $m / \alpha \in F$ and it is integral. Thus $m \mathcal{O}_F \subset \mathfrak{a}$, and $\mathcal{O}_F / \mathfrak{a}$ is a quotient of $\mathcal{O}_F / m \mathcal{O}_F \cong (\mathbb{Z}/m)^n$, which is finite. If $\mathfrak{p}$ is a nonzero prime ideal, then $\mathcal{O}_F / \mathfrak{p}$ is a finite integral domain, so it is a field, and $\mathfrak{p}$ is maximal. $\square$

### Fractional ideals and unique factorization

Let $\mathcal{O}$ be a Dedekind domain with field of fractions $F$. A *fractional ideal* is a nonzero finitely generated $\mathcal{O}$-submodule $\mathfrak{a}$ of $F$. Equivalently, $\mathfrak{a}$ is a nonzero submodule with $c \mathfrak{a} \subset \mathcal{O}$ for some nonzero $c \in \mathcal{O}$. The product $\mathfrak{a} \mathfrak{b}$ is the submodule generated by the products $ab$. For a nonzero prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ let
$$\mathfrak{p}^{-1} = \{x \in F : x \mathfrak{p} \subset \mathcal{O}\}.$$
The word *prime* in this volume means a nonzero prime ideal, unless the text says otherwise.

**Lemma 1.2.3 (two lemmas of Dedekind).** Let $\mathcal{O}$ be a Dedekind domain that is not a field.
1. Each nonzero ideal of $\mathcal{O}$ contains a product $\mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r$ of primes.
2. Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be a prime. Then $\mathfrak{a} \mathfrak{p}^{-1} \ne \mathfrak{a}$ for each nonzero ideal $\mathfrak{a}$.

*Proof.* For part 1, assume that the set of nonzero ideals that do not contain such a product is not empty. As $\mathcal{O}$ is noetherian, the set has a maximal element $\mathfrak{a}$. Then $\mathfrak{a}$ is not prime, so there are $b, c \in \mathcal{O}$ with $bc \in \mathfrak{a}$ and $b, c \notin \mathfrak{a}$. The ideals $\mathfrak{a} + b\mathcal{O}$ and $\mathfrak{a} + c\mathcal{O}$ are larger than $\mathfrak{a}$, so each contains a product of primes. Their product is contained in $\mathfrak{a}$, which is a contradiction.

For part 2, first we show that $\mathfrak{p}^{-1} \ne \mathcal{O}$. Let $a \in \mathfrak{p}$ be nonzero, and choose primes with $\mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r \subset a \mathcal{O}$ and $r$ minimal. Then $\mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r \subset \mathfrak{p}$, so some $\mathfrak{p}_i$, say $\mathfrak{p}_1$, is contained in $\mathfrak{p}$. As $\mathfrak{p}_1$ is maximal, $\mathfrak{p}_1 = \mathfrak{p}$. By the minimality of $r$, there is $b \in \mathfrak{p}_2 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r$ with $b \notin a \mathcal{O}$. Then $b \mathfrak{p} \subset a \mathcal{O}$, so $b/a \in \mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ and $b/a \notin \mathcal{O}$.

Now let $\mathfrak{a}$ be a nonzero ideal with generators $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m$, and assume that $\mathfrak{a} \mathfrak{p}^{-1} = \mathfrak{a}$. Let $x \in \mathfrak{p}^{-1}$. Then $x \alpha_i = \sum_j a_{ij} \alpha_j$ with $a_{ij} \in \mathcal{O}$. As in the proof of Proposition 1.1.1, $\det(x I - (a_{ij})) = 0$, so $x$ is integral over $\mathcal{O}$. Thus $x \in \mathcal{O}$, and $\mathfrak{p}^{-1} \subset \mathcal{O}$, which contradicts the first step. $\square$

**Theorem 1.2.4 (unique factorization of ideals).** Let $\mathcal{O}$ be a Dedekind domain. Each fractional ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ has a unique expression
$$\mathfrak{a} = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}} \mathfrak{p}^{v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})},$$
with integers $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})$ that are zero for almost all $\mathfrak{p}$. The fractional ideals form an abelian group $J_\mathcal{O}$ under multiplication, and $J_\mathcal{O}$ is the free abelian group on the primes of $\mathcal{O}$.

Lean: `Ideal.uniqueFactorizationMonoid`, from Mathlib, for the integral ideals

*Proof.* First we show that $\mathfrak{p} \mathfrak{p}^{-1} = \mathcal{O}$ for each prime $\mathfrak{p}$. We have $\mathfrak{p} \subset \mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}^{-1} \subset \mathcal{O}$, and $\mathfrak{p} \mathfrak{p}^{-1} \ne \mathfrak{p}$ by Lemma 1.2.3. As $\mathfrak{p}$ is maximal, $\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}^{-1} = \mathcal{O}$.

Existence for integral ideals. Assume that some nonzero ideal is not a product of primes, and let $\mathfrak{a}$ be a maximal ideal with this property. Then $\mathfrak{a} \ne \mathcal{O}$, because $\mathcal{O}$ is the empty product. Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be a maximal ideal that contains $\mathfrak{a}$. Then $\mathfrak{a} \subset \mathfrak{a} \mathfrak{p}^{-1} \subset \mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}^{-1} = \mathcal{O}$, and $\mathfrak{a} \ne \mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ by Lemma 1.2.3. By maximality, $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{p}^{-1} = \mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r$, and then $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{p} \mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r$, which is a contradiction.

Uniqueness for integral ideals. Let $\mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r = \mathfrak{q}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{q}_s$. Then $\mathfrak{p}_1$ contains the product of the $\mathfrak{q}_j$. As $\mathfrak{p}_1$ is prime, it contains some $\mathfrak{q}_j$, and as $\mathfrak{q}_j$ is maximal, $\mathfrak{p}_1 = \mathfrak{q}_j$. Multiply both sides by $\mathfrak{p}_1^{-1}$ and use induction on $r$.

Fractional ideals. Let $\mathfrak{a}$ be a fractional ideal and $c \ne 0$ with $c \mathfrak{a} \subset \mathcal{O}$. Then $\mathfrak{a} = (c\mathfrak{a}) (c\mathcal{O})^{-1}$, where the inverse of a product of primes is the product of their inverses. This gives existence, and uniqueness follows from the integral case after we clear the denominators. Finally $\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = \prod \mathfrak{p}^{-v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})}$ is an inverse of $\mathfrak{a}$, so the fractional ideals form a group. $\square$

The theorem has some direct consequences. For integral ideals, $\mathfrak{a} \supset \mathfrak{b}$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}$ divides $\mathfrak{b}$, that is, $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}) \le v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{b})$ for all $\mathfrak{p}$: "to contain is to divide". The sum $\mathfrak{a} + \mathfrak{b}$ is the greatest common divisor, with exponents $\min(v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}), v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{b}))$. For $x \in F^\times$ we write $v_\mathfrak{p}(x) = v_\mathfrak{p}(x \mathcal{O})$. The map $v_\mathfrak{p} : F^\times \to \mathbb{Z}$ is a homomorphism with $v_\mathfrak{p}(x + y) \ge \min(v_\mathfrak{p}(x), v_\mathfrak{p}(y))$. It is the *$\mathfrak{p}$-adic valuation* of $F$, and Chapter 2 starts from it.

**Proposition 1.2.5 (Chinese remainder theorem).** Let $\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_r$ be distinct primes of a Dedekind domain $\mathcal{O}$ and $e_1, \dots, e_r \ge 1$. Then the natural map
$$\mathcal{O} / \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1} \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r^{e_r} \to \prod_{i=1}^r \mathcal{O} / \mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}$$
is an isomorphism.

Lean: `Ideal.quotientInfRingEquivPiQuotient`, from Mathlib, for pairwise coprime ideals

*Proof.* The ideals $\mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}$ are pairwise coprime, since a prime that contains $\mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i} + \mathfrak{p}_j^{e_j}$ contains $\mathfrak{p}_i$ and $\mathfrak{p}_j$ and is equal to both. Their intersection is their product, because both are the ideal with exponents $e_i$ at $\mathfrak{p}_i$. The Chinese remainder theorem for pairwise coprime ideals in any commutative ring gives the claim. $\square$

### The class group

The principal fractional ideals $x \mathcal{O}$, for $x \in F^\times$, form a subgroup $P_\mathcal{O}$ of $J_\mathcal{O}$. For $\mathcal{O} = \mathcal{O}_F$ we write $J_F$ and $P_F$.

**Definition 1.2.6 (class group).** The *class group* of $F$ is $\mathrm{Cl}_F = J_F / P_F$. Its order $h_F$ is the *class number* of $F$.

Lean: `ClassGroup`, from Mathlib

Two elements of $F^\times$ give the same principal ideal if and only if their quotient is a unit of $\mathcal{O}_F$. Thus there is an exact sequence
$$1 \to \mathcal{O}_F^\times \to F^\times \to J_F \to \mathrm{Cl}_F \to 1.$$
The class group measures the failure of unique factorization of elements. Indeed $\mathcal{O}_F$ is a principal ideal domain if and only if $h_F = 1$, and a Dedekind domain is a unique factorization domain if and only if it is a principal ideal domain. Section 1.3 proves that $h_F$ is finite, and that $\mathcal{O}_F^\times$ is finitely generated.

**Example 1.2.7 (a class of order 2).** Let $\mathcal{O} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ and $\mathfrak{p} = (2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$. Then $\mathfrak{p}^2 = (4, 2 + 2\sqrt{-5}, -4 + 2\sqrt{-5})$. This ideal contains $2 = (2 + 2\sqrt{-5}) - (-4 + 2\sqrt{-5}) - 4$, and all its generators are divisible by 2, so $\mathfrak{p}^2 = (2)$. The ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ is not principal. Indeed a generator $a + b \sqrt{-5}$ would have norm $a^2 + 5b^2$ equal to $N(\mathfrak{p}) = 2$ (see below), and this equation has no integer solution. Thus the class of $\mathfrak{p}$ has order 2. The factorization of $6$ in ideals is $(6) = \mathfrak{p}^2 \mathfrak{q} \bar{\mathfrak{q}}$, with $\mathfrak{q} = (3, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$ and $\bar{\mathfrak{q}} = (3, 1 - \sqrt{-5})$. The two factorizations of $6$ in elements group these four prime ideals in two different ways.

### The absolute norm

**Definition 1.2.8 (absolute norm).** The *absolute norm* of a nonzero ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $\mathcal{O}_F$ is $N(\mathfrak{a}) = \# (\mathcal{O}_F / \mathfrak{a})$. For a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ the residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = \mathcal{O}_F / \mathfrak{p}$ is finite with $N(\mathfrak{p})$ elements.

Lean: `Ideal.absNorm`, from Mathlib

**Proposition 1.2.9 (properties of the norm).** The absolute norm is multiplicative: $N(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}) = N(\mathfrak{a}) N(\mathfrak{b})$. Thus it extends to a homomorphism $N : J_F \to \mathbb{Q}_{>0}^\times$. For $\alpha \in F^\times$,
$$N(\alpha \mathcal{O}_F) = |N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)|.$$
If $\mathfrak{p}$ lies over the prime number $p$, that is, $\mathfrak{p} \cap \mathbb{Z} = p \mathbb{Z}$, then $N(\mathfrak{p}) = p^f$ for an integer $f \ge 1$.

Lean: `Ideal.absNorm_span_singleton`, from Mathlib, for the principal ideals

*Proof.* By Proposition 1.2.5 it suffices to show that $N(\mathfrak{p}^e) = N(\mathfrak{p})^e$. For each $i \ge 0$ the quotient $\mathfrak{p}^i / \mathfrak{p}^{i+1}$ is a vector space over $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$. Its dimension is 1. Indeed, there is $a \in \mathfrak{p}^i \setminus \mathfrak{p}^{i+1}$ by unique factorization. Then $\mathfrak{p}^{i+1} \subset a\mathcal{O} + \mathfrak{p}^{i+1} \subset \mathfrak{p}^i$, and the middle ideal is the greatest common divisor of $a \mathcal{O}$ and $\mathfrak{p}^{i+1}$, which is $\mathfrak{p}^i$. Thus the class of $a$ spans $\mathfrak{p}^i / \mathfrak{p}^{i+1}$. The chain $\mathcal{O} \supset \mathfrak{p} \supset \dots \supset \mathfrak{p}^e$ gives $N(\mathfrak{p}^e) = N(\mathfrak{p})^e$.

For $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_F$ nonzero, the index of $\alpha \mathcal{O}_F$ in $\mathcal{O}_F \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$ is the absolute value of the determinant of $m_\alpha$ in an integral basis, which is $|N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)|$. The case $\alpha \in F^\times$ follows by multiplicativity. Finally $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ is a finite field that contains $\mathbb{F}_p$, so its order is a power of $p$. $\square$

The integer $f$ is the *residue degree* of $\mathfrak{p}$ over $p$. Section 1.4 studies it.
