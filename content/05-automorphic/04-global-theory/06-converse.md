## The converse theorem

Section 4.5 attaches to a cuspidal representation an entire $L$-function with a functional equation. The converse theorem says that these analytic properties characterize the cuspidal representations. Hecke proved the first version in level one, Weil extended it to $\Gamma_0(N)$ with twists, and Jacquet and Langlands gave the adelic form.

### Hecke's theorem in level one

**Theorem 4.6.1 (Hecke's converse theorem).** Let $k$ be an even positive integer and let $(a_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be complex numbers with $a_n = O(n^\sigma)$ for some $\sigma$. Put $L(s) = \sum_{n \ge 1}a_nn^{-s}$ and $\Lambda(s) = (2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(s)$. Suppose that $\Lambda$ extends to an entire function, bounded in each vertical strip, with
$$\Lambda(k - s) = (-1)^{k/2}\Lambda(s).$$
Then $f(\tau) = \sum_{n \ge 1}a_ne^{2\pi in\tau}$ lies in $S_k(SL_2(\mathbb{Z}))$.

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* The series for $f$ converges absolutely and locally uniformly on the upper half plane, by the bound on $a_n$, so $f$ is holomorphic and $f(\tau + 1) = f(\tau)$. For $c > \sigma + 1$ and $y > 0$, the formula $e^{-x} = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)x^{-s}\,ds$ for $x > 0$, applied with $x = 2\pi ny$ and summed over $n$, gives
$$f(iy) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Lambda(s)y^{-s}\,ds.$$
We move the line of integration to $\mathrm{Re}(s) = k - c$. On $\mathrm{Re}(s) = c$, Stirling's formula and the bound $|L(s)| \le \sum|a_n|n^{-c}$ give $\Lambda(s) = O(e^{-|t|})$, where $t = \mathrm{Im}(s)$. On $\mathrm{Re}(s) = k - c$ the same holds by the functional equation. The function $F(s) = s^2\Lambda(s)$ is bounded on both lines and of finite order in the strip between them, since $\Lambda$ is bounded there. By the Phragmén-Lindelöf principle $F$ is bounded in the strip, so $\Lambda(s) = O(|t|^{-2})$ uniformly in the strip. So the integrals over the horizontal segments at height $\pm T$ tend to $0$, and since $\Lambda$ has no poles,
$$f(iy) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(k - c)}\Lambda(s)y^{-s}\,ds = \frac{(-1)^{k/2}}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Lambda(u)y^{u - k}\,du = (-1)^{k/2}y^{-k}f(i/y),$$
with the substitution $s = k - u$. At $\tau = iy$ we have $-1/\tau = i/y$ and $\tau^k = (-1)^{k/2}y^k$, so this says $f(-1/\tau) = \tau^kf(\tau)$ on the imaginary axis. Both sides are holomorphic on the upper half plane, so the identity holds everywhere. The matrices $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ generate $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$, and $f$ transforms correctly under both, so $f$ is modular of weight $k$. Its $q$-expansion has no constant term, so $f$ is a cusp form. $\square$

The converse of Theorem 4.6.1 is Hecke's Theorem 2.2.4 of Volume 4. If one allows $\Lambda$ to have simple poles at $s = 0$ and $s = k$, the same proof gives the modular forms that are not cusp forms. For example $L(s) = \zeta(s)\zeta(s - k + 1)$ gives the Eisenstein series of weight $k$ (Exercise 8). The proof uses that $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is generated by $S$ and $T$. For $\Gamma_0(N)$ with $N > 1$, the elements $T$ and $w_N$ do not generate the group, and the functional equation of $L(f, s)$ alone is not enough.

**Corollary 4.6.2 (the adelic converse theorem in level one).** Let $k \ge 2$ be even, and let $\pi = \bigotimes'_v\pi_v$ be an irreducible admissible representation with $\pi_\infty \cong D_k$ and with $\pi_p$ an unramified principal series with trivial central character for every prime $p$. Suppose that $L(s, \pi_f)$ converges absolutely in some right half plane, and that $\Lambda(s, \pi)$ extends to an entire function, bounded in vertical strips, with $\Lambda(s, \pi) = i^k\Lambda(1 - s, \pi)$. Then $\pi$ is a cuspidal automorphic representation, and $\pi = \pi_f$ for a newform $f$ of level one.

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* Write $L(s, \pi_f) = \sum_{n \ge 1}b_nn^{-s}$ and put $a_n = n^{(k-1)/2}b_n$. The coefficients $a_n$ grow at most polynomially, since the Dirichlet series converges absolutely in a half plane. With $s' = s + (k-1)/2$, we have $\Lambda(s, \pi) = 2(2\pi)^{-s'}\Gamma(s')\sum a_nn^{-s'}$, and the functional equation becomes $\Lambda(k - s') = (-1)^{k/2}\Lambda(s')$ for the function of Theorem 4.6.1. So $f = \sum a_nq^n$ lies in $S_k(SL_2(\mathbb{Z}))$. The Euler product shows that $a_1 = 1$, that $a_{mn} = a_ma_n$ for coprime $m, n$, and that $a_pa_{p^r} = a_{p^{r+1}} + p^{k-1}a_{p^{r-1}}$. By Volume 4, Theorem 2.1.3, $f$ is a normalized eigenform, and in level one it is a newform. Then $\pi_f$ has components $D_k$ at infinity and the unramified principal series with the same Satake parameters as $\pi_p$ at each $p$, by Theorem 4.3.3. So $\pi \cong \pi_f$, which is cuspidal automorphic. $\square$

### The theorems of Weil and of Jacquet and Langlands

For a Dirichlet character $\chi$ of conductor $r$ and $f = \sum a_nq^n$, the twisted series is $L(f \otimes \chi, s) = \sum\chi(n)a_nn^{-s}$, and $\Lambda_{Nr^2}(f \otimes \chi, s) = (r^2N)^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f \otimes \chi, s)$.

**Theorem 4.6.3 (Weil's converse theorem).** Let $k$ and $N$ be positive integers and let $(a_n)$ be of polynomial growth. Suppose that $\Lambda_N(s) = N^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\sum a_nn^{-s}$ is entire, bounded in vertical strips, and satisfies $\Lambda_N(k - s) = \varepsilon\,i^k\Lambda_N(s)$ for a sign $\varepsilon$. Let $P$ be a set of primes prime to $N$ that meets every arithmetic progression $a + N\mathbb{Z}$ with $\gcd(a, N) = 1$. Suppose that for every primitive Dirichlet character $\chi$ whose conductor $r$ lies in $P$, the twisted function $\Lambda_{Nr^2}(f \otimes \chi, s)$ is entire, bounded in vertical strips, and satisfies a functional equation that relates it to $\Lambda_{Nr^2}(f \otimes \bar\chi, k - s)$, with the root number that the theory of Volume 4 predicts for $f \otimes \chi$: the product of $\varepsilon i^k$, $\chi(N)$ and a factor built from the Gauss sum of $\chi$. Then $f = \sum a_nq^n$ lies in $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ and $w_Nf = \varepsilon f$.

Lean: not formalized

Weil proved this in Math. Ann. 168 (1967), where the precise constants are given. The twists give enough functional equations to prove invariance under generators of $\Gamma_0(N)$. The same paper stated the conjecture that each elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ of conductor $N$ comes from a form of level $N$.

**Theorem 4.6.4 (converse theorem of Jacquet and Langlands).** Let $\pi = \bigotimes'_v\pi_v$ be an irreducible admissible representation of $GL_2(\mathbb{A})$, with each $\pi_v$ infinite-dimensional, whose central character $\omega$ is trivial on $\mathbb{Q}^\times$. Suppose that for every character $\chi$ of $\mathbb{Q}^\times \backslash \mathbb{A}^\times$ the functions $\Lambda(s, \pi \otimes \chi)$ and $\Lambda(s, \tilde\pi \otimes \chi^{-1})$ converge absolutely in a right half plane, extend to entire functions bounded in vertical strips, and satisfy
$$\Lambda(s, \pi \otimes \chi) = \varepsilon(s, \pi \otimes \chi)\Lambda(1 - s, \tilde\pi \otimes \chi^{-1}).$$
Then $\pi$ is a cuspidal automorphic representation.

Lean: not formalized

*Outline of the proof.* For a pure tensor $W = \prod_vW_v$ in $\bigotimes'_v\mathcal{W}(\pi_v, \psi_v)$ put $\phi(g) = \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Q}^\times}W(a(\xi)g)$. The sum converges and $\phi$ is left invariant under $P(\mathbb{Q})$ by construction, and under $Z(\mathbb{Q})$ since $\omega$ is trivial on $\mathbb{Q}^\times$. So $\phi$ is left invariant under $B(\mathbb{Q})$. By the Bruhat decomposition $GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) = B(\mathbb{Q}) \cup B(\mathbb{Q})wN(\mathbb{Q})$, so it suffices to prove $\phi(wg) = \phi(g)$. Fix $g$. The twisted Hecke integrals of $\phi$ and of $\pi(w)\phi$ are products of local zeta integrals, as in Lemma 4.5.4, and the global functional equations for $\pi \otimes \chi$ with the local ones of Theorem 4.5.2 give
$$\int_{\mathbb{Q}^\times \backslash \mathbb{A}^\times}\left(\phi(a(y)g) - \phi(w\,a(y)g)\right)\chi(y)|y|^{s - 1/2}\,d^\times y = 0$$
for all $\chi$ and $s$. By Fourier analysis on the idele class group the function $y \mapsto \phi(a(y)g) - \phi(wa(y)g)$ vanishes, and $y = 1$ gives the claim. The functions $\phi$ are then automorphic forms, and they are cuspidal because the constant term of a sum of Whittaker functions over $\xi \ne 0$ vanishes. The details, including the growth estimates and the case of the twists that are ramified at a place, are in [JL70], Section 11, and in [Bum97], Chapter 3. $\square$

One can restrict $\chi$ to the characters unramified outside a fixed finite set of places. Cogdell and Piatetski-Shapiro proved converse theorems for $GL_n$ with twists by $GL_{n-1}$ and $GL_{n-2}$, Volume 7, Theorem 1.6.11. These theorems give known cases of functoriality: the symmetric square lift to $GL_3$ of Gelbart and Jacquet, and the symmetric fourth power lift to $GL_5$ of Kim [Kim03].
