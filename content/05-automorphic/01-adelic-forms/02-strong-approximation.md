## Strong approximation

The aim of this section is the decomposition $GL_2(\mathbb{A}) = GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) K_0(N)$ and the identification of the double coset space $GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \backslash GL_2(\mathbb{A}) / K_0(N)$ with $\Gamma_0(N) \backslash GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$. The proof has two parts. The determinant reduces the problem to $SL_2$, by the factorization of the ideles of Section 1.1. For $SL_2$ the key fact is strong approximation: $SL_2(\mathbb{Q})$ is dense in $SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$. For the additive group this is Volume 1, Theorem 3.5.5, with $v_0 = \infty$: the field $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{A}_f$. We deduce the statement for $SL_2$ from the additive one, because $SL_2$ is generated by two copies of the additive group, in a uniform way.

### Strong approximation for SL(2)

**Lemma 1.2.1 (four elementary matrices).** Let $R$ be either a field or the ring $\mathbb{Z}_p$. Each $g \in SL_2(R)$ can be written
$$g = n^-(t) \, n(x) \, n^-(c) \, n(y)$$
with $t \in \{0, -1\}$ and $x, c, y \in R$. If $g = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with $c$ a unit of $R$, one can take $t = 0$, $x = (a - 1)/c$ and $y = (d - 1)/c$.

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* Let $c$ be a unit and $x = (a-1)/c$, $y = (d-1)/c$. Then
$$n(x) n^-(c) n(y) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + xc & (1 + xc) y + x \\ c & cy + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b' \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$$
for some $b' \in R$. This matrix has determinant 1, as $g$ does, and they have the same entries $a$, $c$, $d$. Since $c$ is a unit, $b' = (ad - 1)/c = b$. So $g = n(x)n^-(c)n(y)$. Now let $c$ be a non-unit. If $R$ is a field, then $c = 0$ and $a \ne 0$. If $R = \mathbb{Z}_p$, then $c \in p\mathbb{Z}_p$, and $ad - bc = 1$ shows that $a$ is a unit. In both cases the lower left entry of $n^-(1) g = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ a + c & b + d \end{pmatrix}$ is a unit. By the first case, $n^-(1) g = n(x)n^-(c')n(y)$ for some $x, c', y \in R$, and $g = n^-(-1) n(x) n^-(c') n(y)$. $\square$

**Theorem 1.2.2 (strong approximation for $SL_2$).** The group $SL_2(\mathbb{Q})$ is dense in $SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$.

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* Let $H$ be the closure of $SL_2(\mathbb{Q})$ in $SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$. It is a subgroup, as the closure of a subgroup of a topological group. The map $x \mapsto n(x)$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb{A}_f$ onto the closed subgroup $N(\mathbb{A}_f)$ of $SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$, because $n(x) \in K_p$ if and only if $x \in \mathbb{Z}_p$. By Volume 1, Theorem 3.5.5, $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{A}_f$. So $n(\mathbb{Q})$ is dense in $N(\mathbb{A}_f)$, and $N(\mathbb{A}_f) \subset H$. In the same way $n^-(\mathbb{A}_f) \subset H$. Now let $g \in SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$, and let $S$ be the finite set of primes with $g_p \notin SL_2(\mathbb{Z}_p)$. Apply Lemma 1.2.1 at each prime $p$, with $R = \mathbb{Q}_p$ for $p \in S$ and $R = \mathbb{Z}_p$ for $p \notin S$. This gives $g_p = n^-(t_p) n(x_p) n^-(c_p) n(y_p)$ with $t_p, x_p, c_p, y_p \in \mathbb{Z}_p$ for $p \notin S$. So $t = (t_p)$, $x = (x_p)$, $c = (c_p)$ and $y = (y_p)$ are finite adeles, and $g = n^-(t)n(x)n^-(c)n(y)$. Each factor is in $H$, so $g \in H$. $\square$

The uniform bound of Lemma 1.2.1 is what makes the proof work. A product of elements of $N(\mathbb{A}_f)$ and $n^-(\mathbb{A}_f)$ has a fixed finite length, while each component $g_p$ could need its own word if the length were not bounded.

**Corollary 1.2.3 (the decomposition of $SL_2(\mathbb{A})$).** Let $U$ be an open subgroup of $SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$. Then $SL_2(\mathbb{A}) = SL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \, SL_2(\mathbb{R}) \, U$. In particular $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is dense in $SL_2(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})$.

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* Let $g = (g_\infty, g_f) \in SL_2(\mathbb{A})$. The set $g_f U^{-1} = g_f U$ is open in $SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$, so by Theorem 1.2.2 it contains an element $\gamma \in SL_2(\mathbb{Q})$. Write $\gamma = g_f u^{-1}$ with $u \in U$. In $SL_2(\mathbb{A})$ the rational element $\gamma$ has the components $(\gamma, \gamma)$, and
$$g = \gamma \cdot (\gamma^{-1} g_\infty, \gamma^{-1} g_f) = \gamma \cdot (\gamma^{-1} g_\infty, 1) \cdot (1, u).$$
For the last claim let $k \in SL_2(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})$ and let $U \subset SL_2(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})$ be an open subgroup. By Theorem 1.2.2 the open set $kU$ contains some $\gamma \in SL_2(\mathbb{Q})$. Then $\gamma \in SL_2(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})$, so $\gamma \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ by the argument of Lemma 1.1.2. $\square$

The last claim of the corollary, applied to $U = K(N) \cap SL_2(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})$, says that $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) \to SL_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$ is surjective. This is part 2 of Volume 4, Lemma 1.1.7. Exercise 2 asks for the details.

**Remark 1.2.4 (strong approximation in general).** Let $G$ be a simply connected semisimple group over a number field $F$, and let $S$ be a finite set of places such that $\prod_{v \in S} G(F_v)$ is not compact. The theorem of Kneser and Platonov says that $G(F)$ is dense in the restricted product of the $G(F_v)$ for $v \notin S$. See Platonov and Rapinchuk, *Algebraic groups and number theory*, Chapter 7. Theorem 1.2.2 is the case $G = SL_2$, $F = \mathbb{Q}$ and $S = \{\infty\}$. Volume 7, Theorem 1.1.4, proves the case $G = SL_n$ over a number field with the same method as here. The hypotheses are needed. For the multiplicative group $GL_1$, which is not semisimple, $\mathbb{Q}^\times$ is not dense in the finite ideles; this is Volume 1, Chapter 3, Exercise 12. For $GL_2$ the determinant gives the same obstruction, see Exercise 3. If $D$ is a definite quaternion algebra over $\mathbb{Q}$, then the group of the elements of reduced norm 1 is compact at $\infty$, and strong approximation with $S = \{\infty\}$ fails for it.

### The decomposition of GL(2, A)

**Theorem 1.2.5 (the decomposition of $GL_2(\mathbb{A})$).** Let $U$ be an open subgroup of $GL_2(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})$ with $\det U = \hat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$. Then
$$GL_2(\mathbb{A}) = GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \, GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) \, U.$$
This applies to $U = K_0(N)$ and to $U = K_1(N)$ for each $N \ge 1$.

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* Let $g \in GL_2(\mathbb{A})$. By the factorization of Section 1.1, $\det g = q r u$ with $q \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$, $r \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ and $u \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$. Choose $k \in U$ with $\det k = u$, and let $\delta = \mathrm{diag}(q, 1) \in GL_2(\mathbb{Q})$. The element $g_1 = \delta^{-1} g k^{-1}$ has determinant $r$, an idele with component $r > 0$ at $\infty$ and 1 at each prime. Let $h = \mathrm{diag}(r, 1) \in GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ at the infinite place. Then $g_2 = g_1 h^{-1}$ is in $SL_2(\mathbb{A})$. Since $U_1 = U \cap SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$ is an open subgroup of $SL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$, Corollary 1.2.3 gives $g_2 = \gamma s u_1$ with $\gamma \in SL_2(\mathbb{Q})$, $s \in SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ and $u_1 \in U_1$. The elements of $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ and of $GL_2(\mathbb{A}_f)$ commute in $GL_2(\mathbb{A})$. So
$$g = \delta g_1 k = \delta g_2 h k = (\delta\gamma) \, (s h) \, (u_1 k),$$
with $\delta\gamma \in GL_2(\mathbb{Q})$, $sh \in GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ and $u_1 k \in U$. The last claim holds by part 4 of Lemma 1.1.4. $\square$

**Lemma 1.2.6 (the rational points of $GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) K_0(N)$).** Inside $GL_2(\mathbb{A})$,
$$GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \cap GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) K_0(N) = \Gamma_0(N), \qquad GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \cap GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) K_1(N) = \Gamma_1(N), \qquad GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \cap GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) K(N) = \Gamma(N).$$

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* Let $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in GL_2(\mathbb{Q})$ with $\gamma \in GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ at $\infty$ and $\gamma \in K_0(N)_p$ for each $p$. By Lemma 1.1.2, $\gamma \in GL_2(\mathbb{Z})$. Its determinant is $\pm 1$ and positive, so it is 1. Its entry $c$ is in $N\mathbb{Z}_p$ for each $p$, so $N \mid c$. Hence $\gamma \in \Gamma_0(N)$. If also $\gamma \in K_1(N)_p$ for all $p$, then $d - 1 \in N\mathbb{Z}_p$ for each $p$, so $d \equiv 1 \bmod N$ and $\gamma \in \Gamma_1(N)$. The case of $K(N)$ is the same. The reverse inclusions are clear. $\square$

**Corollary 1.2.7 (the double coset spaces).** The map $g_\infty \mapsto \iota_\infty(g_\infty)$ induces bijections
$$\Gamma_0(N) \backslash GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) \to GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \backslash GL_2(\mathbb{A}) / K_0(N), \qquad \Gamma_0(N) \backslash \mathbb{H} \to GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \backslash GL_2(\mathbb{A}) / \mathbb{R}_{>0} SO(2) K_0(N),$$
where the second map sends $g_\infty i$ to the class of $\iota_\infty(g_\infty)$, and $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is the group of the positive scalar matrices at $\infty$. The same holds with $K_1(N)$ and $\Gamma_1(N)$ in place of $K_0(N)$ and $\Gamma_0(N)$.

Lean: not formalized

*Proof.* The first map is well defined because $\Gamma_0(N) \subset GL_2(\mathbb{Q})$ and $\gamma \cdot \iota_\infty(g_\infty) = \iota_\infty(\gamma g_\infty) \cdot \gamma_f$ with $\gamma_f \in K_0(N)$ for $\gamma \in \Gamma_0(N)$, where $\gamma_f$ is the finite part of $\gamma$. It is surjective by Theorem 1.2.5. Let $g_\infty, g'_\infty \in GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ with $\iota_\infty(g'_\infty) = \gamma \, \iota_\infty(g_\infty) \, k$ for some $\gamma \in GL_2(\mathbb{Q})$ and $k \in K_0(N)$. At the infinite place, $g'_\infty = \gamma g_\infty$, so $\gamma \in GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$. At the finite places, $1 = \gamma k$, so $\gamma_f = k^{-1} \in K_0(N)$. By Lemma 1.2.6, $\gamma \in \Gamma_0(N)$. This is injectivity. The group $\mathbb{R}_{>0}SO(2)$ at $\infty$ commutes with $K_0(N)$, so the second bijection follows from the first and from $\mathbb{H} = GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) / \mathbb{R}_{>0} SO(2)$, see Lemma 1.1.7. The proof for $K_1(N)$ is the same. $\square$

So the modular curve $Y_0(N) = \Gamma_0(N) \backslash \mathbb{H}$ is the double coset space $GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \backslash GL_2(\mathbb{A}) / \mathbb{R}_{>0} SO(2) K_0(N)$. The adelic description has one advantage: the finite part of the level is a product of local groups, and a change of level at one prime $p$ changes only the factor $K_0(N)_p$.

**Example 1.2.8 (decomposing an adele).** Let $p$ be a prime and let $g = \iota_p(\mathrm{diag}(p, 1))$, the element with component $\mathrm{diag}(p, 1)$ at $p$ and 1 at the other places. We find $\tau \in \mathbb{H}$ that corresponds to $g$ under Corollary 1.2.7, for any $N$. Let $\delta = \mathrm{diag}(p, 1) \in GL_2(\mathbb{Q})$. Then $\delta^{-1} g$ has the components
$$(\delta^{-1}g)_\infty = \mathrm{diag}(p^{-1}, 1), \qquad (\delta^{-1}g)_p = 1, \qquad (\delta^{-1} g)_q = \mathrm{diag}(p^{-1}, 1) \ (q \ne p).$$
For $q \ne p$ the number $p^{-1}$ is a unit of $\mathbb{Z}_q$, so $\mathrm{diag}(p^{-1}, 1) \in K_0(N)_q$. Hence $g = \delta \cdot \iota_\infty(\mathrm{diag}(p^{-1}, 1)) \cdot k$ with $k \in K_0(N)$, and the class of $g$ corresponds to $\tau = \mathrm{diag}(p^{-1}, 1) i = i/p$. In the same way $\iota_p(\mathrm{diag}(1, p))$ corresponds to $\tau = pi$, and $\iota_p(n(1/p))$ corresponds to $\tau = i - 1/p$, with $\delta = n(1/p)$ in the last case. The points $i/p$ and $pi$ are the images of $i$ under the maps $\tau \mapsto \tau/p$ and $\tau \mapsto p\tau$ of the classical formula for $T_p$. Section 1.6 explains this.

**Remark 1.2.9 (why $GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$).** Theorem 1.2.5 uses $GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$ and not $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$. With $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ the decomposition still holds, but the analogue of Lemma 1.2.6 is the group of the matrices of $GL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ with $N \mid c$, which contains $\mathrm{diag}(1, -1)$. The quotient $GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \backslash GL_2(\mathbb{A}) / \mathbb{R}^\times O(2) K_0(N)$ is then $\Gamma_0(N) \backslash \mathbb{H}$ again, because $\mathrm{diag}(1, -1)$ exchanges $\mathbb{H}$ and the lower half plane. The condition $\det U = \hat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$ of Theorem 1.2.5 is needed. For $U = K(N)$ the image of $\det$ is the subgroup of $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$ of the units congruent to 1 modulo $N$. Exercise 3 shows that $GL_2(\mathbb{Q}) \backslash GL_2(\mathbb{A}) / GL_2^+(\mathbb{R}) K(N)$ then has $\varphi(N)$ elements, so the adelic quotient of level $K(N)$ is a disjoint union of $\varphi(N)$ copies of $\Gamma(N) \backslash GL_2^+(\mathbb{R})$. This is the reason the Lean predicate `Langlands.IsInLevel` of Remark 1.1.5 gives forms on several copies of $\Gamma(N) \backslash \mathbb{H}$.
