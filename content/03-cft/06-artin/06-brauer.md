## Brauer's theorem and the continuation

Theorem 6.3.3 shows that $L(s, \mathrm{Ind}_H^G \psi)$ is an abelian $L$-function when $\psi$ has dimension one, and Section 6.5 shows that abelian $L$-functions are Hecke $L$-functions. To treat an arbitrary $\rho$ it is enough to write its character as an integral combination of such induced characters. Artin proved that a rational combination exists. Brauer proved in 1947 that an integral combination exists, and this gives the meromorphic continuation of every Artin $L$-function.

### The induction theorems

For a cyclic group $C$ of order $c$, let $\theta_C$ be the function on $C$ with $\theta_C(x) = c$ if $x$ generates $C$ and $\theta_C(x) = 0$ otherwise.

**Theorem 6.6.1 (Artin's induction theorem).** Let $G$ be a finite group.
1. For each cyclic group $C$, $\theta_C \in R(C)$.
2. $\sum_C \mathrm{Ind}_C^G \theta_C = |G| \cdot \mathbf{1}_G$, the sum over the cyclic subgroups $C$ of $G$.
3. For each $\chi \in R(G)$, $|G| \chi$ is an integral combination of characters $\mathrm{Ind}_C^G \psi$, with $C$ cyclic and $\psi$ a character of dimension one of $C$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* For part 2, let $g \in G$. By Proposition 6.1.1, part 1,
$$\mathrm{Ind}_C^G \theta_C(g) = \frac{1}{|C|} \sum_{x \in G,\ x^{-1} g x \in C} \theta_C(x^{-1} g x) = \#\{x \in G : x^{-1} g x \text{ generates } C\}.$$
Each $x \in G$ is counted for exactly one $C$, namely $C = \langle x^{-1} g x \rangle$. So the sum over $C$ is $|G|$. For part 1, apply part 2 to the cyclic group $C$ itself: $|C| \cdot \mathbf{1}_C = \sum_{B \subset C} \mathrm{Ind}_B^C \theta_B$. So $\theta_C = |C| \cdot \mathbf{1}_C - \sum_{B \subsetneq C} \mathrm{Ind}_B^C \theta_B$, and induction on $|C|$ gives $\theta_C \in R(C)$, starting with $\theta_{\{1\}} = \mathbf{1}$. For part 3, multiply part 2 by $\chi$ and use Proposition 6.1.1, part 3: $|G|\chi = \sum_C \mathrm{Ind}_C^G(\theta_C \cdot \mathrm{Res}_C \chi)$. Each $\theta_C \cdot \mathrm{Res}_C \chi$ lies in $R(C)$, and $R(C)$ is spanned by the characters of dimension one, since $C$ is abelian. $\square$

**Remark 6.6.2 (what Artin's theorem gives).** By Theorem 6.6.1, part 3, Theorem 6.3.3 and Corollary 6.5.3, $L(s, \chi)^{|G|}$ is a product of integral powers of Hecke $L$-functions. So $L(s, \chi)^{|G|}$ is meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$. This does not show that $L(s, \chi)$ is meromorphic, because a meromorphic function with a zero or pole of order prime to $|G|$ has no meromorphic $|G|$-th root. Artin had this partial result. The integrality in Brauer's theorem removes the difficulty.

A group $E$ is *elementary* if $E = C \times P$ with $C$ cyclic and $P$ a $p$-group for some prime $p$. Elementary groups are nilpotent, and every irreducible representation of a nilpotent group is induced from a character of dimension one of a subgroup. See Serre's *Linear representations of finite groups*, Chapter 8.

**Theorem 6.6.3 (Brauer's induction theorem).** Let $G$ be a finite group.
1. Every $\chi \in R(G)$ is an integral combination of characters $\mathrm{Ind}_E^G \psi$, with $E$ elementary and $\psi$ a character of dimension one of $E$.
2. Every $\chi \in R(G)$ is an integral combination $\chi = \sum_i n_i \mathrm{Ind}_{H_i}^G \psi_i$, with $H_i \subset G$, $\psi_i$ a character of dimension one of $H_i$ and $n_i \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Lean: not stated yet

We sketch the proof of part 1. A complete proof is in Serre's *Linear representations of finite groups*, Chapter 10. Let $V \subset R(G)$ be the subgroup generated by the characters in part 1. By Proposition 6.1.1, part 3, $\chi \cdot \mathrm{Ind}_E^G \psi = \mathrm{Ind}_E^G(\psi \cdot \mathrm{Res}_E \chi)$. The restriction $\mathrm{Res}_E \chi$ is an integral combination of characters induced from characters of dimension one of subgroups of $E$, and these subgroups are elementary. So $V$ is an ideal of $R(G)$, and it suffices to show $\mathbf{1}_G \in V$. Fix a prime $\ell$. For an element $x$ of order prime to $\ell$, let $C = \langle x \rangle$ and let $P$ be a Sylow $\ell$-subgroup of the centralizer of $x$. Then $C \times P$ is elementary. Serre uses the functions $\theta_C$ of Theorem 6.6.1 on these groups to find an element of $V$ with values in $\mathbb{Z}$ that are prime to $\ell$ at every element of $G$. From this he deduces that $V$ contains a constant function with value prime to $\ell$. As this holds for every $\ell$, the constants in $V$ form a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ that contains $1$. Part 2 follows from part 1, because each $\psi$ of dimension one of $E$ is already of the required form, and the subgroups $E$ are among the $H_i$.

The coefficients $n_i$ in Theorem 6.6.3 can be negative, and in general they must be. This is the reason why Brauer's theorem gives meromorphic and not holomorphic continuation.

### The continuation and the functional equation

**Theorem 6.6.4 (Brauer).** Theorem 6.4.6 holds. That is, for every $\chi \in R(G)$, the function $L(s, \chi)$ extends to a meromorphic function on $\mathbb{C}$, and $\Lambda(s, \chi) = W(\chi) A(\chi)^{1/2 - s} \Lambda(1 - s, \bar\chi)$, with $|W(\chi)| = 1$, $W(\bar\chi) = \overline{W(\chi)}$, and $W(\chi) = \pm 1$ if $\chi$ is real.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Write $\chi = \sum_i n_i \mathrm{Ind}_{H_i}^G \psi_i$ as in Theorem 6.6.3, part 2, and let $M_i = L^{H_i}$. The character $\psi_i$ is trivial on the commutator subgroup $H_i'$, so it is inflated from a character $\psi_i'$ of the abelian group $H_i / H_i' = \mathrm{Gal}(L_i / M_i)$, where $L_i = L^{H_i'}$. By Theorem 6.3.3, parts 2 and 3,
$$L(s, \chi) = \prod_i L(s, \psi_i, L/M_i)^{n_i} = \prod_i L(s, \psi_i', L_i/M_i)^{n_i}.$$
By Theorem 6.5.2 each factor is a Hecke $L$-function of $M_i$, which is meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$ by Theorem 5.5.3 and not identically zero. So $L(s, \chi)$ is meromorphic.

By Propositions 6.4.2 and 6.4.5, the functions $\Gamma_\infty$ and $A$ are compatible with inflation and induction, and they are multiplicative. Hence $\Lambda(s, \chi) = \prod_i \Lambda(s, \psi_i')^{n_i}$ and $A(\chi) = \prod_i A(\psi_i')^{n_i}$, where $\psi_i'$ is viewed as a character for $L_i/M_i$. The dual of an induced representation is induced from the dual (Proposition 6.1.1, part 4). So $\bar\chi = \sum_i n_i \mathrm{Ind}_{H_i}^G \bar\psi_i$, and in the same way $\Lambda(1 - s, \bar\chi) = \prod_i \Lambda(1 - s, \bar\psi_i')^{n_i}$. By Theorem 6.5.2, part 3, $\Lambda(s, \psi_i') = W(\psi_i') A(\psi_i')^{1/2 - s} \Lambda(1 - s, \bar\psi_i')$. Taking the product gives the functional equation with $W(\chi) = \prod_i W(\psi_i')^{n_i}$, and $|W(\chi)| = 1$. The constant is determined by $\chi$, since $\Lambda(1 - s, \bar\chi)$ is not identically zero. So it does not depend on the choice of the combination.

For the last claims, note that $\overline{L(\bar s, \chi)} = L(s, \bar\chi)$ for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$, because the eigenvalues of $\rho(\mathrm{Frob}_\mathfrak{P})$ on $V^{I_\mathfrak{P}}$ are roots of unity and those for $\rho^\vee$ are their inverses. The gamma factors of $\chi$ and $\bar\chi$ are equal, since $\chi(c_w)$ is real, and they are real on the real axis. Also $A(\bar\chi) = A(\chi)$. So $\overline{\Lambda(\bar s, \chi)} = \Lambda(s, \bar\chi)$ on $\mathbb{C}$. Apply complex conjugation to the functional equation at $\bar s$. This gives $\Lambda(s, \bar\chi) = \overline{W(\chi)} A(\chi)^{1/2 - s} \Lambda(1 - s, \chi)$. Comparison with the functional equation for $\bar\chi$ gives $W(\bar\chi) = \overline{W(\chi)}$. If $\chi$ is real, then $W(\chi)$ is real of absolute value 1. $\square$

**Corollary 6.6.5 (the order at $s = 1$).** For $\chi \in R(G)$, the order of $L(s, \chi)$ at $s = 1$ is $-\langle \chi, \mathbf{1}_G \rangle$. In particular, if $\rho$ does not contain the trivial representation, then $L(s, \rho)$ is holomorphic at $s = 1$ and $L(1, \rho) \ne 0$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* With the notation of the proof of Theorem 6.6.4, $\mathrm{ord}_{s=1} L(s, \chi) = \sum_i n_i \, \mathrm{ord}_{s=1} L(s, \psi_i')$. If $\psi_i'$ is trivial, $L(s, \psi_i')$ is the Dedekind zeta function of $M_i$, with a simple pole at $s = 1$. If not, $L(s, \psi_i')$ is holomorphic and nonzero at $s = 1$, by Corollary 6.5.3. So the order is $-\sum_i n_i \langle \psi_i, \mathbf{1}_{H_i} \rangle$. By Frobenius reciprocity this is $-\sum_i n_i \langle \mathrm{Ind}_{H_i}^G \psi_i, \mathbf{1}_G \rangle = -\langle \chi, \mathbf{1}_G \rangle$. $\square$

The nonvanishing of $L(s, \chi)$ on the whole line $\mathrm{Re}(s) = 1$ follows in the same way from the corresponding property of Hecke $L$-functions. This is the input of the density theorem of Chebotarev, see [Neu99], Chapter VII, Section 13.

### The theorem of Aramata and Brauer

The quotient $\zeta_M / \zeta_F$ of Corollary 6.3.4 is the $L$-function of a true character. Artin's conjecture of Section 6.7 would imply that it is entire. For Galois extensions this is a theorem.

**Theorem 6.6.6 (Aramata, Brauer).** Let $L/F$ be a finite Galois extension. Then $\zeta_L(s) / \zeta_F(s)$ is entire.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* For a cyclic subgroup $C$ of order $c$, let $\lambda_C = \varphi(c) r_C - \theta_C \in R(C)$, where $\varphi$ is the Euler function and $r_C$ is the regular character. By Proposition 6.1.1, part 4, $\mathrm{Ind}_C^G r_C = r_G$. Each element of $G$ generates exactly one cyclic subgroup, so $\sum_C \varphi(|C|) = |G|$. Theorem 6.6.1, part 2, then gives
$$\sum_C \mathrm{Ind}_C^G \lambda_C = |G| \, r_G - |G| \, \mathbf{1}_G = |G| (r_G - \mathbf{1}_G).$$
We claim that $\lambda_C$ is a combination of nontrivial characters of $C$ with nonnegative integer coefficients. For a character $\psi$ of $C$ of dimension one, $\langle r_C, \psi \rangle = 1$ and $\langle \theta_C, \psi \rangle = \sum_x \overline{\psi(x)}$, the sum over the $\varphi(c)$ generators $x$ of $C$. So $\langle \lambda_C, \psi \rangle = \varphi(c) - \sum_x \overline{\psi(x)}$. For $\psi = \mathbf{1}$ this is $0$. For every $\psi$ it is an integer, since $\lambda_C \in R(C)$. Its real part is at least $\varphi(c) - \varphi(c) = 0$. This proves the claim. By Corollary 6.3.4 and Theorem 6.3.3,
$$\Big(\frac{\zeta_L(s)}{\zeta_F(s)}\Big)^{|G|} = L(s, |G|(r_G - \mathbf{1}_G)) = \prod_C \prod_{\psi \ne \mathbf{1}} L(s, \psi, L/L^C)^{\langle \lambda_C, \psi \rangle}.$$
Each factor on the right is entire by Corollary 6.5.3, part 1, and all the exponents are nonnegative. So the left side is entire. A meromorphic function whose $|G|$-th power is entire is itself entire, because a pole of the function would be a pole of its power. $\square$

For a field $M$ between $F$ and $L$ that is not Galois over $F$, the statement that $\zeta_M / \zeta_F$ is entire is the conjecture of Dedekind. It follows from Artin's conjecture, by Corollary 6.3.4, part 3. Uchida and van der Waall proved it in 1975 when the Galois closure of $M$ over $F$ is solvable.

**Example 6.6.7 (the pure cubic field, continued).** Let $K$, $L$ and $\rho$ be as in Example 6.3.5. The real place of $\mathbb{Q}$ has complex conjugation $c$ equal to a transposition in $S_3$, and $\rho(c)$ has the eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$. So $\Gamma_\infty(s, \rho) = \Gamma_\mathbb{R}(s) \Gamma_\mathbb{R}(s + 1) = \Gamma_\mathbb{C}(s)$, and $\Lambda(s, \rho) = \Gamma_\mathbb{C}(s) L(s, \rho)$. The field $K$ has one real and one complex place, and $d_K = -108$. So $\Lambda_K(s) = \Gamma_\mathbb{R}(s) \Gamma_\mathbb{C}(s) \zeta_K(s) = \Lambda_\mathbb{Q}(s) \Lambda(s, \rho)$, where $\Lambda_\mathbb{Q}(s) = \Gamma_\mathbb{R}(s) \zeta(s)$. Corollary 5.4.8 gives $\Lambda_K(s) = 108^{1/2 - s} \Lambda_K(1 - s)$ and $\Lambda_\mathbb{Q}(s) = \Lambda_\mathbb{Q}(1 - s)$. Since $\rho^\vee \cong \rho$,
$$\Lambda(s, \rho) = 108^{1/2 - s} \Lambda(1 - s, \rho).$$
This agrees with Theorem 6.6.4: $A(\rho) = 108$ by Corollary 6.4.3, and $W(\rho) = 1$. On the other hand $\rho = \mathrm{Ind}_{A_3}^{S_3} \psi$, so $\Lambda(s, \rho) = \Lambda(s, \psi)$ for a cubic character $\psi$ of $\mathrm{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3}))$, by Proposition 6.4.5. As $A(\psi) = 3 N(\mathfrak{f}(\psi)) = 108$, the conductor of $\psi$ has norm $36$. The prime $2$ is inert in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, with norm $4$, and $\sqrt{-3}$ has norm $3$. So $\mathfrak{f}(\psi) = 2 (\sqrt{-3})^2 = (6)$. By Corollary 6.5.3, $L(s, \rho) = L(s, \psi)$ is entire, which is Dedekind's theorem on $\zeta_K / \zeta$.
