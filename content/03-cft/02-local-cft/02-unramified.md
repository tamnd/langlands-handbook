## The unramified case

For an unramified extension the reciprocity map has an explicit formula, and the theorem can be proved directly. This section gives the proof. It is the model for the rest of the chapter: the valuation of $x$ gives the power of Frobenius, and the units are norms.

Let $L = K_n$ be the unramified extension of degree $n$, with residue field $\ell = k_n$. The element $\varpi$ is also a uniformizer of $L$, and the restriction to $\ell$ is an isomorphism $\mathrm{Gal}(L/K) \cong \mathrm{Gal}(\ell / k)$ by the theorem on unramified extensions of Chapter 1. For $i \ge 0$ let $U_L^{(i)}$ be the principal units of $L$ of level $i$, with $U_L^{(0)} = \mathcal{O}_L^\times$.

**Lemma 2.2.1 (norm and trace of finite fields).** Let $\ell / k$ be an extension of finite fields of degree $n$. The norm $N_{\ell/k} : \ell^\times \to k^\times$ and the trace $\mathrm{Tr}_{\ell/k} : \ell \to k$ are surjective.

*Proof.* The group $\mathrm{Gal}(\ell/k)$ is generated by $x \mapsto x^q$. Thus $N_{\ell/k}(x) = x^{1 + q + \dots + q^{n-1}} = x^{(q^n - 1)/(q-1)}$. The group $\ell^\times$ is cyclic of order $q^n - 1$, so the image of this power map has order $q - 1$, and it is $k^\times$. The trace is $\mathrm{Tr}_{\ell/k}(x) = x + x^q + \dots + x^{q^{n-1}}$. This is a polynomial of degree $q^{n-1}$, so its kernel has at most $q^{n-1}$ elements. The trace is $k$-linear, so its image is a $k$-subspace of $k$ with at least $q^n / q^{n-1} = q$ elements. Thus the image is $k$. $\square$

**Proposition 2.2.2 (units are norms).** Let $L = K_n$. Then $N_{L/K}(U_L^{(i)}) = U^{(i)}$ for each $i \ge 0$. In particular $N_{L/K}(\mathcal{O}_L^\times) = \mathcal{O}_K^\times$.

*Proof.* First we compute the norm modulo higher levels. Let $y \in \mathcal{O}_L^\times$. The conjugates of $y$ are the elements $\sigma(y)$ with $\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$, and the reduction of $\sigma(y)$ is $\bar\sigma(\bar y)$. Thus $N_{L/K}(y)$ reduces to $N_{\ell/k}(\bar y)$. Next let $i \ge 1$ and $y = 1 + \varpi^i z$ with $z \in \mathcal{O}_L$. Then
$$N_{L/K}(y) = \prod_{\sigma} (1 + \varpi^i \sigma(z)) \equiv 1 + \varpi^i \, \mathrm{Tr}_{L/K}(z) \pmod{\varpi^{2i}}.$$
As $2i \ge i + 1$, this gives $N_{L/K}(y) \equiv 1 + \varpi^i \mathrm{Tr}_{L/K}(z) \pmod{\varpi^{i+1}}$, and $\mathrm{Tr}_{L/K}(z)$ reduces to $\mathrm{Tr}_{\ell/k}(\bar z)$. These computations show three facts. The norm maps $U_L^{(i)}$ into $U^{(i)}$. On $U_L^{(0)}/U_L^{(1)} \cong \ell^\times$ it induces $N_{\ell/k}$. On $U_L^{(i)} / U_L^{(i+1)} \cong \ell$ it induces $\mathrm{Tr}_{\ell/k}$, for $i \ge 1$. By Lemma 2.2.1 all the induced maps are surjective.

Now let $u \in U^{(i)}$. We build units $y_j \in U_L^{(j)}$ for $j \ge i$ such that $u \, N_{L/K}(y_i y_{i+1} \cdots y_j)^{-1} \in U^{(j+1)}$. For $j = i$, choose $y_i$ such that $N_{L/K}(y_i)$ and $u$ have the same image in $U^{(i)}/U^{(i+1)}$. For $j > i$, the element $u_j = u \, N_{L/K}(y_i \cdots y_{j-1})^{-1}$ is in $U^{(j)}$, and we choose $y_j \in U_L^{(j)}$ with $N_{L/K}(y_j) \equiv u_j$ modulo $U^{(j+1)}$. The product $y = \prod_{j \ge i} y_j$ converges in $U_L^{(i)}$, because $y_j \to 1$. The norm is continuous, so $N_{L/K}(y) = u$. Thus $U^{(i)} \subset N_{L/K}(U_L^{(i)})$, and the other inclusion was shown above. $\square$

The proof uses only that $L / K$ is unramified. For a ramified extension the induced maps on the graded pieces are not all surjective, and the units are not all norms. Corollary 2.1.9 says exactly this.

**Theorem 2.2.3 (reciprocity for unramified extensions).** Let $L = K_n$. Then
$$N_{L/K}(L^\times) = \{x \in K^\times : n \mid v(x)\} = \langle \varpi^n \rangle \times \mathcal{O}_K^\times.$$
The map $x \mapsto \Phi_{L/K}^{v(x)}$ is a surjective homomorphism $K^\times \to \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$ with kernel $N_{L/K}(L^\times)$. It is the map $r_{L/K}$ of Theorem 2.1.3.

*Proof.* Let $y \in L^\times$. As $L$ is unramified, $v_L$ restricts to $v_K$ on $K$, and the valuation formula of Section 2.1 gives $v(N_{L/K}(y)) = n v_L(y)$. Thus every norm has valuation divisible by $n$. Conversely, $N_{L/K}(\varpi) = \varpi^n$, and $N_{L/K}(\mathcal{O}_L^\times) = \mathcal{O}_K^\times$ by Proposition 2.2.2. Thus the norm group is $\langle \varpi^n \rangle \times \mathcal{O}_K^\times$. The map $x \mapsto \Phi_{L/K}^{v(x)}$ is a homomorphism. It is surjective because $\Phi_{L/K}$ generates the cyclic group $\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$ of order $n$, and its kernel is the set of $x$ with $n \mid v(x)$. The map agrees with $r_{L/K}$, because $r_K(x)$ acts on $K^{ur}$ as $\Phi^{v(x)}$ by property 1 of Theorem 2.1.3. $\square$

The theorem proves Theorem 2.1.3 for the unramified extensions, with no cohomology. It also shows that the family of maps $x \mapsto \Phi_{K_n/K}^{v(x)}$ is compatible: for $n \mid m$, the restriction of $\Phi_{K_m/K}$ to $K_n$ is $\Phi_{K_n/K}$. The limit is the homomorphism $K^\times \to \mathrm{Gal}(K^{ur}/K)$, $x \mapsto \Phi^{v(x)}$. Its image is the subgroup $\Phi^{\mathbb{Z}}$, and its kernel is $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$.

**Example 2.2.4 (the unramified quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$).** Let $p$ be odd and let $\varepsilon \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ be a unit whose reduction is not a square in $\mathbb{F}_p$. The field $L = \mathbb{Q}_p(\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ is the unramified quadratic extension, because $X^2 - \varepsilon$ is irreducible modulo $p$. By Theorem 2.2.3, an element $x$ is a norm from $L$ if and only if $v_p(x)$ is even. For example, $p$ is not a norm, and every unit is a norm. One can check one case by hand. The form $a^2 - \varepsilon b^2$ takes every value in $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$, because it is the norm form of $\mathbb{F}_{p^2} / \mathbb{F}_p$. Hensel's lemma lifts a solution of $a^2 - \varepsilon b^2 \equiv u$ to a solution in $\mathbb{Z}_p$, since $p$ is odd and $a$ or $b$ is a unit.

**Remark 2.2.5 (the normalization in the unramified case).** In the arithmetic normalization the map is $x \mapsto \mathrm{Frob}^{v(x)}$, where $\mathrm{Frob}$ is the arithmetic Frobenius. The kernel is the same. The geometric choice $x \mapsto \Phi^{v(x)}$ has a useful feature. Let $\|w\| = q^{d(w)}$ for $w \in W_K$, where $d$ is the degree of Chapter 1. A geometric Frobenius has $d(\Phi) = -1$, so $\|\Phi^{v(x)}\| = q^{-v(x)} = |x|$. Thus the absolute value of $K^\times$ corresponds to the norm of $W_K$ with no inverse.
