## Unramified twists

Let $X_{nr}(K^\times)$ be the group of unramified characters of $K^\times$, and let $X_{nr}(W_K)$ be the group of unramified characters of $W_K$. By Proposition 4.1.4, $\chi \mapsto \chi(\varpi)$ is an isomorphism $X_{nr}(K^\times) \cong \mathbb{C}^\times$. In the same way $\phi \mapsto \phi(\Phi)$ is an isomorphism $X_{nr}(W_K) \cong \mathbb{C}^\times$, because $W_K / I_K \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is generated by the class of $\Phi$. By Theorem 4.2.3, part 3, the correspondence maps $X_{nr}(W_K)$ onto $X_{nr}(K^\times)$, and it is compatible with these two isomorphisms. In terms of $s$, it maps $\|\cdot\|^s$ to $|\cdot|^s$.

The group $X_{nr}(K^\times)$ acts on the set of all characters of $K^\times$ by multiplication. The character $\chi |\cdot|^s$ is an *unramified twist* of $\chi$.

**Proposition 4.4.1 (orbits of unramified twists).** Two characters $\chi$ and $\chi'$ of $K^\times$ are unramified twists of each other if and only if $\chi|_{\mathcal{O}_K^\times} = \chi'|_{\mathcal{O}_K^\times}$. The map $\chi \mapsto \chi|_{\mathcal{O}_K^\times}$ is a bijection from the set of orbits of $X_{nr}(K^\times)$ to the set of characters of $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$. The group $X_{nr}(K^\times)$ acts simply transitively on each orbit.

*Proof.* If $\chi' = \chi \mu$ with $\mu$ unramified, the two characters agree on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$. Conversely, if they agree on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$, then $\chi' \chi^{-1}$ is unramified. Each character of $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ extends to $K^\times$ by Proposition 4.1.5, so the map on orbits is surjective. If $\chi\mu = \chi$, then $\mu = 1$, so the action is free. $\square$

Thus each orbit is a copy of $\mathbb{C}^\times$, a complex torus of dimension 1, after the choice of a base point. The characters of $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ are countable, and each one factors through a finite group $(\mathcal{O}_K/\varpi^n)^\times$. So the set of all characters of $K^\times$ is a countable disjoint union of copies of $\mathbb{C}^\times$. For $GL_n$ the analogous decomposition of the set of irreducible representations is the theory of Bernstein components. For $GL_1$ it is the proposition above.

**Proposition 4.4.2 (invariants of a twist).** Let $\chi$ be a character of $K^\times$ and $s_0 \in \mathbb{C}$. Then
1. $a(\chi |\cdot|^{s_0}) = a(\chi)$.
2. $L(\chi|\cdot|^{s_0}, s) = L(\chi, s + s_0)$.
3. $\varepsilon(\chi|\cdot|^{s_0}, s, \psi) = \varepsilon(\chi, s + s_0, \psi)$.

*Proof.* Part 1 holds because $|\cdot|^{s_0}$ is trivial on the units. For parts 2 and 3, the zeta integral satisfies $Z(f, \chi|\cdot|^{s_0}, s) = Z(f, \chi, s + s_0)$ by definition. In the same way, $Z(\hat f, (\chi|\cdot|^{s_0})^{-1}, 1 - s) = Z(\hat f, \chi^{-1}, 1 - (s + s_0))$. Thus $\gamma(\chi|\cdot|^{s_0}, s, \psi) = \gamma(\chi, s + s_0, \psi)$. For $L$, if $\chi$ is unramified then $\chi|\cdot|^{s_0}(\varpi) q^{-s} = \chi(\varpi) q^{-s - s_0}$, and both sides are 1 otherwise. Part 3 follows from the formula for $\gamma$. $\square$

Thus the variable $s$ in the local factors is itself a twist. Many texts write $L(\chi, s)$ as $L(\chi|\cdot|^s)$ for this reason, and Tate's thesis treats $\chi |\cdot|^s$ as one quasi-character.

On the Galois side the statements are the same. Two characters of $W_K$ are unramified twists of each other if and only if they agree on $I_K$. The restriction to $I_K$ maps the orbits of $X_{nr}(W_K)$ bijectively onto the characters of $I_K$ that extend to $W_K$. By Theorem 4.2.3, and since $\mathrm{rec}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ is the image of $I_K$ in $W_K^{ab}$, these are the characters of $I_K$ that factor through the image of $I_K$ in $W_K^{ab}$, that is, through $\mathrm{Gal}(K^{ab}/K^{ur})$. Not every character of $I_K$ has this property, because $I_K$ is not abelian in general.

**Example 4.4.3 (tame orbits).** The orbits of the characters with $a(\chi) \le 1$ correspond to the $q - 1$ characters of $k^\times$. By Example 4.2.5, on the Galois side they correspond to the characters $\eta \circ \theta$ of $I_K$, where $\theta$ is the tame character and $\eta$ is a character of $\mu_{q-1}(K)$. The orbit of the trivial character of $k^\times$ is $X_{nr}$ itself.
