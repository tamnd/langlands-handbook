## Root systems, bases and the Weyl group

This section studies root systems as objects of Euclidean geometry, without reference to Lie algebras. A root system is a finite set of vectors that is stable under the reflections in its own elements, with integrality conditions on the angles and the lengths. We prove that every root system has a base, a set of simple roots in terms of which every root is an integer combination with coefficients of one sign, and that the Weyl group acts simply transitively on the bases. The examples to keep in mind are the four root systems of rank 2. We follow [Hum72], Sections 9 and 10.

### Root systems

Let $E$ be a Euclidean space, that is, a finite dimensional real vector space with a positive definite symmetric bilinear form $(\cdot, \cdot)$. For $\alpha \in E$, $\alpha \neq 0$, the *reflection* $\sigma_\alpha$ is the orthogonal map that fixes the hyperplane $P_\alpha = \{\beta : (\beta, \alpha) = 0\}$ pointwise and maps $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$. It is given by
$$\sigma_\alpha(\beta) = \beta - \frac{2(\beta, \alpha)}{(\alpha, \alpha)} \alpha.$$
We write $\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle = 2(\beta, \alpha)/(\alpha, \alpha)$. This number is linear in $\beta$ only. We also write $\alpha^\vee = 2\alpha/(\alpha, \alpha)$, so that $\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle = (\beta, \alpha^\vee)$ and $\sigma_\alpha(\beta) = \beta - (\beta, \alpha^\vee) \alpha$.

**Definition 2.5.1 (root system).** A *root system* in $E$ is a subset $\Phi \subset E$ with these properties:
1. (R1) $\Phi$ is finite, spans $E$ and does not contain $0$.
2. (R2) If $\alpha \in \Phi$, then the only multiples of $\alpha$ in $\Phi$ are $\pm \alpha$.
3. (R3) If $\alpha \in \Phi$, then $\sigma_\alpha(\Phi) = \Phi$.
4. (R4) If $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$, then $\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}$.

The elements of $\Phi$ are the *roots*, the vectors $\alpha^\vee$ are the *coroots*, and $\ell = \dim E$ is the *rank* of $\Phi$. An *isomorphism* of root systems $(E, \Phi) \to (E', \Phi')$ is a linear isomorphism $\varphi : E \to E'$ with $\varphi(\Phi) = \Phi'$ and $\langle \varphi(\beta), \varphi(\alpha) \rangle = \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$.

Some authors drop (R2). The set $\{\pm \alpha, \pm 2\alpha\}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ then becomes a root system, of type $BC_1$, and such *nonreduced* root systems appear in the theory of groups that are not split. In this chapter all root systems are reduced. Mathlib's notion is more general in another direction: a `RootPairing` consists of a family of roots in a module $M$, a family of coroots in a second module $N$, and a perfect pairing between $M$ and $N$, with no inner product given in advance. This is the form in which the dual root system and the root datum of Chapter 3 appear.

**Definition 2.5.2 (Weyl group).** The *Weyl group* $W$ of $\Phi$ is the subgroup of $GL(E)$ generated by the reflections $\sigma_\alpha$, $\alpha \in \Phi$.

By (R3), each $w \in W$ permutes $\Phi$, and $w$ is determined by this permutation because $\Phi$ spans $E$. So $W$ embeds into the symmetric group of $\Phi$, and $W$ is finite. All elements of $W$ are orthogonal maps.

**Lemma 2.5.3.** Let $w$ be an orthogonal map of $E$ with $w(\Phi) = \Phi$. Then $w \sigma_\alpha w^{-1} = \sigma_{w(\alpha)}$ and $\langle w(\beta), w(\alpha) \rangle = \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$. In particular $W$ is a normal subgroup of the group $\mathrm{Aut}(\Phi)$ of orthogonal automorphisms of $\Phi$.

*Proof.* The inner product is $w$-invariant, so $\langle w\beta, w\alpha \rangle = \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$. For $x \in E$,
$$w \sigma_\alpha w^{-1}(x) = w\big(w^{-1}x - \langle w^{-1} x, \alpha \rangle \alpha\big) = x - \langle x, w\alpha \rangle\, w\alpha = \sigma_{w\alpha}(x). \qquad \square$$

[Hum72], Section 9.1, proves a stronger form: the same holds for any linear automorphism of $E$ that maps $\Phi$ onto $\Phi$.

### Pairs of roots

**Lemma 2.5.4 (pairs of roots).** Let $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$ with $\beta \neq \pm \alpha$, and let $\theta$ be the angle between them. Then $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle = 4 \cos^2 \theta \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$. If $(\beta, \beta) \ge (\alpha, \alpha)$, then the possibilities are these:

| $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle$ | $\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$ | $\theta$ | $(\beta, \beta) / (\alpha, \alpha)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $0$ | $\pi/2$ | undetermined |
| $1$ | $1$ | $\pi/3$ | $1$ |
| $-1$ | $-1$ | $2\pi/3$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $2$ | $\pi/4$ | $2$ |
| $-1$ | $-2$ | $3\pi/4$ | $2$ |
| $1$ | $3$ | $\pi/6$ | $3$ |
| $-1$ | $-3$ | $5\pi/6$ | $3$ |

Lean: `RootPairing.coxeterWeight`, from Mathlib, is the product $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$

*Proof.* We have $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle = 4 (\alpha, \beta)^2 / ((\alpha, \alpha)(\beta, \beta)) = 4 \cos^2 \theta$. This is a nonnegative integer by (R4). It is less than 4, because $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are not proportional by (R2). The two factors have the same sign. If $(\beta, \beta) \ge (\alpha, \alpha)$, then $|\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle| \le |\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle|$, so $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle \in \{0, \pm 1\}$. The ratio of the squared lengths is $\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle / \langle \alpha, \beta \rangle$ when $(\alpha, \beta) \neq 0$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.5.5.** Let $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$ be nonproportional. If $(\alpha, \beta) > 0$, then $\alpha - \beta \in \Phi$. If $(\alpha, \beta) < 0$, then $\alpha + \beta \in \Phi$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Assume $(\alpha, \beta) > 0$. By Lemma 2.5.4, $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle = 1$ or $\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle = 1$. In the first case $\sigma_\beta(\alpha) = \alpha - \beta \in \Phi$. In the second case $\sigma_\alpha(\beta) = \beta - \alpha \in \Phi$, and then $\alpha - \beta = -(\beta - \alpha) \in \Phi$, because $\sigma_\gamma(\gamma) = -\gamma$ for each root $\gamma$. For $(\alpha, \beta) < 0$ apply the first case to $\alpha$ and $-\beta$. $\square$

**Proposition 2.5.6 (root strings).** Let $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$ be nonproportional. Let $r$ and $q$ be the largest integers with $\beta - r\alpha \in \Phi$ and $\beta + q \alpha \in \Phi$. Then $\beta + i\alpha \in \Phi$ for all $-r \le i \le q$, the reflection $\sigma_\alpha$ reverses this string, $r - q = \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$, and the string has at most 4 roots.

Lean: `RootPairing.chainTopCoeff` and `RootPairing.chainBotCoeff`, from Mathlib, are $q$ and $r$

*Proof.* Suppose that the string has a gap. Then there are $p < s$ in $[-r, q]$ with $\beta + p\alpha \in \Phi$, $\beta + (p+1)\alpha \notin \Phi$, $\beta + (s-1)\alpha \notin \Phi$ and $\beta + s\alpha \in \Phi$. By Lemma 2.5.5, $(\beta + p\alpha, \alpha) \ge 0$ and $(\beta + s\alpha, \alpha) \le 0$. Subtracting, $(p - s)(\alpha, \alpha) \ge 0$, which contradicts $p < s$. So the string is unbroken. The reflection $\sigma_\alpha$ maps $\beta + i\alpha$ to $\beta - (\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle + i)\alpha$, so it maps the string onto itself, and it reverses the order. Thus it exchanges the ends: $-\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle - q = -r$. For the length, apply this to the first root $\beta' = \beta - r\alpha$ of the string. Its string is the same set, with $r' = 0$ and $q' = r + q$. So $r + q = -\langle \beta', \alpha \rangle \le 3$ by Lemma 2.5.4. $\square$

For a root system that comes from a semisimple Lie algebra, Theorem 2.4.10 already gave the first three statements.

### The root systems of rank 2

**Example 2.5.7 (rank 1 and rank 2).** In rank 1 the only root system is $A_1 = \{\pm \alpha\}$, with $W = \{1, \sigma_\alpha\}$ of order 2. In rank 2, Lemma 2.5.4 leaves four possibilities for the smallest angle between two roots, and each gives one root system. The Weyl group is the dihedral group of the regular polygon spanned by the roots.
1. $A_1 \times A_1$: the roots $\pm \varepsilon_1$, $\pm \varepsilon_2$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Here $|\Phi| = 4$, the smallest angle is $\pi/2$, and $|W| = 4$.
2. $A_2$: the roots $\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j$, $i \neq j$, in the plane $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Here $|\Phi| = 6$, all roots have the same length, the smallest angle is $\pi/3$, and $W \cong S_3$ has order 6.
3. $B_2$: the roots $\pm \varepsilon_1$, $\pm \varepsilon_2$, $\pm \varepsilon_1 \pm \varepsilon_2$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Here $|\Phi| = 8$, there are two root lengths with ratio $\sqrt{2}$, the smallest angle is $\pi/4$, and $|W| = 8$.
4. $G_2$: in the plane $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$, the six short roots $\pm(\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j)$ and the six long roots $\pm(2\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j - \varepsilon_k)$, where $\{i, j, k\} = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Here $|\Phi| = 12$, the ratio of the lengths is $\sqrt{3}$, the smallest angle is $\pi/6$, and $|W| = 12$.

For $G_2$, take $\alpha = \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2$ and $\beta = -2\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \varepsilon_3$. Then $(\alpha, \alpha) = 2$, $(\beta, \beta) = 6$ and $(\alpha, \beta) = -3$, so $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle = -1$ and $\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle = -3$. The $\alpha$-string through $\beta$ is $\beta, \beta + \alpha, \beta + 2\alpha, \beta + 3\alpha$, of the maximal length 4. These four root systems are, up to isomorphism, the root systems of $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \times \mathfrak{sl}_2$, $\mathfrak{sl}_3$, $\mathfrak{so}_5 \cong \mathfrak{sp}_4$, and the exceptional Lie algebra of dimension 14. The proof that there are no others is [Hum72], Section 9.3, and it is also a special case of Theorem 2.6.6.

### Bases

**Definition 2.5.8 (base).** A *base* of $\Phi$ is a subset $\Delta \subset \Phi$ with these properties:
1. $\Delta$ is a basis of $E$;
2. each root $\beta$ can be written as $\beta = \sum_{\alpha \in \Delta} k_\alpha \alpha$ with integers $k_\alpha$ that are all $\ge 0$ or all $\le 0$.

The elements of $\Delta$ are the *simple roots*. A root is *positive*, written $\beta > 0$, if all $k_\alpha \ge 0$, and *negative* otherwise. We write $\Phi^+$ and $\Phi^-$ for the sets of positive and negative roots, so $\Phi = \Phi^+ \sqcup \Phi^-$ and $\Phi^- = -\Phi^+$. The *height* of $\beta$ is $\mathrm{ht}(\beta) = \sum_\alpha k_\alpha$. We write $\mu \prec \lambda$ if $\lambda - \mu$ is a sum of positive roots, or $\lambda = \mu$.

The coefficients $k_\alpha$ are unique because $\Delta$ is a basis. So $|\Delta| = \ell$.

**Lemma 2.5.9.** If $\Delta$ is a base and $\alpha \neq \beta$ are in $\Delta$, then $(\alpha, \beta) \le 0$ and $\alpha - \beta \notin \Phi$.

Lean: `RootPairing.Base.sub_notMem_range_root`, from Mathlib, for the second part

*Proof.* The root $\alpha - \beta$ would have the coefficients $1$ and $-1$, so it is not a root. Then $(\alpha, \beta) \le 0$ by Lemma 2.5.5. $\square$

So the angle between two simple roots is obtuse or right. An element $\gamma \in E$ is *regular* if $(\gamma, \alpha) \neq 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Phi$, and *singular* otherwise. Regular elements exist, because $E$ is not the union of the finitely many hyperplanes $P_\alpha$. For a regular $\gamma$, let $\Phi^+(\gamma) = \{\alpha \in \Phi : (\gamma, \alpha) > 0\}$. Then $\Phi = \Phi^+(\gamma) \sqcup -\Phi^+(\gamma)$. An element $\alpha \in \Phi^+(\gamma)$ is *decomposable* if $\alpha = \beta_1 + \beta_2$ with $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in \Phi^+(\gamma)$, and *indecomposable* otherwise. Let $\Delta(\gamma)$ be the set of indecomposable elements of $\Phi^+(\gamma)$.

**Theorem 2.5.10 (existence of bases).** For each regular $\gamma \in E$, the set $\Delta(\gamma)$ is a base of $\Phi$, and $\Phi^+(\gamma)$ is its set of positive roots. Every base of $\Phi$ is of this form.

Lean: `RootPairing.nonempty_base`, from Mathlib, for a finite crystallographic reduced root pairing over a field of characteristic 0

*Proof.* We follow [Hum72], Section 10.1, in five steps.
1. Each $\alpha \in \Phi^+(\gamma)$ is a combination of $\Delta(\gamma)$ with nonnegative integer coefficients. If not, choose such an $\alpha$ with $(\gamma, \alpha)$ minimal among the counterexamples. It is not in $\Delta(\gamma)$, so $\alpha = \beta_1 + \beta_2$ with $\beta_i \in \Phi^+(\gamma)$. Then $(\gamma, \alpha) = (\gamma, \beta_1) + (\gamma, \beta_2)$ with both terms positive, so $(\gamma, \beta_i) < (\gamma, \alpha)$. By minimality, $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are combinations of the required form, and so is $\alpha$. This is a contradiction.
2. If $\alpha \neq \beta$ are in $\Delta(\gamma)$, then $(\alpha, \beta) \le 0$. Otherwise $\alpha - \beta \in \Phi$ by Lemma 2.5.5, so $\alpha - \beta$ or $\beta - \alpha$ is in $\Phi^+(\gamma)$. In the first case $\alpha = \beta + (\alpha - \beta)$ is decomposable, and in the second case $\beta$ is.
3. $\Delta(\gamma)$ is linearly independent. Let $\sum_{\alpha} r_\alpha \alpha = 0$ be a relation. Put the terms with $r_\alpha > 0$ on one side and the others on the other side: $\varepsilon = \sum_{\alpha \in A} s_\alpha \alpha = \sum_{\beta \in B} t_\beta \beta$, with $A \cap B = \emptyset$ and all $s_\alpha, t_\beta > 0$. Then $(\varepsilon, \varepsilon) = \sum_{\alpha, \beta} s_\alpha t_\beta (\alpha, \beta) \le 0$ by step 2, so $\varepsilon = 0$. Then $0 = (\gamma, \varepsilon) = \sum_{\alpha \in A} s_\alpha (\gamma, \alpha)$, and each $(\gamma, \alpha) > 0$, so $A = \emptyset$. In the same way $B = \emptyset$.
4. $\Delta(\gamma)$ is a base. Each root is in $\Phi^+(\gamma)$ or in $-\Phi^+(\gamma)$, so step 1 gives condition 2 of Definition 2.5.8. Then $\Delta(\gamma)$ spans $E$, because $\Phi$ does, and it is a basis by step 3. Its positive roots are $\Phi^+(\gamma)$.
5. Every base $\Delta$ is some $\Delta(\gamma)$. Choose $\gamma$ with $(\gamma, \alpha) > 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$, for example $\gamma = \sum_\alpha \varpi'_\alpha$, where $(\varpi'_\alpha)$ is the basis dual to $\Delta$ for the inner product. Then $(\gamma, \beta) > 0$ for each $\beta \in \Phi^+$ and $(\gamma, \beta) < 0$ for each $\beta \in \Phi^-$. So $\gamma$ is regular and $\Phi^+ = \Phi^+(\gamma)$. A simple root is not the sum of two positive roots, by the uniqueness of the coefficients, so $\Delta \subset \Delta(\gamma)$. Both sets have $\ell$ elements by step 3, so $\Delta = \Delta(\gamma)$. $\square$

The connected components of $E \setminus \bigcup_{\alpha \in \Phi} P_\alpha$ are the *Weyl chambers*. Two regular elements $\gamma$ and $\gamma'$ lie in the same chamber if and only if $\Phi^+(\gamma) = \Phi^+(\gamma')$, that is, if and only if $\Delta(\gamma) = \Delta(\gamma')$. So the chambers correspond bijectively to the bases. The chamber of the base $\Delta$ is $\mathfrak{C}(\Delta) = \{\gamma \in E : (\gamma, \alpha) > 0 \text{ for all } \alpha \in \Delta\}$, the *fundamental chamber*. The group $W$ permutes the chambers and the bases, and $w(\mathfrak{C}(\Delta)) = \mathfrak{C}(w\Delta)$.

**Example 2.5.11 (bases of the classical root systems).** In $A_{n-1} = \{\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j\}$, take $\gamma = (n, n-1, \dots, 1)$, projected to the hyperplane $\sum x_i = 0$. Then $\Phi^+(\gamma) = \{\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j : i < j\}$, and $\Delta(\gamma) = \{\alpha_i = \varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i+1} : 1 \le i \le n-1\}$, because $\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j = \alpha_i + \alpha_{i+1} + \cdots + \alpha_{j-1}$ for $i < j$. In $B_2$, the same $\gamma = (2, 1)$ gives the positive roots $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2, \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2, \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2$ and the base $\{\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2, \varepsilon_2\}$. In $G_2$ with the notation of Example 2.5.7, the base $\{\alpha, \beta\}$ with $\alpha = \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2$ and $\beta = -2\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \varepsilon_3$ gives the positive roots $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\alpha + \beta$, $2\alpha + \beta$, $3\alpha + \beta$ and $3\alpha + 2\beta$.

### Simple roots and the Weyl group

Fix a base $\Delta$, and write $\sigma_i = \sigma_{\alpha_i}$ for the simple reflections, where $\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_\ell\}$.

**Lemma 2.5.12.** Let $\alpha \in \Delta$. Then $\sigma_\alpha$ permutes $\Phi^+ \setminus \{\alpha\}$. If $\delta = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\beta \in \Phi^+} \beta$, then $\sigma_\alpha(\delta) = \delta - \alpha$.

*Proof.* Let $\beta \in \Phi^+$, $\beta \neq \alpha$, and write $\beta = \sum_{\gamma \in \Delta} k_\gamma \gamma$. By (R2), $\beta$ is not a multiple of $\alpha$, so $k_\gamma > 0$ for some $\gamma \neq \alpha$. The root $\sigma_\alpha(\beta) = \beta - \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle \alpha$ has the same coefficient $k_\gamma > 0$. So it is positive, and it is not $\alpha$. Thus $\sigma_\alpha$ maps $\Phi^+ \setminus \{\alpha\}$ into itself, and it is a bijection because it is injective. Then $\sigma_\alpha(\delta) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\beta \in \Phi^+ \setminus \{\alpha\}} \beta - \frac{1}{2} \alpha = \delta - \alpha$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.5.13 (deletion).** Let $\alpha_{i_1}, \dots, \alpha_{i_t}$ be simple roots, not necessarily distinct, and write $\sigma_{(k)} = \sigma_{\alpha_{i_k}}$. If $\sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t-1)}(\alpha_{i_t})$ is negative, then there is $1 \le s < t$ with
$$\sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t)} = \sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(s-1)} \sigma_{(s+1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t-1)}.$$

*Proof.* Put $\beta_k = \sigma_{(k+1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t-1)}(\alpha_{i_t})$ for $0 \le k \le t-1$, so that $\beta_{t-1} = \alpha_{i_t}$ is positive and $\beta_0$ is negative. Let $s$ be the least index with $\beta_s > 0$. Then $s \ge 1$ and $\sigma_{(s)}(\beta_s) = \beta_{s-1} < 0$. By Lemma 2.5.12, $\beta_s = \alpha_{i_s}$. Let $w = \sigma_{(s+1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t-1)}$, so $w(\alpha_{i_t}) = \alpha_{i_s}$. By Lemma 2.5.3, $\sigma_{(s)} = w \sigma_{(t)} w^{-1}$, so $\sigma_{(s)} w \sigma_{(t)} = w$. Substituting this into $\sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t)} = \sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(s-1)} (\sigma_{(s)} w \sigma_{(t)})$ gives the claim. $\square$

An expression $w = \sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t)}$ as a product of simple reflections with $t$ as small as possible is *reduced*, and then $t = \ell(w)$ is the *length* of $w$. By Theorem 2.5.14 below, each $w \in W$ has such an expression. The proof of part 5 of that theorem shows that $w(\alpha_{i_t}) < 0$ for each reduced expression.

**Theorem 2.5.14 (the Weyl group and the bases).** Let $\Delta$ be a base of $\Phi$.
1. For each regular $\gamma \in E$ there is $w \in W$ with $(w(\gamma), \alpha) > 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$. So $W$ acts transitively on the Weyl chambers.
2. If $\Delta'$ is another base, then $w(\Delta') = \Delta$ for some $w \in W$.
3. For each $\beta \in \Phi$ there is $w \in W$ with $w(\beta) \in \Delta$.
4. $W$ is generated by the simple reflections $\sigma_\alpha$, $\alpha \in \Delta$.
5. If $w(\Delta) = \Delta$ for some $w \in W$, then $w = 1$. So $W$ acts simply transitively on the bases and on the chambers.

*Proof.* Let $W' \subset W$ be the subgroup generated by the simple reflections. We prove parts 1 to 3 for $W'$ in place of $W$, and then part 4 shows $W' = W$.

For part 1, choose $w \in W'$ with $(w(\gamma), \delta)$ as large as possible, which is possible because $W'$ is finite. For $\alpha \in \Delta$, the element $\sigma_\alpha w$ is also in $W'$, and Lemma 2.5.12 gives
$$(w(\gamma), \delta) \ge (\sigma_\alpha w(\gamma), \delta) = (w(\gamma), \sigma_\alpha(\delta)) = (w(\gamma), \delta) - (w(\gamma), \alpha).$$
So $(w(\gamma), \alpha) \ge 0$. Equality would give $(\gamma, w^{-1}\alpha) = 0$, but $\gamma$ is regular. So $(w(\gamma), \alpha) > 0$.

For part 2, write $\Delta' = \Delta(\gamma')$ with $\gamma'$ regular, by Theorem 2.5.10. Part 1 gives $w \in W'$ with $w(\gamma') \in \mathfrak{C}(\Delta)$. Then $\Phi^+(w(\gamma')) = \Phi^+$, so $\Delta(w(\gamma')) = \Delta$. Also $w(\Delta(\gamma')) = \Delta(w(\gamma'))$, because $w$ preserves $\Phi$ and the inner product. So $w(\Delta') = \Delta$.

For part 3, by part 2 it suffices to find a base that contains $\beta$. The hyperplanes $P_\alpha$ for $\alpha \neq \pm \beta$ meet $P_\beta$ in proper subspaces, so there is $\gamma_0 \in P_\beta$ outside all of them. Choose $\gamma$ near $\gamma_0$ with $(\gamma, \beta) = \varepsilon > 0$ and $|(\gamma, \alpha)| > \varepsilon$ for all $\alpha \neq \pm \beta$. Then $\gamma$ is regular, $\beta \in \Phi^+(\gamma)$, and $\beta$ is indecomposable: if $\beta = \beta_1 + \beta_2$ with $\beta_i \in \Phi^+(\gamma)$, then $(\gamma, \beta_i) > \varepsilon$ and $(\gamma, \beta) > 2\varepsilon$. So $\beta \in \Delta(\gamma)$.

For part 4, let $\beta \in \Phi$. By part 3 there are $w \in W'$ and $\alpha \in \Delta$ with $\beta = w(\alpha)$. By Lemma 2.5.3, $\sigma_\beta = w \sigma_\alpha w^{-1} \in W'$. So $W = W'$.

For part 5, assume that $w(\Delta) = \Delta$ and $w \neq 1$. Write $w = \sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t)}$ with $t \ge 1$ minimal. If $w(\alpha_{i_t}) = -\sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t-1)}(\alpha_{i_t})$ is positive, then $\sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t-1)}(\alpha_{i_t})$ is negative, and Lemma 2.5.13 writes $w$ as a product of $t - 2$ simple reflections. This contradicts the minimality of $t$. So $w(\alpha_{i_t}) < 0$, which contradicts $w(\Delta) = \Delta$. $\square$

### Length and the longest element

For $w \in W$, let $n(w)$ be the number of $\beta \in \Phi^+$ with $w(\beta) < 0$.

**Proposition 2.5.15 (length).** For all $w \in W$, $\ell(w) = n(w)$. There is a unique element $w_0 \in W$ with $w_0(\Phi^+) = \Phi^-$. It is the unique element of maximal length, $\ell(w_0) = |\Phi^+|$, $w_0^2 = 1$ and $w_0(\Delta) = -\Delta$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Let $\alpha \in \Delta$. By Lemma 2.5.12, $n(w \sigma_\alpha) = n(w) - 1$ if $w(\alpha) < 0$ and $n(w \sigma_\alpha) = n(w) + 1$ if $w(\alpha) > 0$, because $\sigma_\alpha$ permutes $\Phi^+ \setminus \{\alpha\}$ and maps $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$. So $n(w) \le \ell(w)$ by induction on $\ell(w)$. We prove $n(w) = \ell(w)$ by induction on $t = \ell(w)$, the case $t = 0$ being clear. Let $w = \sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t)}$ be reduced. As in the proof of part 5 of Theorem 2.5.14, $w(\alpha_{i_t}) < 0$. The expression $\sigma_{(1)} \cdots \sigma_{(t-1)}$ of $w \sigma_{(t)}$ is reduced, so $n(w \sigma_{(t)}) = t - 1$ by induction. Since $(w \sigma_{(t)})(\alpha_{i_t}) = -w(\alpha_{i_t}) > 0$, we get $n(w) = n(w \sigma_{(t)} \sigma_{(t)}) = t$.

The set $-\Delta$ is a base, with positive roots $\Phi^-$. By parts 2 and 5 of Theorem 2.5.14, there is exactly one $w_0$ with $w_0(\Delta) = -\Delta$. An element $w$ satisfies $w(\Phi^+) = \Phi^-$ if and only if $w(\Delta) = -\Delta$. So $w_0$ is the unique element with $n(w) = |\Phi^+|$, and every other element has smaller length. Finally $w_0^2(\Delta) = \Delta$, so $w_0^2 = 1$. $\square$

**Example 2.5.16 (the classical Weyl groups).** Let $(\varepsilon_i)$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
1. For $A_{n-1}$, the reflection $\sigma_{\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j}$ exchanges the coordinates $i$ and $j$. So $W \cong S_n$, acting on the hyperplane $\sum x_i = 0$ by permutations of the coordinates. The simple reflections are the transpositions $(i, i+1)$, and Theorem 2.5.14 contains the familiar fact that they generate $S_n$. The length of a permutation is its number of inversions, and $w_0$ is the permutation $i \mapsto n + 1 - i$, with $\binom{n}{2}$ inversions.
2. For $B_n = \{\pm \varepsilon_i, \pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j\}$ and $C_n = \{\pm 2\varepsilon_i, \pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j\}$, the reflections in $\varepsilon_i$ and in $2\varepsilon_i$ change the sign of the coordinate $i$. So $W$ is the group of signed permutations, $W \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2)^n \rtimes S_n$, of order $2^n n!$. Here $w_0 = -1$.
3. For $D_n = \{\pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j\}$, $n \ge 2$, the reflection in $\varepsilon_i + \varepsilon_j$ maps $\varepsilon_i \mapsto -\varepsilon_j$ and $\varepsilon_j \mapsto -\varepsilon_i$. So $W$ is the group of permutations with an even number of sign changes, of order $2^{n-1} n!$. Here $w_0 = -1$ if $n$ is even. If $n$ is odd, then $-1 \notin W$, and $w_0$ is $-1$ composed with the sign change of the last coordinate.

### Irreducible root systems

**Definition 2.5.17 (irreducible root system).** A root system $\Phi$ is *irreducible* if it is not the union of two nonempty subsets that are orthogonal to each other.

**Theorem 2.5.18 (irreducible components and the highest root).** Let $\Phi$ be a root system with base $\Delta$.
1. $\Phi$ is irreducible if and only if $\Delta$ is not the union of two nonempty orthogonal subsets.
2. $\Phi$ is the orthogonal union of irreducible root systems $\Phi_i$ in subspaces $E_i$ with $E = \bigoplus_i E_i$, in a unique way, and $\Delta \cap E_i$ is a base of $\Phi_i$.
3. If $\Phi$ is irreducible, then there is a unique root $\theta$ that is maximal for the order $\prec$. Its coefficients in terms of $\Delta$ are all positive, and $(\theta, \alpha) \ge 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$.
4. If $\Phi$ is irreducible, then $W$ acts irreducibly on $E$, the roots have at most two lengths, and $W$ acts transitively on the roots of a given length.

*Proof sketch.* For part 1, if $\Delta = \Delta_1 \sqcup \Delta_2$ is an orthogonal decomposition, then each root is $W$-conjugate to a simple root by Theorem 2.5.14, and $W$ is generated by the reflections in $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$, which commute. So every root lies in the span of $\Delta_1$ or in the span of $\Delta_2$. For part 3, take a maximal root $\theta = \sum k_\alpha \alpha$. Then $\theta > 0$, and $(\theta, \alpha) \ge 0$ for $\alpha \in \Delta$, since otherwise $\theta + \alpha$ would be a root by Lemma 2.5.5. Assume that the set $\Delta_2$ of simple roots $\alpha$ with $k_\alpha = 0$ is not empty, and let $\Delta_1$ be its complement. By part 1 there are $\alpha \in \Delta_2$ and $\alpha' \in \Delta_1$ with $(\alpha, \alpha') \neq 0$, so $(\alpha, \alpha') < 0$ by Lemma 2.5.9. Then $(\theta, \alpha) = \sum_{\alpha'' \in \Delta_1} k_{\alpha''} (\alpha'', \alpha) < 0$, a contradiction. If $\theta'$ is a second maximal root, then $\theta'$ has positive coefficients too, so $(\theta, \theta') > 0$, and $\theta - \theta'$ is a root by Lemma 2.5.5. Then $\theta \prec \theta'$ or $\theta' \prec \theta$, which contradicts the maximality. See [Hum72], Section 10.4, for the details and for part 4. $\square$

The root $\theta$ is the *highest root*. For $A_{n-1}$ it is $\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_n = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_{n-1}$. For $G_2$ with the base of Example 2.5.11 it is $3\alpha + 2\beta$. In an irreducible root system with two root lengths, the roots are called *long* and *short*, and the highest root is long.
