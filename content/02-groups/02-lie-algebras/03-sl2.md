## Representations of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$

This section classifies the finite dimensional representations of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ over an algebraically closed field $F$ of characteristic 0. For each integer $m \ge 0$ there is exactly one irreducible module $V(m)$ of dimension $m + 1$, and every finite dimensional module is a direct sum of such modules. The eigenvalues of $h$ on a finite dimensional module are integers, and this integrality is the source of all the integrality statements about roots in Sections 2.4 to 2.7. The example to keep in mind is the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree $m$ in two variables. We follow [Hum72], Section 7.

### Weights and maximal vectors

Let $(e, h, f)$ be the standard basis of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ from Example 2.1.4, with $[h, e] = 2e$, $[h, f] = -2f$ and $[e, f] = h$. Let $V$ be an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module.

**Definition 2.3.1 (weights and maximal vectors).** For $\lambda \in F$ let $V_\lambda = \{v \in V : h v = \lambda v\}$. If $V_\lambda \neq 0$, then $\lambda$ is a *weight* of $V$, and $V_\lambda$ is its *weight space*. A *maximal vector* of weight $\lambda$ is a vector $v \in V_\lambda$, $v \neq 0$, with $e v = 0$.

**Lemma 2.3.2.** For all $\lambda \in F$ we have $e V_\lambda \subset V_{\lambda + 2}$ and $f V_\lambda \subset V_{\lambda - 2}$. If $V$ is finite dimensional and $V \neq 0$, then $V$ has a maximal vector.

*Proof.* For $v \in V_\lambda$, we have $h e v = e h v + [h, e] v = (\lambda + 2) e v$, and in the same way $h f v = (\lambda - 2) f v$. If $V \neq 0$ is finite dimensional, then $h$ has an eigenvector $v$, because $F$ is algebraically closed. Say $v \in V_\lambda$. The nonzero vectors among $v, ev, e^2 v, \dots$ lie in the distinct eigenspaces $V_{\lambda + 2k}$, so they are linearly independent, and $e^k v = 0$ for some $k \ge 1$. If $k$ is the least such integer, then $e^{k-1} v$ is a maximal vector. $\square$

The sums $\sum_\lambda V_\lambda$ are always direct, but they need not be all of $V$ when $V$ is infinite dimensional. Corollary 2.3.6 below shows that they are all of $V$ when $V$ is finite dimensional.

**Lemma 2.3.3.** Let $v_0 \in V_\lambda$ be a maximal vector. Put $v_{-1} = 0$ and $v_i = f^i v_0 / i!$ for $i \ge 0$. Then for all $i \ge 0$:
1. $h v_i = (\lambda - 2i) v_i$;
2. $f v_i = (i + 1) v_{i+1}$;
3. $e v_i = (\lambda - i + 1) v_{i-1}$.

Lean: `IsSl2Triple.HasPrimitiveVectorWith.lie_e_pow_succ_toEnd_f`, from Mathlib, is part 3 in the form $e f^{n+1} v_0 = (n+1)(\lambda - n) f^n v_0$

*Proof.* Part 1 follows from Lemma 2.3.2, and part 2 is the definition. We prove part 3 by induction on $i$. For $i = 0$ it says $e v_0 = 0$. For $i \ge 1$,
$$i\, e v_i = e f v_{i-1} = [e, f] v_{i-1} + f e v_{i-1} = h v_{i-1} + (\lambda - i + 2) f v_{i-2}.$$
By parts 1 and 2 this is $(\lambda - 2i + 2) v_{i-1} + (\lambda - i + 2)(i - 1) v_{i-1} = i (\lambda - i + 1) v_{i-1}$. Divide by $i$. $\square$

### The irreducible modules

**Theorem 2.3.4 (irreducible $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-modules).** Let $V$ be an irreducible $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module of finite dimension $m + 1$.
1. $V$ is the direct sum of the weight spaces $V_\mu$ for $\mu = m, m - 2, \dots, -m + 2, -m$, and each of them has dimension 1.
2. Up to a scalar, $V$ has a unique maximal vector, and its weight is $m$.
3. In the basis $v_0, \dots, v_m$ of Lemma 2.3.3, the action is given by parts 1 to 3 of Lemma 2.3.3, with $\lambda = m$ and $v_{m+1} = 0$.

Conversely, for each integer $m \ge 0$ these formulas define an irreducible module $V(m)$ of dimension $m + 1$. So there is exactly one irreducible module of each dimension, up to isomorphism.

*Proof.* By Lemma 2.3.2, $V$ has a maximal vector $v_0$ of some weight $\lambda$. The nonzero vectors among the $v_i$ of Lemma 2.3.3 have distinct weights $\lambda - 2i$, so they are linearly independent. Since $V$ is finite dimensional, there is a least integer $n$ with $v_{n+1} = 0$, and then $v_i = 0$ for all $i > n$. The span of $v_0, \dots, v_n$ is stable under $e$, $f$ and $h$ by Lemma 2.3.3, and it is nonzero, so it is $V$. Thus $n = m$. Part 3 of Lemma 2.3.3 for $i = m + 1$ gives $0 = e v_{m+1} = (\lambda - m) v_m$, and $v_m \neq 0$, so $\lambda = m$. This proves parts 1 and 3. For part 2, a maximal vector lies in a weight space $V_\mu$ with $V_{\mu + 2} = 0$, so $\mu = m$, and $V_m = F v_0$.

For the converse, let $V(m)$ have the basis $v_0, \dots, v_m$, and define $e$, $f$ and $h$ by the formulas, with $v_{-1} = v_{m+1} = 0$. We check the three relations on $v_i$. First $[h, e] v_i = ((\lambda - 2i + 2) - (\lambda - 2i))(\lambda - i + 1) v_{i-1} = 2 e v_i$, and $[h, f] = -2f$ in the same way. Next,
$$[e, f] v_i = (i + 1)(m - i) v_i - i (m - i + 1) v_i = (m - 2i) v_i = h v_i.$$
For $i = m$ the first term is $0$ in both readings, because $m - i = 0$. So $V(m)$ is a module. A nonzero submodule $W$ contains an eigenvector of $h$, so it contains some $v_i$, because the weights $m - 2i$ are distinct. Then $e^i v_i$ is a nonzero multiple of $v_0$, and $W$ contains $v_0$ and all $f^j v_0$. So $W = V(m)$. $\square$

**Example 2.3.5 (the polynomial model).** Let $P_m$ be the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree $m$ in $x$ and $y$. The derivations
$$e = x \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \qquad f = y \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \qquad h = x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} - y \frac{\partial}{\partial y}$$
preserve $P_m$, and $[e, f] = h$, $[h, e] = 2e$, $[h, f] = -2f$, by a direct computation. So $P_m$ is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module of dimension $m + 1$. The monomial $x^{m-i} y^i$ has weight $(m - i) - i = m - 2i$. The polynomial $x^m$ is a maximal vector, and
$$v_i = \frac{1}{i!} f^i x^m = \binom{m}{i} x^{m-i} y^i.$$
So $P_m \cong V(m)$. For $m = 1$, the space $P_1$ with the basis $x, y$ is the standard module $F^2$ of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. In general $P_m$ is the $m$-th symmetric power $\mathrm{Sym}^m F^2$, because $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ acts on polynomials by derivations. The group $SL_2$ acts on $P_m$ by the linear substitutions of $x$ and $y$, and the action of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is the differential of this action. Chapter 4 shows that the $P_m$ are the irreducible representations of $SL_2$.

### Arbitrary finite dimensional modules

**Corollary 2.3.6 (finite dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-modules).** Let $V$ be a finite dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module.
1. $V$ is a direct sum of modules $V(m)$, and $h$ acts diagonalizably on $V$.
2. All weights of $V$ are integers, and $\dim V_\mu = \dim V_{-\mu}$ for all $\mu$.
3. The number of summands in part 1 is $\dim V_0 + \dim V_1$.
4. For $r \ge 0$, the multiplicity of $V(r)$ in $V$ is $\dim V_r - \dim V_{r+2}$.
5. The maps $e^\mu : V_{-\mu} \to V_\mu$ and $f^\mu : V_\mu \to V_{-\mu}$ are isomorphisms for each integer $\mu \ge 0$.

*Proof.* By Weyl's theorem (Theorem 2.2.22), $V$ is a direct sum of irreducible modules. By Theorem 2.3.4, each of them is some $V(m)$, and $h$ is diagonal on $V(m)$. This gives parts 1 and 2. Each $V(m)$ has exactly one of the weights $0$ and $1$, with multiplicity 1: the weight $0$ if $m$ is even and the weight $1$ if $m$ is odd. This gives part 3. The module $V(m)$ contributes 1 to $\dim V_r - \dim V_{r+2}$ if $m = r$, and $0$ otherwise, because its weights are $m, m - 2, \dots, -m$. This gives part 4. For part 5, it suffices to look at one summand $V(m)$ with $\mu \le m$ and $\mu \equiv m \bmod 2$. Write $\mu = m - 2i$. Then $f^\mu v_i$ is a nonzero multiple of $v_{i + \mu} = v_{m - i}$ by Lemma 2.3.3, and $e^\mu v_{m-i}$ is a nonzero multiple of $v_i$, because the factors $\lambda - j + 1$ with $\lambda = m$ and $j \le m$ are nonzero. $\square$

The proof of part 1 used Weyl's theorem to decompose $V$, and the diagonalizability of $h$ then came from Theorem 2.3.4. [Hum72], Section 7.1, instead deduces the diagonalizability of $h$ on every finite dimensional module from the preservation of the Jordan decomposition (Theorem 2.2.21). Both proofs rest on the Casimir element of Theorem 2.2.22.

**Example 2.3.7 (the Casimir element).** Let $c = ef + fe + \tfrac{1}{2} h^2$, an endomorphism of a given module $V$. A direct computation with the bracket relations shows that $c$ commutes with $e$, $f$ and $h$. For example $[e, ef + fe] = e[e, f] + [e, f] e = eh + he$, and $[e, h^2] = [e, h] h + h [e, h] = -2(eh + he)$. On $V(m)$ the operator $c$ is a scalar. Indeed, $c$ commutes with $h$, so it preserves $V(m)_m = F v_0$, and $c v_0 = s v_0$ for some $s \in F$. Since $c$ commutes with $f$, it acts by $s$ on each $v_i = f^i v_0 / i!$. Its value on $v_0$ is
$$c v_0 = e f v_0 + f e v_0 + \tfrac{1}{2} m^2 v_0 = m v_0 + 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} m^2 v_0 = \tfrac{1}{2} m(m+2) v_0,$$
by Lemma 2.3.3. The dual basis of $(e, h, f)$ for the Killing form of Example 2.2.16 is $(f/4, h/8, e/4)$. So the Casimir element of the adjoint representation, as in the proof of Theorem 2.2.22, is $\tfrac{1}{4}(ef + fe) + \tfrac{1}{8} h^2 = c / 4$. On the adjoint module $V(2)$ it acts by $\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 4 = 1 = \dim \mathfrak{g} / \dim V$, as it must.

**Example 2.3.8 (small modules).** The trivial module $F$ is $V(0)$. The standard module $F^2$ is $V(1)$, with $v_0 = e_1$ and $v_1 = e_2$. The adjoint module is $V(2)$, with the maximal vector $v_0 = e$, then $v_1 = [f, e] = -h$ and $v_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}[f, -h] = -f$. The weights of the adjoint module are $2, 0, -2$, the eigenvalues of $\mathrm{ad}\, h$ in Example 2.1.4. The module $F^2 \otimes F^2$ has the weights $2, 0, 0, -2$. By Corollary 2.3.6 it is $V(2) \oplus V(0)$, the symmetric tensors plus the alternating tensors.

**Example 2.3.9 (a decomposition).** Let $V = \mathfrak{gl}_3$, viewed as an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module by the adjoint action of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ embedded in the upper left corner. The element $h = \mathrm{diag}(1, -1, 0)$ acts on $E_{ij}$ by $h_i - h_j$. The weights are $2, -2$ (from $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$), $1, 1, -1, -1$ (from $E_{13}$, $E_{32}$, $E_{31}$, $E_{23}$) and $0$ three times (the diagonal). So $\dim V_0 = 3$, $\dim V_1 = 2$ and $\dim V_2 = 1$. By Corollary 2.3.6, $V$ has $5$ summands, with $\dim V_2 - \dim V_4 = 1$ copy of $V(2)$, $\dim V_1 - \dim V_3 = 2$ copies of $V(1)$ and $\dim V_0 - \dim V_2 = 2$ copies of $V(0)$. So $V \cong V(2) \oplus V(1)^2 \oplus V(0)^2$. The copy of $V(2)$ is $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, the two copies of $V(1)$ are the last column and the last row, and the two copies of $V(0)$ are spanned by the identity and by $\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -2)$.

**Remark 2.3.10 (integrality).** Part 2 of Corollary 2.3.6 is the integrality statement that the rest of the chapter uses. Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be any Lie algebra with an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple $(e, f, h)$, and let $V$ be a finite dimensional $\mathfrak{g}$-module. Then $h$ acts diagonalizably on $V$ with integer eigenvalues, and the weights of $V$ for $h$ are symmetric about 0. In Section 2.4 we apply this to the adjoint module of a semisimple Lie algebra and to an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple for each root. In Section 2.7 we apply it to an arbitrary finite dimensional module. In both cases part 5 of Corollary 2.3.6 gives the symmetry of the weights under the reflections, which are the generators of the Weyl group.
