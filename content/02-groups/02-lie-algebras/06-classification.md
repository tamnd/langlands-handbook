## The Cartan matrix and the classification

A root system is determined by a finite amount of integer data: the Cartan integers of a base. This section encodes these integers in the Cartan matrix and in the Dynkin diagram, and it classifies the irreducible root systems. The proof reduces to a problem about sets of unit vectors with obtuse angles, and the answer is the list $A_\ell$, $B_\ell$, $C_\ell$, $D_\ell$, $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$, $F_4$, $G_2$. We then state the theorems that carry the classification back to semisimple Lie algebras, and we define the dual root system, which is the germ of the dual group of Chapter 5. We follow [Hum72], Sections 11 and 12, for the root systems and Sections 14 and 18 for the Lie algebras.

### The Cartan matrix

Let $\Phi$ be a root system in $E$ with a base $\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_\ell\}$, ordered in some way.

**Definition 2.6.1 (Cartan matrix).** The *Cartan matrix* of $\Phi$ with respect to the ordered base $\Delta$ is the $\ell \times \ell$ integer matrix $C = (\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j \rangle)_{i, j}$. Its entries are the *Cartan integers*.

Lean: `RootPairing.Base.cartanMatrix`, from Mathlib, with entries $\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle$ as here

The diagonal entries are $2$. The entries off the diagonal are $0$, $-1$, $-2$ or $-3$, by Lemma 2.5.4 and Lemma 2.5.9, and $\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j \rangle = 0$ if and only if $\langle \alpha_j, \alpha_i \rangle = 0$. If $D$ is the diagonal matrix with entries $(\alpha_j, \alpha_j)/2$, then $CD = ((\alpha_i, \alpha_j))$ is the Gram matrix of the basis $\Delta$. So $CD$ is symmetric and positive definite, and $\det C > 0$. A change of base replaces $\Delta$ by $w\Delta$ for some $w \in W$, by Theorem 2.5.14, and $\langle w\alpha_i, w\alpha_j \rangle = \langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j \rangle$ by Lemma 2.5.3. So the Cartan matrix depends only on $\Phi$, up to a simultaneous permutation of the rows and the columns.

**Example 2.6.2 (rank 2).** For the four root systems of Example 2.5.7 the Cartan matrices are
$$A_1 \times A_1: \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_2: \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B_2: \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad G_2: \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
For $B_2$ the base is $\alpha_1 = \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2$, $\alpha_2 = \varepsilon_2$, as in Example 2.5.11. Then $(\alpha_1, \alpha_1) = 2$, $(\alpha_2, \alpha_2) = 1$ and $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2) = -1$, so $\langle \alpha_1, \alpha_2 \rangle = -2$ and $\langle \alpha_2, \alpha_1 \rangle = -1$. For $G_2$ the base is $\alpha_1 = \alpha$ short and $\alpha_2 = \beta$ long, with the Cartan integers of Example 2.5.7. The determinants are $4$, $3$, $2$ and $1$. Mathlib's matrix `CartanMatrix.G₂` lists the long root first, so it is the transpose of the matrix above.

**Proposition 2.6.3 (the Cartan matrix determines the root system).** Let $\Phi \subset E$ and $\Phi' \subset E'$ be root systems with bases $\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_\ell\}$ and $\Delta' = \{\alpha'_1, \dots, \alpha'_\ell\}$, and assume that $\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j \rangle = \langle \alpha'_i, \alpha'_j \rangle$ for all $i, j$. Then the linear map $\varphi : E \to E'$ with $\varphi(\alpha_i) = \alpha'_i$ is an isomorphism of root systems.

Lean: `RootPairing.Base.equivOfCartanMatrixEq`, from Mathlib

*Proof.* The map $\varphi$ is a linear isomorphism, since $\Delta$ and $\Delta'$ are bases of $E$ and $E'$. For $\alpha, \beta \in \Delta$,
$$\varphi(\sigma_\alpha(\beta)) = \varphi(\beta) - \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle \varphi(\alpha) = \sigma_{\varphi(\alpha)}(\varphi(\beta)).$$
Both sides are linear in $\beta$, so $\varphi \sigma_\alpha \varphi^{-1} = \sigma_{\varphi(\alpha)}$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$. The Weyl groups $W$ and $W'$ are generated by the simple reflections, by Theorem 2.5.14, so $w \mapsto \varphi w \varphi^{-1}$ maps $W$ onto $W'$. Every root is in $W\Delta$, by Theorem 2.5.14 again. Hence $\varphi(\Phi) = \varphi(W\Delta) = W'\Delta' = \Phi'$. Finally, let $\alpha = w\alpha_i \in \Phi$. Then $\sigma_\alpha = w \sigma_{\alpha_i} w^{-1}$ by Lemma 2.5.3, and conjugation by $\varphi$ gives $\varphi \sigma_\alpha \varphi^{-1} = \sigma_{\varphi(\alpha)}$. Compare the two sides on $\varphi(\beta)$: the result is $\langle \varphi(\beta), \varphi(\alpha) \rangle = \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$. $\square$

### Coxeter graphs and Dynkin diagrams

**Definition 2.6.4 (Coxeter graph and Dynkin diagram).** The *Coxeter graph* of $\Phi$ has one vertex for each simple root $\alpha_i$, and the vertices $\alpha_i \neq \alpha_j$ are joined by $\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j \rangle \langle \alpha_j, \alpha_i \rangle \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ edges. The *Dynkin diagram* is the Coxeter graph with an arrow on each double or triple edge. The arrow points to the shorter of the two roots.

The Coxeter graph gives the angles between the simple roots. When $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_j$ are joined by two or three edges, the arrow gives the ratio of the lengths, by the table of Lemma 2.5.4. So the Dynkin diagram determines the Cartan matrix, and by Proposition 2.6.3 it determines $\Phi$ up to isomorphism.

**Proposition 2.6.5 (connected diagrams).** The root system $\Phi$ is irreducible if and only if its Coxeter graph is connected. In general, the connected components of the Coxeter graph are the Coxeter graphs of the irreducible components of $\Phi$.

Lean: `RootPairing.Base.induction_on_cartanMatrix`, from Mathlib, for the direction from irreducible to connected

*Proof.* Two simple roots are orthogonal if and only if they are not joined. So the Coxeter graph is disconnected if and only if $\Delta$ is the union of two nonempty orthogonal subsets. By Theorem 2.5.18, part 1, this happens if and only if $\Phi$ is reducible. The second claim follows from Theorem 2.5.18, part 2. $\square$

So it suffices to classify the connected Dynkin diagrams.

### The classification theorem

**Theorem 2.6.6 (classification of irreducible root systems).** Let $\Phi$ be an irreducible root system of rank $\ell$. Then its Dynkin diagram is one of the following.
1. $A_\ell$, $\ell \ge 1$: a chain of $\ell$ vertices joined by single edges.
2. $B_\ell$, $\ell \ge 2$: a chain of $\ell$ vertices, with single edges except for a double edge between the last two vertices, and the arrow pointing to the last vertex.
3. $C_\ell$, $\ell \ge 3$: the same chain as $B_\ell$, with the arrow pointing away from the last vertex.
4. $D_\ell$, $\ell \ge 4$: a chain of $\ell - 2$ vertices joined by single edges, and two further vertices, each joined by a single edge to the last vertex of the chain.
5. $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$: a chain of $\ell - 1$ vertices joined by single edges, and one further vertex joined by a single edge to the third vertex of the chain, for $\ell = 6, 7, 8$.
6. $F_4$: a chain of four vertices, with a double edge in the middle and single edges at the ends.
7. $G_2$: two vertices joined by a triple edge.

Each of these diagrams is the Dynkin diagram of an irreducible root system, and no two of them are the Dynkin diagrams of isomorphic root systems.

The restrictions on $\ell$ remove repetitions: $B_1 = C_1 = A_1$, $C_2 = B_2$, $D_3 = A_3$, and $D_2$ is the disconnected diagram $A_1 \times A_1$.

*Proof sketch.* Put $e_i = \alpha_i / |\alpha_i|$. The set $\mathfrak{A} = \{e_1, \dots, e_\ell\}$ is linearly independent, $(e_i, e_j) \le 0$ for $i \neq j$, and $4(e_i, e_j)^2 = \langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j \rangle \langle \alpha_j, \alpha_i \rangle \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$. Call any such set of unit vectors *admissible*, and give it a graph $\Gamma$ in which $e_i$ and $e_j$ are joined by $4(e_i, e_j)^2$ edges. Then $\Gamma$ is the Coxeter graph of $\Phi$. We classify the connected graphs of admissible sets. Here are the steps of [Hum72], Section 11.4.
1. A subset of an admissible set is admissible.
2. If $\mathfrak{A}$ has $n$ vectors, then fewer than $n$ pairs of vertices of $\Gamma$ are joined. Indeed, put $e = \sum e_i \neq 0$. Then $0 < (e, e) = n + 2\sum_{i < j} (e_i, e_j)$. If $e_i$ and $e_j$ are joined, then $4(e_i, e_j)^2 \ge 1$, so $2(e_i, e_j) \le -1$.
3. $\Gamma$ has no cycles, since a cycle is an admissible subset with as many joined pairs as vertices.
4. At most three edges meet at a vertex. Let $\eta_1, \dots, \eta_k$ be the vertices joined to $e$. By step 3 they are orthonormal, and $e$ is not in their span. So $\sum (e, \eta_i)^2 < 1$, and the number of edges at $e$ is $\sum 4(e, \eta_i)^2 < 4$.
5. If $\Gamma$ is connected and has a triple edge, then $\Gamma$ is $G_2$, by step 4.
6. Let $\{\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_k\} \subset \mathfrak{A}$ be a simple chain, that is, $\varepsilon_i$ and $\varepsilon_{i+1}$ are joined by one edge and there are no other edges among them. Then $\varepsilon = \sum \varepsilon_i$ is a unit vector, since $(\varepsilon, \varepsilon) = k - (k - 1) = 1$. A vertex outside the chain is joined to at most one $\varepsilon_i$, by step 3. So if we replace the chain by $\varepsilon$, we get an admissible set, whose graph is $\Gamma$ with the chain contracted to one vertex.
7. $\Gamma$ has no two double edges, no double edge together with a branch point, and no two branch points. Otherwise a simple chain joins the two special places, and step 6 contracts it to a vertex with four edges, which step 4 forbids.
8. So a connected $\Gamma$ is $G_2$, a simple chain, a chain with exactly one double edge, or a tree with one branch point and three simple chains as arms.
9. Let $\Gamma$ be the chain $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_p, \eta_q, \dots, \eta_1$, with a double edge between $\varepsilon_p$ and $\eta_q$. Put $\varepsilon = \sum i \varepsilon_i$ and $\eta = \sum j \eta_j$. Then $(\varepsilon, \varepsilon) = p(p+1)/2$, $(\eta, \eta) = q(q+1)/2$ and $(\varepsilon, \eta)^2 = p^2 q^2 (\varepsilon_p, \eta_q)^2 = p^2 q^2 / 2$. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is strict, since $\varepsilon$ and $\eta$ are independent. This gives $2pq < (p+1)(q+1)$, that is, $(p-1)(q-1) < 2$. So $q = 1$, or $p = 1$, or $p = q = 2$. These are $B_\ell$ or $C_\ell$, and $F_4$.
10. Let $\Gamma$ have a branch point $\psi$ with arms $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_{p-1}$, $\eta_1, \dots, \eta_{q-1}$ and $\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_{r-1}$, where $\varepsilon_{p-1}$, $\eta_{q-1}$ and $\zeta_{r-1}$ are the neighbours of $\psi$. Put $\varepsilon = \sum i \varepsilon_i$, and define $\eta$ and $\zeta$ in the same way. These three vectors are orthogonal, and $\psi$ is not in their span. The squared cosine of the angle between $\varepsilon$ and $\psi$ is $(\varepsilon, \psi)^2 / (\varepsilon, \varepsilon) = \frac{(p-1)^2/4}{p(p-1)/2} = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \frac{1}{p})$. The three squared cosines add up to less than $1$, so $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} + \frac{1}{r} > 1$. With $p \ge q \ge r \ge 2$ the solutions are $(p, 2, 2)$ for all $p \ge 2$, and $(3, 3, 2)$, $(4, 3, 2)$, $(5, 3, 2)$. These are $D_{p+2}$, $E_6$, $E_7$ and $E_8$.

The arrows are the only data that the Coxeter graph does not give. They matter only for a double or a triple edge, and the two directions give different diagrams only for the chain with a double edge at one end. This gives $B_\ell$ and $C_\ell$. For existence, Example 2.6.7 and Example 2.6.8 construct a root system of each type. Distinct diagrams have distinct Cartan matrices up to permutation, so the root systems are not isomorphic, by the remark after Definition 2.6.1. $\square$

**Example 2.6.7 (the classical types).** Let $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
1. $A_\ell$: in the hyperplane $\sum x_i = 0$ of $\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}$, the roots $\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j$, $i \neq j$, with base $\alpha_i = \varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i+1}$, $1 \le i \le \ell$. This is the root system of $\mathfrak{sl}_{\ell+1}$, by Example 2.4.12.
2. $B_\ell$: in $\mathbb{R}^\ell$, the roots $\pm \varepsilon_i$ and $\pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j$, $i < j$, with base $\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2, \dots, \varepsilon_{\ell-1} - \varepsilon_\ell, \varepsilon_\ell$.
3. $C_\ell$: in $\mathbb{R}^\ell$, the roots $\pm 2\varepsilon_i$ and $\pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j$, $i < j$, with base $\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2, \dots, \varepsilon_{\ell-1} - \varepsilon_\ell, 2\varepsilon_\ell$.
4. $D_\ell$: in $\mathbb{R}^\ell$, the roots $\pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j$, $i < j$, with base $\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2, \dots, \varepsilon_{\ell-1} - \varepsilon_\ell, \varepsilon_{\ell-1} + \varepsilon_\ell$.

We check $C_\ell$ against $\mathfrak{sp}_{2\ell}$, defined by the form with matrix $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_\ell \\ -I_\ell & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The diagonal matrices $h = \mathrm{diag}(t_1, \dots, t_\ell, -t_1, \dots, -t_\ell)$ in $\mathfrak{sp}_{2\ell}$ form a toral subalgebra $\mathfrak{h}$ of dimension $\ell$. Let $\varepsilon_i(h) = t_i$. The matrices $E_{ij} - E_{\ell+j, \ell+i}$ for $i \neq j$, $E_{i, \ell+j} + E_{j, \ell+i}$ and $E_{\ell+i, j} + E_{\ell+j, i}$ for $i \le j$ lie in $\mathfrak{sp}_{2\ell}$, and they are eigenvectors of $\mathrm{ad}\, h$ with eigenvalues $\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j$, $\varepsilon_i + \varepsilon_j$ and $-\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_j$. With $i = j$ the last two give $\pm 2\varepsilon_i$. Together with $\mathfrak{h}$ these $2\ell^2$ eigenvectors span a space of dimension $\ell + 2\ell^2 = \dim \mathfrak{sp}_{2\ell}$, so $\mathfrak{h}$ is maximal toral and the roots are those of $C_\ell$. Over $F$ all nondegenerate symmetric forms are equivalent, and the same computation with the form $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_\ell \\ I_\ell & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, bordered by a $1$ in the odd case, gives $B_\ell$ for $\mathfrak{so}_{2\ell+1}$ and $D_\ell$ for $\mathfrak{so}_{2\ell}$. See [Hum72], Section 1.2, for the matrices. Exercise 6 of Section 2.8 does the case $\ell = 2$.

**Example 2.6.8 (the exceptional types).**
1. $E_8$: in $\mathbb{R}^8$, the $112$ vectors $\pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j$, $i < j$, and the $128$ vectors $\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^8 (-1)^{k_i} \varepsilon_i$ with $\sum k_i$ even. All $240$ roots have squared length $2$, and the inner product of two of them is an integer, so (R4) holds. The root systems $E_7$ and $E_6$ are the roots of $E_8$ in the span of the first seven and the first six simple roots of a suitable ordering of the base. See [Hum72], Section 12.1.
2. $F_4$: in $\mathbb{R}^4$, the $8$ vectors $\pm \varepsilon_i$, the $24$ vectors $\pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j$, $i < j$, and the $16$ vectors $\frac{1}{2}(\pm \varepsilon_1 \pm \varepsilon_2 \pm \varepsilon_3 \pm \varepsilon_4)$. A base is $\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_3$, $\varepsilon_3 - \varepsilon_4$, $\varepsilon_4$, $\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_3 - \varepsilon_4)$.
3. $G_2$: the twelve vectors of Example 2.5.7.

We check the $F_4$ base. The first two vectors have squared length $2$ and the last two have squared length $1$. The inner products of consecutive vectors are $-1$, $-1$ and $-\frac{1}{2}$, and the other pairs are orthogonal. So the Coxeter graph is a chain with single, double and single edges, and the arrow points from the long roots to the short roots. This is $F_4$.

### The dual root system

**Proposition 2.6.9 (dual root system).** Let $\Phi$ be a root system in $E$. Then $\Phi^\vee = \{\alpha^\vee : \alpha \in \Phi\}$ is a root system in $E$, with $\sigma_{\alpha^\vee} = \sigma_\alpha$, $(\alpha^\vee)^\vee = \alpha$ and $\langle \alpha^\vee, \beta^\vee \rangle = \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle$. Its Weyl group is $W$. If $\Delta$ is a base of $\Phi$, then $\Delta^\vee$ is a base of $\Phi^\vee$, and the Cartan matrix of $\Phi^\vee$ with respect to $\Delta^\vee$ is the transpose of the Cartan matrix of $\Phi$ with respect to $\Delta$.

Lean: `RootPairing.flip`, from Mathlib, which exchanges the roots and the coroots

*Proof.* The vector $\alpha^\vee = 2\alpha/(\alpha, \alpha)$ is a positive multiple of $\alpha$, so $\sigma_{\alpha^\vee} = \sigma_\alpha$, and $(\alpha^\vee, \alpha^\vee) = 4/(\alpha, \alpha)$, so $(\alpha^\vee)^\vee = 2\alpha^\vee / (\alpha^\vee, \alpha^\vee) = \alpha$. An orthogonal map $w$ satisfies $w(\alpha^\vee) = (w\alpha)^\vee$. So $\sigma_\alpha(\Phi^\vee) = \Phi^\vee$, and (R1), (R2), (R3) hold. Next,
$$\langle \alpha^\vee, \beta^\vee \rangle = \frac{2(\alpha^\vee, \beta^\vee)}{(\beta^\vee, \beta^\vee)} = \frac{2 \cdot 4(\alpha, \beta)/((\alpha, \alpha)(\beta, \beta))}{4/(\beta, \beta)} = \frac{2(\alpha, \beta)}{(\alpha, \alpha)} = \langle \beta, \alpha \rangle,$$
which gives (R4) and the transpose. The Weyl groups agree, since the reflections agree. The claim about $\Delta^\vee$ is Exercise 11 of Section 2.8. $\square$

**Example 2.6.10 (duality of types).** In $B_\ell$ the short roots $\pm \varepsilon_i$ have squared length $1$, so their coroots are $\pm 2\varepsilon_i$. The long roots $\pm \varepsilon_i \pm \varepsilon_j$ have squared length $2$ and are their own coroots. So $B_\ell^\vee = C_\ell$ and $C_\ell^\vee = B_\ell$. In the types $A_\ell$, $D_\ell$, $E_6$, $E_7$ and $E_8$ all roots have the same length, and $\Phi^\vee$ is $\Phi$ up to a scalar. The diagrams of $F_4$ and $G_2$ are symmetric up to the arrow, so these types are self-dual too, although $\Phi^\vee \neq \Phi$ as sets. Chapter 3 attaches to a reductive group both its roots and its coroots, and Chapter 5 exchanges them. On the level of root systems this exchanges $B_\ell$ and $C_\ell$, and it is the reason why the dual group of $SO_{2\ell+1}$ is $Sp_{2\ell}(\mathbb{C})$.

### From root systems back to Lie algebras

In the rest of this section $F$ is algebraically closed of characteristic $0$. Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be semisimple over $F$ with a maximal toral subalgebra $\mathfrak{h}$ and root system $\Phi$, as in Section 2.4. By Theorem 2.4.7, a second choice of $\mathfrak{h}$ gives an isomorphic root system.

**Theorem 2.6.11 (simple and irreducible).** The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is simple if and only if $\Phi$ is irreducible. More precisely, if $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g}_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathfrak{g}_t$ is the decomposition into simple ideals of Theorem 2.2.18, then $\mathfrak{h}_i = \mathfrak{h} \cap \mathfrak{g}_i$ is a maximal toral subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}_i$, and $\Phi$ is the orthogonal union of the root systems $\Phi_i$ of $(\mathfrak{g}_i, \mathfrak{h}_i)$.

Lean: `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.isSimple_iff_isIrreducible`, from Mathlib, for the first claim

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $\Phi = \Phi_1 \sqcup \Phi_2$ with $\Phi_1 \perp \Phi_2$ and both nonempty. If $\alpha \in \Phi_1$ and $\beta \in \Phi_2$, then $\alpha + \beta$ is not a root. Indeed, it would lie in $\Phi_1$ or in $\Phi_2$, but $(\alpha + \beta, \beta) = (\beta, \beta) \neq 0$ and $(\alpha + \beta, \alpha) = (\alpha, \alpha) \neq 0$. So $[\mathfrak{g}_\alpha, \mathfrak{g}_\beta] = 0$, and the subalgebra generated by the $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha$, $\alpha \in \Phi_1$, is a proper nonzero ideal. Conversely, each root space lies in one simple ideal, since it is one dimensional and $\mathfrak{h}$ preserves each ideal. Roots in different ideals are orthogonal, because $[\mathfrak{g}_i, \mathfrak{g}_j] = 0$ for $i \neq j$. See [Hum72], Section 14.1. $\square$

**Theorem 2.6.12 (isomorphism theorem).** Let $\mathfrak{g}$ and $\mathfrak{g}'$ be simple Lie algebras over $F$ with maximal toral subalgebras $\mathfrak{h}$ and $\mathfrak{h}'$ and root systems $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$. Let $\varphi : \Phi \to \Phi'$ be an isomorphism of root systems, with induced linear isomorphism $\varphi : \mathfrak{h} \to \mathfrak{h}'$, and let $\Delta$ be a base of $\Phi$. For $\alpha \in \Delta$ choose nonzero $x_\alpha \in \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$ and $x'_{\varphi\alpha} \in \mathfrak{g}'_{\varphi\alpha}$. Then there is a unique isomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}'$ that extends $\varphi$ on $\mathfrak{h}$ and maps $x_\alpha$ to $x'_{\varphi\alpha}$ for every $\alpha \in \Delta$.

Lean: `LieAlgebra.equivOfRootSystemEquiv`, from Mathlib, for the existence of an isomorphism

*Proof sketch.* The elements $x_\alpha$, $y_\alpha$, $\alpha \in \Delta$, generate $\mathfrak{g}$, because every positive root is a sum of simple roots in which each partial sum is a root ([Hum72], Section 10.2), and by part 5 of Theorem 2.4.10. One constructs the isomorphism as the projection of the diagonal subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g} \oplus \mathfrak{g}'$ generated by the pairs $(x_\alpha, x'_{\varphi\alpha})$ and $(y_\alpha, y'_{\varphi\alpha})$. The main point is to show that this subalgebra meets $\mathfrak{g}'$ trivially. This uses the highest root of Theorem 2.5.18. See [Hum72], Section 14.2. $\square$

The isomorphism theorem also holds for semisimple $\mathfrak{g}$, by Theorem 2.6.11. So a semisimple Lie algebra is determined up to isomorphism by its root system. The next theorem gives the converse: every root system arises, and it gives a presentation of $\mathfrak{g}$.

**Theorem 2.6.13 (Serre).** Let $\Phi$ be a root system with base $\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_\ell\}$ and Cartan matrix $(c_{ij}) = (\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j \rangle)$. Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be the Lie algebra with generators $e_i, f_i, h_i$, $1 \le i \le \ell$, and the relations
$$[h_i, h_j] = 0, \quad [e_i, f_i] = h_i, \quad [e_i, f_j] = 0 \ (i \neq j), \quad [h_i, e_j] = c_{ji} e_j, \quad [h_i, f_j] = -c_{ji} f_j,$$
$$(\mathrm{ad}\, e_i)^{1 - c_{ji}}(e_j) = 0, \quad (\mathrm{ad}\, f_i)^{1 - c_{ji}}(f_j) = 0 \quad (i \neq j).$$
Then $\mathfrak{g}$ is finite dimensional and semisimple, the span $\mathfrak{h}$ of the $h_i$ is a maximal toral subalgebra, and the root system of $(\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h})$ is $\Phi$.

The relation $[h_i, e_j] = c_{ji} e_j$ says $\alpha_j(h_{\alpha_i}) = \langle \alpha_j, \alpha_i \rangle$, as in Theorem 2.4.10. *Proof sketch.* One first studies the algebra $\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}$ with only the relations of the first line. It decomposes as $\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^- \oplus \tilde{\mathfrak{h}} \oplus \tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^+$, with $\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}$ of dimension $\ell$, by a construction of a module on the tensor algebra. The second line generates an ideal of $\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}$, and in the quotient $\mathfrak{g}$ the operators $\mathrm{ad}\, e_i$ and $\mathrm{ad}\, f_i$ are locally nilpotent. Then the automorphisms $\exp(\mathrm{ad}\, e_i) \exp(-\mathrm{ad}\, f_i) \exp(\mathrm{ad}\, e_i)$ of $\mathfrak{g}$ act on the weights of $\mathfrak{h}$ as the simple reflections. So the set of weights is $W$-stable, every root space $\mathfrak{g}_{w\alpha_i}$ has dimension $1$, and no other weights occur. This gives $\dim \mathfrak{g} = \ell + |\Phi|$. See [Hum72], Section 18.3. $\square$

**Corollary 2.6.14 (simple Lie algebras over $F$).** The isomorphism classes of simple Lie algebras over $F$ correspond to the connected Dynkin diagrams of Theorem 2.6.6. The Lie algebras are $\mathfrak{sl}_{\ell+1}$ ($\ell \ge 1$), $\mathfrak{so}_{2\ell+1}$ ($\ell \ge 2$), $\mathfrak{sp}_{2\ell}$ ($\ell \ge 3$), $\mathfrak{so}_{2\ell}$ ($\ell \ge 4$), and five exceptional algebras of dimensions $78$, $133$, $248$, $52$ and $14$.

**Remark 2.6.15 (automorphisms).** A symmetry of the Dynkin diagram gives an automorphism of $\Phi$ that preserves $\Delta$, by Proposition 2.6.3, and then an automorphism of $\mathfrak{g}$, by Theorem 2.6.12. The group of these symmetries is trivial or of order $2$, except for $D_4$, where it is $S_3$. The automorphism group of $\Phi$ is the semidirect product of $W$ and the symmetries of the diagram ([Hum72], Section 12.2). For $A_\ell$, $\ell \ge 2$, the nontrivial symmetry corresponds to $x \mapsto -x^t$ on $\mathfrak{sl}_{\ell+1}$. These automorphisms reappear in the $L$-group, where the Galois group acts on the dual group through the symmetries of its Dynkin diagram.
