## The theorem of the highest weight

This section classifies the finite dimensional irreducible modules of a semisimple Lie algebra. The answer is the set of dominant weights: each such module has a unique highest weight, and each dominant weight occurs. The proof uses the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-theory of Section 2.3 once for each simple root, and the Weyl group of Section 2.5 to control the set of weights. At the end we state the Weyl character formula and the Weyl dimension formula. We follow [Hum72], Sections 13, 20, 21 and 24.

### Weights

Let $\Phi$ be a root system in $E$ with base $\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_\ell\}$, Weyl group $W$ and positive roots $\Phi^+$.

**Definition 2.7.1 (weights).** The *weight lattice* of $\Phi$ is $\Lambda = \{\lambda \in E : \langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ for all } \alpha \in \Phi\}$, and its elements are the *weights*. The *root lattice* $\Lambda_r$ is the subgroup of $E$ generated by $\Phi$. The *fundamental weights* $\varpi_1, \dots, \varpi_\ell$ are the basis of $E$ dual to the coroots $\alpha_1^\vee, \dots, \alpha_\ell^\vee$, so that $\langle \varpi_i, \alpha_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$. A weight $\lambda$ is *dominant* if $\langle \lambda, \alpha_i \rangle \ge 0$ for all $i$. We write $\Lambda^+$ for the set of dominant weights.

**Proposition 2.7.2 (the weight lattice).**
1. $\Lambda$ is a lattice with basis $\varpi_1, \dots, \varpi_\ell$, and $\Lambda^+ = \sum_i \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \varpi_i$.
2. $\Lambda_r \subset \Lambda$, and $\alpha_j = \sum_i \langle \alpha_j, \alpha_i \rangle \varpi_i$. So the Cartan matrix $C$ expresses the simple roots in terms of the fundamental weights, and $|\Lambda / \Lambda_r| = \det C$.
3. $W$ preserves $\Lambda$ and $\Lambda_r$.
4. Each $W$-orbit in $\Lambda$ contains exactly one dominant weight. If $\lambda$ is dominant, then $w\lambda \preceq \lambda$ for all $w \in W$.
5. The vector $\delta = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \alpha$ is equal to $\varpi_1 + \cdots + \varpi_\ell$.
6. For $\lambda \in \Lambda^+$, the set of dominant weights $\mu$ with $\mu \preceq \lambda$ is finite.

Lean: not stated yet. The nondegeneracy of $C$ is `RootPairing.Base.cartanMatrix_nondegenerate`, from Mathlib

*Proof.* For part 1, let $\lambda \in E$ and write $\lambda = \sum_i c_i \varpi_i$, with $c_i = \langle \lambda, \alpha_i \rangle$. If $\lambda \in \Lambda$, then all $c_i \in \mathbb{Z}$. Conversely, if all $c_i \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $\langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}$ for each $\alpha \in \Phi$, because $\alpha^\vee$ is an integer combination of the $\alpha_i^\vee$. Indeed $\Delta^\vee$ is a base of $\Phi^\vee$ by Proposition 2.6.9. For part 2, (R4) gives $\Phi \subset \Lambda$, and the formula for $\alpha_j$ follows from part 1. The index of a sublattice is the absolute value of the determinant of the matrix of a basis, and $\det C > 0$. For part 3, $\langle w\lambda, \alpha \rangle = \langle \lambda, w^{-1} \alpha \rangle$ for $w \in W$. For part 4, the orbit $W\lambda$ is finite, so it contains an element $\mu$ that is maximal for $\preceq$. If $\langle \mu, \alpha_i \rangle < 0$, then $\sigma_i \mu = \mu - \langle \mu, \alpha_i \rangle \alpha_i \succ \mu$, a contradiction. So $\mu$ is dominant. The uniqueness and the inequality $w\lambda \preceq \lambda$ are [Hum72], Section 13.2, Lemma A. They follow from the fact that the closure of the fundamental Weyl chamber is a fundamental domain for $W$ ([Hum72], Section 10.3). Part 5 holds because $\sigma_i \delta = \delta - \alpha_i$ by Lemma 2.5.12, so $\langle \delta, \alpha_i \rangle = 1$. For part 6, if $\mu$ is dominant and $\mu \preceq \lambda$, then $\lambda - \mu$ is a sum of positive roots and $\lambda + \mu$ is dominant, so $(\lambda + \mu, \lambda - \mu) \ge 0$. So $(\mu, \mu) \le (\lambda, \lambda)$, and a lattice has only finitely many points in a ball. $\square$

**Example 2.7.3 (rank 1 and $A_2$).** For $A_1 = \{\pm \alpha\}$ we have $\varpi = \alpha / 2$, $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z} \varpi$ and $\Lambda / \Lambda_r \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$. For $A_2$, part 2 gives $\alpha_1 = 2\varpi_1 - \varpi_2$ and $\alpha_2 = -\varpi_1 + 2\varpi_2$, so
$$\varpi_1 = \tfrac{1}{3}(2\alpha_1 + \alpha_2), \qquad \varpi_2 = \tfrac{1}{3}(\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2),$$
and $\Lambda / \Lambda_r \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$, generated by $\varpi_1$. In the coordinates of Example 2.6.7, $\varpi_1$ is the projection of $\varepsilon_1$ to the plane $\sum x_i = 0$, and $\varpi_2$ is the projection of $\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2$. For $A_{n-1}$ in general, $\varpi_k$ is the projection of $\varepsilon_1 + \cdots + \varepsilon_k$, and $\Lambda / \Lambda_r \cong \mathbb{Z}/n$. For $G_2$, $\det C = 1$, so $\Lambda = \Lambda_r$. Exercise 8 of Section 2.8 computes $\det C$ for all types.

### Weights of modules

From now on $F$ is algebraically closed of characteristic $0$, and $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple over $F$ with a maximal toral subalgebra $\mathfrak{h}$, root system $\Phi$ and base $\Delta$. For each $\alpha \in \Phi$ we fix $x_\alpha$, $y_\alpha$ and $h_\alpha$ as in Proposition 2.4.9, and we write $x_i, y_i, h_i$ for $\alpha = \alpha_i$. The root system lives in the real span $E$ of $\Phi$ in $\mathfrak{h}^*$, by Theorem 2.4.11, and $\lambda(h_\alpha) = \langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle$ for $\lambda$ in the rational span of $\Phi$. Put
$$\mathfrak{n}^+ = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha, \qquad \mathfrak{n}^- = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}, \qquad \mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{n}^+.$$
Then $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{n}^- \oplus \mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{n}^+$, and $\mathfrak{b}$ is a solvable subalgebra, the *Borel subalgebra* attached to $\Delta$. For $\mathfrak{sl}_n$ with the base of Example 2.5.11, $\mathfrak{b}$ is the algebra $\mathfrak{b}_n$ of upper triangular matrices of trace $0$, and $\mathfrak{n}^+ = \mathfrak{n}_n$.

For a $\mathfrak{g}$-module $V$, possibly infinite dimensional, and $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$, the *weight space* is $V_\lambda = \{v \in V : hv = \lambda(h) v \text{ for all } h \in \mathfrak{h}\}$. If $V_\lambda \neq 0$, then $\lambda$ is a *weight* of $V$. As for $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ in Lemma 2.3.2, $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha V_\lambda \subset V_{\lambda + \alpha}$, and the sum of the weight spaces is direct.

**Proposition 2.7.4 (finite dimensional modules).** Let $V$ be a finite dimensional $\mathfrak{g}$-module. Then $V = \bigoplus_\lambda V_\lambda$, every weight of $V$ lies in $\Lambda$, and $\dim V_\lambda = \dim V_{w\lambda}$ for all $\lambda$ and all $w \in W$.

*Proof.* The elements of $\mathfrak{h}$ are ad-semisimple, so they act on $V$ by semisimple endomorphisms, by Theorem 2.2.21. These endomorphisms commute, so they are simultaneously diagonalizable, and $V$ is the sum of its weight spaces. Let $\lambda$ be a weight and $\alpha \in \Phi$. The space $U = \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} V_{\lambda + k\alpha}$ is a module for the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple $S_\alpha$, and $h_\alpha$ acts on $V_{\lambda + k\alpha}$ by the scalar $\lambda(h_\alpha) + 2k$. By Corollary 2.3.6, part 2, the eigenvalues of $h_\alpha$ on $U$ are integers, so $\lambda(h_\alpha) \in \mathbb{Z}$. This holds for all $\alpha$, so $\lambda = \sum_i \lambda(h_i) \varpi_i \in \Lambda$. Corollary 2.3.6 also gives that the eigenvalues $\lambda(h_\alpha)$ and $-\lambda(h_\alpha)$ have the same multiplicity. The $-\lambda(h_\alpha)$-eigenspace of $h_\alpha$ on $U$ is $V_{\lambda - \lambda(h_\alpha)\alpha} = V_{\sigma_\alpha \lambda}$. So $\dim V_\lambda = \dim V_{\sigma_\alpha \lambda}$, and $W$ is generated by the $\sigma_\alpha$. $\square$

### Highest weight modules

**Definition 2.7.5 (maximal vectors and highest weight modules).** Let $V$ be a $\mathfrak{g}$-module. A *maximal vector* of weight $\lambda$ is a nonzero $v \in V_\lambda$ with $\mathfrak{n}^+ v = 0$. The module $V$ is a *highest weight module* of highest weight $\lambda$ if $V = U(\mathfrak{g}) v$ for a maximal vector $v$ of weight $\lambda$.

Lean: not stated yet

Here $U(\mathfrak{g})$ is the universal enveloping algebra, the associative algebra generated by $\mathfrak{g}$ with the relations $xy - yx = [x, y]$, so that $\mathfrak{g}$-modules are the same as $U(\mathfrak{g})$-modules. Mathlib defines it as `UniversalEnvelopingAlgebra`. The *Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem* says that if $z_1, \dots, z_n$ is an ordered basis of $\mathfrak{g}$, then the monomials $z_1^{a_1} \cdots z_n^{a_n}$ form a basis of $U(\mathfrak{g})$ ([Hum72], Section 17.3). Mathlib does not have this theorem yet. If the basis is a basis of $\mathfrak{n}^-$, then of $\mathfrak{h}$, then of $\mathfrak{n}^+$, the theorem gives $U(\mathfrak{g}) = U(\mathfrak{n}^-) U(\mathfrak{h}) U(\mathfrak{n}^+)$.

A vector $v$ is a maximal vector as soon as $x_i v = 0$ for $i = 1, \dots, \ell$ and $v$ is a weight vector, because the $x_i$ generate $\mathfrak{n}^+$. This follows from part 5 of Theorem 2.4.10 and from the fact that every positive root is a sum of simple roots with all partial sums roots ([Hum72], Section 10.2).

**Theorem 2.7.6 (highest weight modules).** Let $V = U(\mathfrak{g}) v^+$ be a highest weight module of highest weight $\lambda$. Let $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_m$ be the positive roots.
1. $V$ is spanned by the vectors $y_{\beta_1}^{a_1} \cdots y_{\beta_m}^{a_m} v^+$ with $a_j \ge 0$. Such a vector has weight $\lambda - \sum_j a_j \beta_j$.
2. $V = \bigoplus_\mu V_\mu$, all weights $\mu$ satisfy $\mu \preceq \lambda$, every $V_\mu$ is finite dimensional, and $V_\lambda = F v^+$.
3. Every submodule of $V$ is the sum of its intersections with the weight spaces. $V$ has a unique maximal proper submodule, and a unique irreducible quotient.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* By the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem, $V = U(\mathfrak{n}^-) U(\mathfrak{h}) U(\mathfrak{n}^+) v^+ = U(\mathfrak{n}^-) v^+$, since $\mathfrak{n}^+$ kills $v^+$ and $\mathfrak{h}$ acts on it by scalars. The monomials in the $y_{\beta_j}$ span $U(\mathfrak{n}^-)$, and $y_{\beta_j}$ lowers the weight by $\beta_j$. This is part 1. For part 2, only finitely many monomials have a given weight $\mu$, and only the empty monomial has weight $\lambda$. For part 3, let $V' \subset V$ be a submodule and let $v = v_1 + \cdots + v_k \in V'$ with $v_j \in V_{\mu_j}$ for distinct $\mu_j$. Choose $h \in \mathfrak{h}$ with distinct values $\mu_j(h)$. Then $h$ acts on the span of $v, hv, \dots, h^{k-1} v$ with distinct eigenvalues, and a Vandermonde argument shows that all $v_j \in V'$. If $V'$ is proper, then $V'_\lambda = 0$, since $V_\lambda = F v^+$ generates $V$. So the sum of all proper submodules has no component of weight $\lambda$, and it is the unique maximal proper submodule. $\square$

**Theorem 2.7.7 (Verma modules).** For each $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$, let $F_\lambda$ be the one dimensional $\mathfrak{b}$-module on which $\mathfrak{h}$ acts by $\lambda$ and $\mathfrak{n}^+$ acts by $0$. The *Verma module* $M(\lambda) = U(\mathfrak{g}) \otimes_{U(\mathfrak{b})} F_\lambda$ is a highest weight module of highest weight $\lambda$, and it is a free $U(\mathfrak{n}^-)$-module of rank $1$. Every highest weight module of highest weight $\lambda$ is a quotient of $M(\lambda)$. The irreducible quotient $L(\lambda)$ of $M(\lambda)$ is the unique irreducible highest weight module of highest weight $\lambda$, up to isomorphism.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem shows that $U(\mathfrak{g})$ is a free right $U(\mathfrak{b})$-module with basis the monomials in a basis of $\mathfrak{n}^-$. So $M(\lambda) = U(\mathfrak{n}^-) \otimes_F F_\lambda$ as a $U(\mathfrak{n}^-)$-module, and in particular $1 \otimes 1 \neq 0$. If $V = U(\mathfrak{g}) v^+$ is a highest weight module of highest weight $\lambda$, then $F v^+ \cong F_\lambda$ as $\mathfrak{b}$-modules, and the adjunction gives a surjection $M(\lambda) \to V$. If $V$ is irreducible, then its kernel is a maximal proper submodule, so it is the unique one by Theorem 2.7.6, part 3, and $V \cong L(\lambda)$. See [Hum72], Sections 20.2 and 20.3. $\square$

For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and $\lambda = m \in F$, the Verma module $M(m)$ has the basis $v_i = f^i v_0 / i!$, $i \ge 0$, with the action of Lemma 2.3.3. If $m \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$, then $e v_{m+1} = 0$ by Lemma 2.3.3, part 3, so $v_{m+1}$ is a maximal vector. It generates the maximal proper submodule, which is isomorphic to $M(-m-2)$, and $L(m) = M(m) / M(-m-2)$ is the module $V(m)$ of Theorem 2.3.4. If $m \notin \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$, then $M(m)$ is irreducible and infinite dimensional.

### Finite dimensional irreducible modules

**Theorem 2.7.8 (theorem of the highest weight).**
1. Every finite dimensional irreducible $\mathfrak{g}$-module is isomorphic to $L(\lambda)$ for a unique $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$, and this $\lambda$ is a dominant weight.
2. If $\lambda \in \Lambda^+$, then $L(\lambda)$ is finite dimensional. Its set of weights $\Pi(\lambda)$ is stable under $W$, and $\dim L(\lambda)_\mu = \dim L(\lambda)_{w\mu}$ for all $w \in W$.

So $\lambda \mapsto L(\lambda)$ is a bijection from $\Lambda^+$ onto the set of isomorphism classes of finite dimensional irreducible $\mathfrak{g}$-modules.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Part 1. Let $V$ be finite dimensional and irreducible. The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{b}$ is solvable, so by Lie's theorem (Theorem 2.2.9) it has a common eigenvector $v^+ \in V$. Its eigenvalue on $[\mathfrak{b}, \mathfrak{b}] \supset \mathfrak{n}^+$ is $0$, since a commutator acts on a common eigenvector by $\chi(x)\chi(y) - \chi(y)\chi(x) = 0$. So $v^+$ is a maximal vector of some weight $\lambda$, and $V = U(\mathfrak{g}) v^+$ since $V$ is irreducible. By Theorem 2.7.7, $V \cong L(\lambda)$. The weight $\lambda$ is the unique maximal weight of $V$ for $\preceq$, by Theorem 2.7.6, so $V$ determines $\lambda$. By Proposition 2.7.4, $\lambda \in \Lambda$. For each $i$, the vector $v^+$ is a maximal vector for the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple $(x_i, y_i, h_i)$, with eigenvalue $\lambda(h_i)$ for $h_i$. The vectors $y_i^k v^+$ span a finite dimensional space, so $y_i^{m+1} v^+ = 0 \neq y_i^m v^+$ for some $m \ge 0$. Lemma 2.3.3, part 3, gives $0 = (\lambda(h_i) - m) y_i^m v^+ / m!$, so $\lambda(h_i) = m \ge 0$.

Part 2. Let $V = L(\lambda)$ with maximal vector $v^+$, and put $m_i = \lambda(h_i) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$. We proceed in four steps.

Step 1: $y_i^{m_i + 1} v^+ = 0$. Put $w = y_i^{m_i + 1} v^+$. For $j \neq i$ we have $[x_j, y_i] = 0$, since $\alpha_j - \alpha_i$ is not a root, so $x_j w = y_i^{m_i+1} x_j v^+ = 0$. Lemma 2.3.3, part 3, gives $x_i w = (m_i + 1)(m_i - m_i) y_i^{m_i} v^+ = 0$. So if $w \neq 0$, then $w$ is a maximal vector of weight $\lambda - (m_i + 1)\alpha_i$. It generates a submodule whose weights are $\preceq \lambda - (m_i+1)\alpha_i$, by Theorem 2.7.6. This submodule is proper, which contradicts the irreducibility of $V$.

Step 2: $x_i$ and $y_i$ act locally nilpotently on $V$. Let $V'$ be the set of $v \in V$ with $y_i^N v = 0$ for some $N$. In $U(\mathfrak{g})$,
$$y_i^N z = \sum_{k=0}^{N} \binom{N}{k} \big((\mathrm{ad}\, y_i)^k z\big)\, y_i^{N-k} \qquad (z \in \mathfrak{g}),$$
and $\mathrm{ad}\, y_i$ is nilpotent on $\mathfrak{g}$, since it maps $\mathfrak{g}_\mu$ to $\mathfrak{g}_{\mu - \alpha_i}$. So $V'$ is a submodule. It contains $v^+$ by step 1, so $V' = V$. The same argument works for $x_i$, which kills $v^+$.

Step 3: $V$ is a sum of finite dimensional $S_i$-submodules, where $S_i$ is spanned by $x_i, y_i, h_i$. Let $v \in V_\mu$. By the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem for $S_i$, the $S_i$-submodule generated by $v$ is spanned by the vectors $y_i^a x_i^b v$. Only finitely many of them are nonzero, by step 2.

Step 4: $W$-invariance and finiteness. By step 3 and Corollary 2.3.6, $V$ is a direct sum of $S_i$-modules $V(m)$. Each $V(m)$ has the same dimension in the $h_i$-eigenvalues $n$ and $-n$. As in the proof of Proposition 2.7.4, this gives $\dim V_\mu = \dim V_{\sigma_i \mu}$ for all $\mu$. These dimensions are finite by Theorem 2.7.6, part 2. The $\sigma_i$ generate $W$ by Theorem 2.5.14, so $\Pi(\lambda)$ is $W$-stable with $W$-invariant multiplicities. Every $\mu \in \Pi(\lambda)$ is in $\Lambda$, because $\mu = \lambda - \sum k_j \alpha_j$. By Proposition 2.7.2, part 4, it is $W$-conjugate to a dominant weight $\mu' \in \Pi(\lambda)$, and $\mu' \preceq \lambda$. By Proposition 2.7.2, part 6, there are only finitely many such $\mu'$. So $\Pi(\lambda)$ is finite, and $V$ is finite dimensional.

The bijection follows from parts 1 and 2. See [Hum72], Sections 20.3 and 21.2. $\square$

By Weyl's theorem (Theorem 2.2.22), every finite dimensional $\mathfrak{g}$-module is a direct sum of modules $L(\lambda)$ with $\lambda \in \Lambda^+$. The *fundamental representations* are the modules $L(\varpi_i)$. Every $L(\lambda)$ with $\lambda = \sum m_i \varpi_i$ is a submodule of $\bigotimes_i L(\varpi_i)^{\otimes m_i}$, generated by the tensor product of the maximal vectors, which is a maximal vector of weight $\lambda$.

**Example 2.7.9 ($\mathfrak{sl}_n$).** Let $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_n$ with the diagonal $\mathfrak{h}$ and the base $\alpha_i = \varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i+1}$. The standard module $F^n$ has the weights $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n$ (restricted to $\mathfrak{h}$), each with multiplicity $1$. The basis vector $e_1$ is killed by the upper triangular matrices $\mathfrak{n}^+$, so it is a maximal vector, and $F^n \cong L(\varepsilon_1) = L(\varpi_1)$. More generally, $\Lambda^k F^n \cong L(\varpi_k)$, with maximal vector $e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_k$. This is Exercise 10 of Section 2.8. The adjoint module has the highest weight $\theta = \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_n = \varpi_1 + \varpi_{n-1}$, the highest root, with maximal vector $E_{1n}$. For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$, the modules $L(\varpi_1) = F^3$, $L(\varpi_2) = \Lambda^2 F^3 \cong (F^3)^*$ and $L(\varpi_1 + \varpi_2) = \mathfrak{sl}_3$ have dimensions $3$, $3$ and $8$.

**Example 2.7.10 (a weight diagram).** We find the weights of $V = L(2\varpi_1)$ for $\mathfrak{sl}_3$. Write $\mu = 2\varpi_1 - a\alpha_1 - b\alpha_2 = (2 - 2a + b)\varpi_1 + (a - 2b)\varpi_2$ with $a, b \ge 0$. This is dominant only if $a \ge 2b$ and $2 + b \ge 2a \ge 4b$. So $b = 0$ and $a \in \{0, 1\}$, and the dominant weights $\mu \preceq 2\varpi_1$ are $2\varpi_1$ and $2\varpi_1 - \alpha_1 = \varpi_2$. The orbit of $2\varpi_1 = 2\varepsilon_1$ is $\{2\varepsilon_1, 2\varepsilon_2, 2\varepsilon_3\}$, and the orbit of $\varpi_2 = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2$ is $\{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2, \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_3, \varepsilon_2 + \varepsilon_3\}$. The weight $2\varepsilon_1 - \alpha_1 = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2$ has multiplicity $1$, since $V_{\lambda - \alpha_1} = F y_1 v^+$ by Theorem 2.7.6. So $\dim V = 6$. Indeed $V \cong \mathrm{Sym}^2 F^3$, with the weights $\varepsilon_i + \varepsilon_j$, $i \le j$.

### Characters and the Weyl formulas

The *character* of a finite dimensional $\mathfrak{g}$-module $V$ is $\mathrm{ch}\, V = \sum_\mu \dim V_\mu\, e^\mu$, an element of the group ring $\mathbb{Z}[\Lambda]$ with basis $e^\mu$, $\mu \in \Lambda$. It is $W$-invariant by Proposition 2.7.4. By Weyl's theorem and Theorem 2.7.8, $V$ is determined up to isomorphism by $\mathrm{ch}\, V$, since the highest weights of the summands can be read off from the character one at a time. Write $\varepsilon(w) = \det(w) = \pm 1$ for $w \in W$.

**Theorem 2.7.11 (Weyl character formula).** For $\lambda \in \Lambda^+$,
$$\Big( \sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w)\, e^{w\delta} \Big) \cdot \mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda) = \sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w)\, e^{w(\lambda + \delta)}.$$
The first factor is equal to $\prod_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} (e^{\alpha/2} - e^{-\alpha/2})$, the *Weyl denominator*.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof sketch.* The proof in [Hum72] uses the centre of $U(\mathfrak{g})$. By the theorem of Harish-Chandra ([Hum72], Section 23), the centre acts on $M(\mu)$ and on $M(\lambda)$ by the same character only if $\mu + \delta \in W(\lambda + \delta)$. From this one writes $\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda)$ as an integer combination of the characters of the Verma modules $M(w(\lambda + \delta) - \delta)$, $w \in W$. The character of $M(\mu)$ is $e^{\mu} / \prod_{\alpha > 0} (1 - e^{-\alpha})$, by the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem. The $W$-invariance of $\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda)$ then fixes the coefficients as $\varepsilon(w)$. See [Hum72], Sections 23 and 24. $\square$

Chapter 4 explains the analytic proof of the same formula for compact groups, due to Weyl, by integration over a maximal torus.

**Corollary 2.7.12 (Weyl dimension formula).** For $\lambda \in \Lambda^+$,
$$\dim L(\lambda) = \prod_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \frac{\langle \lambda + \delta, \alpha \rangle}{\langle \delta, \alpha \rangle} = \prod_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \frac{(\lambda + \delta, \alpha)}{(\delta, \alpha)}.$$

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof sketch.* Apply both sides of Theorem 2.7.11 to the homomorphism $e^\mu \mapsto e^{t(\mu, \delta)}$ from $\mathbb{Z}[\Lambda]$ to the functions of a real variable $t$, and let $t \to 0$. The denominator identity, applied to $\lambda + \delta$ in place of $\delta$, turns each alternating sum into a product $\prod_{\alpha > 0} (e^{t(\alpha, \lambda + \delta)/2} - e^{-t(\alpha, \lambda+\delta)/2})$. Compare the leading terms in $t$. See [Hum72], Section 24.3. $\square$

**Example 2.7.13 ($\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and $\mathfrak{sl}_2$).** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and $\lambda = m\varpi$, the only positive root is $\alpha$, with $\langle \lambda + \delta, \alpha \rangle = m + 1$ and $\langle \delta, \alpha \rangle = 1$. So $\dim L(m\varpi) = m + 1$, as in Theorem 2.3.4. For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and $\lambda = a\varpi_1 + b\varpi_2$, the positive roots are $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, all of the same length, so $\langle \cdot, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 \rangle = \langle \cdot, \alpha_1 \rangle + \langle \cdot, \alpha_2 \rangle$. This gives
$$\dim L(a\varpi_1 + b\varpi_2) = \frac{(a+1)(b+1)(a+b+2)}{2}.$$
For $(a, b) = (1, 0), (1, 1), (2, 0), (3, 0)$ we get $3, 8, 6, 10$, the dimensions of $F^3$, $\mathfrak{sl}_3$, $\mathrm{Sym}^2 F^3$ and $\mathrm{Sym}^3 F^3$. Exercise 9 of Section 2.8 treats $\mathfrak{sp}_4$ and $\mathfrak{g}_2$.

**Remark 2.7.14 (groups and lattices).** Let $G$ be a connected semisimple algebraic group over $F$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, and let $T \subset G$ be a maximal torus with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{h}$. The differentials of the characters of $T$ form a lattice $X$ with $\Lambda_r \subset X \subset \Lambda$, and the irreducible representations of $G$ correspond to the dominant weights in $X$. For $SL_n$ the lattice is $X = \Lambda$, and every $L(\lambda)$ integrates to $SL_n$. For $PGL_n$ it is $X = \Lambda_r$, so the standard module $L(\varpi_1)$ does not come from $PGL_n$. The quotient $\Lambda / X$ is dual to the kernel of the isogeny from the simply connected cover, and $\Lambda / \Lambda_r \cong \mathbb{Z}/n$ is dual to the centre $\mu_n$ of $SL_n$. Chapter 3 encodes the lattice $X$, the roots and the coroots in the root datum, and Chapter 4 proves the group version of Theorem 2.7.8. For the dual group of Chapter 5, the lattices $X$ and the cocharacter lattice exchange their roles, so the dual group of $SL_n$ is $PGL_n(\mathbb{C})$.
