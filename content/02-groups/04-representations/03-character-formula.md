## The Weyl character formula

In characteristic $0$ the character of $L(\lambda)$ is a quotient of two alternating sums over the Weyl group. This section states the formula, proves the denominator formula and the dimension formula, and derives the multiplicity formula of Kostant and a rule for tensor products. We give the main steps of the algebraic proof of the character formula. Section 4.6 gives a complete analytic proof for compact groups, which also proves the formula over $\mathbb{C}$. We keep the notation of Section 4.1, and we fix a positive definite inner product $(\ ,\ )$ on $X \otimes \mathbb{R}$ that is invariant under $W$. Since $s_\alpha$ is then the orthogonal reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to $\alpha$, we have
$$\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = \frac{2 (x, \alpha)}{(\alpha, \alpha)} \quad \text{for } x \in X \otimes \mathbb{R} \text{ and } \alpha \in R.$$

### Alternating sums

The half sum $\rho$ of the positive roots need not lie in $X$. Let $X' = X + \mathbb{Z}\rho \subset X \otimes \mathbb{Q}$. It is a finitely generated torsion-free group, so it is a lattice, and the group ring $\mathbb{Z}[X']$ is a ring of Laurent polynomials. In particular it is an integral domain. The group $W$ acts on $X'$, because $w \rho - \rho \in \mathbb{Z} R$ by Section 4.1. For $w \in W$ write $\varepsilon(w) = \det(w) = \pm 1$, so that $\varepsilon(s_\alpha) = -1$.

**Definition 4.3.1 (alternating sum).** For $\mu \in X'$, the *alternating sum* of $\mu$ is
$$A_\mu = \sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w) \, e^{w \mu} \in \mathbb{Z}[X'].$$
An element $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X']$ is *anti-invariant* if $w f = \varepsilon(w) f$ for all $w \in W$.

The element $A_\mu$ is anti-invariant, and $A_{w\mu} = \varepsilon(w) A_\mu$. Recall from Section 4.1 that $\mu$ is strictly dominant if $\langle \mu, \alpha^\vee \rangle > 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$. For $\mu \in X'$ these numbers are integers, because $\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 1$ for $\alpha \in \Delta$.

**Lemma 4.3.2 (anti-invariant elements).** The elements $A_\mu$, for $\mu \in X'$ strictly dominant, form a basis of the $\mathbb{Z}$-module of anti-invariant elements of $\mathbb{Z}[X']$.

*Proof.* Let $f = \sum_\mu c_\mu e^\mu$ be anti-invariant. Then $c_{w\mu} = \varepsilon(w) c_\mu$. If $\langle \mu, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 0$ for some root $\alpha$, then $s_\alpha \mu = \mu$, so $c_\mu = -c_\mu$ and $c_\mu = 0$. Now let $\mu$ be a weight with $\langle \mu, \alpha^\vee \rangle \neq 0$ for all roots $\alpha$. By Lemma 4.1.9, which holds for $X'$, some $w \mu$ is dominant, and then it is strictly dominant. It is the only strictly dominant element of $W\mu$, and its stabilizer in $W$ is trivial, because the stabilizer of a dominant weight is generated by the simple reflections that fix it ([Hum72], Section 10). So $f = \sum c_\mu A_\mu$, where $\mu$ runs over the strictly dominant elements of $X'$. The sets $W \mu$ for different strictly dominant $\mu$ are disjoint, so the $A_\mu$ are linearly independent. $\square$

The proof shows that the coefficient of $e^\mu$ in $A_\mu$ is $1$ and that all other terms of $A_\mu$ are $\pm e^{w\mu}$ with $w \mu < \mu$, by Lemma 4.1.9.

**Theorem 4.3.3 (the denominator formula).**
$$A_\rho = \prod_{\alpha \in R^+} \left( e^{\alpha/2} - e^{-\alpha/2} \right) = e^\rho \prod_{\alpha \in R^+} \left( 1 - e^{-\alpha} \right).$$

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The two products are equal, because $\rho = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha > 0} \alpha$, and the second one lies in $\mathbb{Z}[X']$. Call it $D$. Let $\alpha \in \Delta$. The reflection $s_\alpha$ permutes $R^+ \setminus \{\alpha\}$ and maps $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$ ([Hum72], Section 10). So it permutes the factors of the first product except one, and it changes the sign of that factor. Thus $s_\alpha D = -D$. The simple reflections generate $W$, so $D$ is anti-invariant, and $D = \sum_\mu c_\mu A_\mu$ by Lemma 4.3.2, with $\mu$ strictly dominant. When we expand the second product, every term has the form $\pm e^{\rho - \sigma}$, where $\sigma$ is a sum of distinct positive roots. So $c_\mu \neq 0$ implies $\mu \le \rho$. Let $\mu$ be strictly dominant with $\mu \le \rho$, and put $\nu = \mu - \rho$. For $\alpha \in \Delta$ we have $\langle \nu, \alpha^\vee \rangle = \langle \mu, \alpha^\vee \rangle - 1 \ge 0$, so $(\nu, \alpha) \ge 0$. Since $-\nu$ is a sum of simple roots with coefficients $n_\alpha \ge 0$, we get $(\nu, \nu) = -\sum_\alpha n_\alpha (\nu, \alpha) \le 0$, so $\nu = 0$. Hence $D = c_\rho A_\rho$. The coefficient of $e^\rho$ is $1$ in both $D$ and $A_\rho$, so $D = A_\rho$. $\square$

### The character formula

**Theorem 4.3.4 (Weyl character formula).** Let $k$ have characteristic $0$ and let $\lambda \in X^+$. Then
$$A_\rho \cdot \mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda) = A_{\lambda + \rho}.$$
Since $\mathbb{Z}[X']$ is a domain, this determines $\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda)$ as the quotient $A_{\lambda+\rho} / A_\rho$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Outline of the proof.* We describe the proof by Lie algebras, from [Hum72], Section 24, and [Kna02], Chapter 5. The characters of $G$ and of its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ agree, and the center of $\mathfrak{g}$ acts on $L(\lambda)$ by scalars, so we may assume that $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple and that $k$ is algebraically closed. We use formal characters with infinite support, in the ring $\mathcal{E}$ of formal sums $\sum c_\mu e^\mu$ whose support lies in a finite union of sets $\nu - \mathbb{N} R^+$.
1. For each $\mu \in \mathfrak{t}^*$ there is a Verma module $M(\mu)$, a module with highest weight $\mu$ that is free over $U(\mathfrak{n}^-)$ of rank one. By the theorem of Poincaré, Birkhoff and Witt its character is $e^\mu \prod_{\alpha > 0} (1 - e^{-\alpha})^{-1} = e^{\mu + \rho} / A_\rho$, by Theorem 4.3.3.
2. The Casimir element of $U(\mathfrak{g})$ acts on every quotient of $M(\mu)$ by the scalar $(\mu + \rho, \mu + \rho) - (\rho, \rho)$.
3. The module $L(\lambda)$ has a finite filtration, in a suitable category of modules, whose pieces are quotients of Verma modules. Comparing the Casimir scalars, one finds integers $b_\mu$ with $\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda) = \sum_\mu b_\mu \, \mathrm{ch}\, M(\mu)$, where $b_\lambda = 1$ and $\mu$ runs over the weights $\mu \le \lambda$ with $(\mu + \rho, \mu + \rho) = (\lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho)$.
4. Multiply by $A_\rho$. Step 1 gives $A_\rho \, \mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda) = \sum_\mu b_\mu e^{\mu + \rho}$. The left side is anti-invariant, so it is $\sum_\nu c_\nu A_\nu$ with $\nu$ strictly dominant, by Lemma 4.3.2. Each $\nu$ is some $\mu + \rho$ as in step 3, so $\nu - \rho$ is dominant, $\nu - \rho \le \lambda$, and $(\nu, \nu) = (\lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho)$.
5. For dominant $\lambda$ and $\lambda' = \nu - \rho$ we write $\lambda - \lambda' = \sum n_\alpha \alpha$ with $n_\alpha \ge 0$. Then
$$(\lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho) - (\lambda' + \rho, \lambda' + \rho) = (\lambda - \lambda', \lambda + \lambda' + 2\rho) = \sum_{\alpha \in \Delta} n_\alpha (\alpha, \lambda + \lambda' + 2 \rho).$$
Each term is $\ge 0$, and it is $> 0$ when $n_\alpha > 0$, because $\lambda + \lambda' + 2\rho$ is strictly dominant. So equality forces $\lambda' = \lambda$, and the only term is $A_{\lambda + \rho}$, with coefficient $b_\lambda = 1$. $\square$

In characteristic $p$, the same formula holds for the character of $H^0(\lambda)$, by Remark 4.2.14.

**Example 4.3.5 (the formula for $SL_2$).** For $SL_2$ we have $X = \mathbb{Z} \varpi$, $\alpha = 2 \varpi$ and $\rho = \varpi$. Write $x = e^\varpi$. Then $A_\rho = x - x^{-1}$ and
$$\mathrm{ch}\, L(n \varpi) = \frac{x^{n+1} - x^{-n-1}}{x - x^{-1}} = x^n + x^{n-2} + \dots + x^{-n}.$$
Section 4.4 computes the quotient for $GL_n$, where $A_\rho$ becomes a Vandermonde determinant.

### The dimension formula

**Corollary 4.3.6 (Weyl dimension formula).** Let $k$ have characteristic $0$ and let $\lambda \in X^+$. Then
$$\dim L(\lambda) = \prod_{\alpha \in R^+} \frac{\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle}{\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle} = \prod_{\alpha \in R^+} \frac{(\lambda + \rho, \alpha)}{(\rho, \alpha)}.$$

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The dimension is the value of the character at $e^\mu \mapsto 1$, but both alternating sums vanish there, so we need a limit. For $\nu \in X' \otimes \mathbb{R}$, let $F_\nu : \mathbb{Z}[X'] \to C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ be the ring homomorphism with $F_\nu(e^\mu)(t) = \exp(t (\mu, \nu))$. Since $W$ preserves the inner product and $\varepsilon(w^{-1}) = \varepsilon(w)$,
$$F_\nu(A_\mu)(t) = \sum_{w} \varepsilon(w) \exp(t(w\mu, \nu)) = \sum_w \varepsilon(w) \exp(t(\mu, w^{-1}\nu)) = F_\mu(A_\nu)(t).$$
Apply this with $\nu = \rho$ and use Theorem 4.3.3 for $F_\mu(A_\rho)$:
$$F_\rho(A_\mu)(t) = F_\mu(A_\rho)(t) = \prod_{\alpha > 0} \left( e^{t(\mu, \alpha)/2} - e^{-t(\mu,\alpha)/2} \right) = t^N \prod_{\alpha > 0} (\mu, \alpha) + O(t^{N+1}),$$
where $N = |R^+|$. Theorem 4.3.4 gives $F_\rho(A_\rho) \, F_\rho(\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda)) = F_\rho(A_{\lambda + \rho})$. The value of $F_\rho(\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda))$ at $t = 0$ is $\dim L(\lambda)$. The numbers $(\rho, \alpha)$ are positive, because $\rho$ is strictly dominant. Divide by $t^N$ and let $t \to 0$: the first formula follows from the second, since $\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2(x,\alpha)/(\alpha,\alpha)$. $\square$

**Example 4.3.7 (dimensions in rank $2$).** For $SL_3$, the positive coroots are $\alpha_1^\vee$, $\alpha_2^\vee$ and $\alpha_1^\vee + \alpha_2^\vee$. For $\lambda = a \varpi_1 + b \varpi_2$ the numbers $\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ are $a + 1$, $b + 1$ and $a + b + 2$, and for $\rho$ they are $1, 1, 2$. So
$$\dim L(a \varpi_1 + b \varpi_2) = \frac{(a+1)(b+1)(a+b+2)}{2}.$$
This gives $3$ for $\varpi_1$ and $\varpi_2$, $6$ for $2 \varpi_1$, and $8$ for $\varpi_1 + \varpi_2$, the adjoint representation. For $Sp_4$, with the diagonal torus $\mathrm{diag}(x_1, x_2, x_2^{-1}, x_1^{-1})$, the positive roots are $e_1 - e_2$, $2 e_2$, $e_1 + e_2$ and $2 e_1$, with coroots $e_1 - e_2$, $e_2$, $e_1 + e_2$ and $e_1$ in the dual basis. The fundamental weights are $\varpi_1 = e_1$ and $\varpi_2 = e_1 + e_2$, and $\rho = 2 e_1 + e_2$. For $\lambda = a \varpi_1 + b \varpi_2 = (a + b) e_1 + b e_2$ we get
$$\dim L(a \varpi_1 + b \varpi_2) = \frac{(a+1)(b+1)(a+2b+3)(a+b+2)}{6}.$$
This gives $4$ for $\varpi_1$, the standard representation; $5$ for $\varpi_2$, which is the kernel of the contraction $\wedge^2 k^4 \to k$ given by the symplectic form; and $10$ for $2 \varpi_1$, the adjoint representation $\mathrm{Sym}^2 k^4$. Exercise 3 does the same for $G_2$.

### Multiplicities and tensor products

Let $P : \mathbb{Z} R \to \mathbb{N}$ be the *Kostant partition function*: $P(\nu)$ is the number of ways to write $\nu$ as a sum of positive roots, with repetitions and without regard to order. So $P(0) = 1$, and $P(\nu) = 0$ unless $\nu \ge 0$. In the ring $\mathcal{E}$ of the proof of Theorem 4.3.4, the element $\prod_{\alpha > 0}(1 - e^{-\alpha})$ is invertible, with inverse $\prod_{\alpha > 0} (1 + e^{-\alpha} + e^{-2\alpha} + \dots) = \sum_{\nu} P(\nu) e^{-\nu}$.

**Theorem 4.3.8 (Kostant multiplicity formula).** Let $k$ have characteristic $0$, and let $\lambda \in X^+$ and $\mu \in X$. Then
$$m_{L(\lambda)}(\mu) = \sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w) \, P\big( w(\lambda + \rho) - (\mu + \rho) \big).$$

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* By Theorems 4.3.3 and 4.3.4, in $\mathcal{E}$,
$$\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda) = \frac{e^{-\rho} A_{\lambda+\rho}}{\prod_{\alpha > 0}(1 - e^{-\alpha})} = \sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w) e^{w(\lambda + \rho) - \rho} \sum_{\nu} P(\nu) e^{-\nu}.$$
The coefficient of $e^\mu$ comes from the pairs $(w, \nu)$ with $\nu = w(\lambda + \rho) - \rho - \mu$. $\square$

**Example 4.3.9 (the adjoint representation of $SL_3$).** Take $\lambda = \varpi_1 + \varpi_2 = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, which equals $\rho$, and $\mu = 0$. The orbit $W\rho$ is the set of all six roots, and $w(\lambda + \rho) - \rho = 2 w \rho - \rho$. For $w = 1$ this is $\rho = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, and $P(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2) = 2$: it is the root $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, or the sum $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$ of two simple roots. For $w \rho = \alpha_2$ we get $\alpha_2 - \alpha_1$, which is not $\ge 0$, and in the same way $w \rho = \alpha_1$ gives $\alpha_1 - \alpha_2$. For the three negative roots, $2 w\rho - \rho$ is negative. So $m(0) = 2$. This is the dimension of the diagonal Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak{sl}_3$, which is the zero weight space of the adjoint representation.

For any $\eta \in X$ put $\chi_\eta = A_{\eta + \rho} / A_\rho$. If $\eta + \rho$ is fixed by a reflection, then $A_{\eta + \rho} = 0$ and $\chi_\eta = 0$. Otherwise there is a unique $w \in W$ with $w(\eta + \rho)$ strictly dominant, and then $\chi_\eta = \varepsilon(w) \, \mathrm{ch}\, L(w(\eta + \rho) - \rho)$. The weight $w(\eta + \rho) - \rho$ is the image of $\eta$ under the *dot action* $w \cdot \eta = w(\eta + \rho) - \rho$.

**Theorem 4.3.10 (Brauer and Klimyk).** Let $k$ have characteristic $0$, and let $\lambda, \mu \in X^+$. Then
$$\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda) \cdot \mathrm{ch}\, L(\mu) = \sum_{\nu \in X} m_{L(\mu)}(\nu) \, \chi_{\lambda + \nu}.$$
In particular, if $\lambda + \nu$ is dominant for every weight $\nu$ of $L(\mu)$, then $L(\lambda) \otimes L(\mu) \cong \bigoplus_\nu L(\lambda + \nu)^{m_{L(\mu)}(\nu)}$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Write $m(\nu) = m_{L(\mu)}(\nu)$. By Proposition 4.1.4, $m(w\nu) = m(\nu)$. So
$$A_{\lambda + \rho} \, \mathrm{ch}\, L(\mu) = \sum_{w} \varepsilon(w) e^{w(\lambda + \rho)} \sum_{\nu} m(w \nu) e^{w \nu} = \sum_\nu m(\nu) \sum_w \varepsilon(w) e^{w(\lambda + \nu + \rho)} = \sum_\nu m(\nu) A_{\lambda + \nu + \rho}.$$
Divide by $A_\rho$ and use Theorem 4.3.4. If all $\lambda + \nu$ are dominant, then every $\chi_{\lambda + \nu}$ is the character of $L(\lambda + \nu)$, and Corollary 4.2.12 gives the decomposition. $\square$

In practice one lists the weights of the smaller representation $L(\mu)$, adds them to $\lambda$, and moves each $\lambda + \nu$ that is not dominant to the dominant chamber by the dot action, with a sign. Section 4.4 applies this to $SL_2$ and to the standard representation of $GL_n$.
