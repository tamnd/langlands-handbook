## Weights and characters

This section decomposes a representation of a split reductive group into weight spaces for a maximal torus. It shows that the root groups move the weights by multiples of the roots and that the Weyl group permutes the weights, and it packs the weights with their multiplicities into the character, an element of the group ring of $X^*(T)$ that is invariant under the Weyl group. It ends with the dominant weights, which Section 4.2 shows to be the labels of the irreducible representations. We follow [Mil17], Chapter 22. The example to keep in mind is $GL_n$ with its diagonal torus, where the character of a representation is a symmetric Laurent polynomial in $n$ variables.

### The setting

In this chapter $G$ is a split connected reductive group over a field $k$, with a split maximal torus $T$ and a Borel subgroup $B \supset T$. We write $X = X^*(T)$ and $X^\vee = X_*(T)$, and $(X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$ for the root datum of Chapter 3. The Borel subgroup $B$ determines the set $R^+$ of positive roots, the roots of $T$ on the Lie algebra of the unipotent radical $U$ of $B$, and the set $\Delta \subset R^+$ of simple roots. The opposite Borel subgroup $B^-$ is the Borel subgroup that contains $T$ and has the roots $-R^+$. Its unipotent radical is $U^-$. The Weyl group $W = N_G(T)/T$ acts on $X$ and on $X^\vee$, and it is generated by the simple reflections $s_\alpha(\lambda) = \lambda - \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha$ for $\alpha \in \Delta$. The element $w_0 \in W$ is the longest element, the one that maps $R^+$ to $-R^+$.

We use the following facts from Chapter 3.
1. For each root $\alpha$ there is a *root group* $U_\alpha \subset G$ and an isomorphism $u_\alpha : \mathbb{G}_a \to U_\alpha$ with $t \, u_\alpha(c) \, t^{-1} = u_\alpha(\alpha(t) c)$ for $t \in T$.
2. The product map $\prod_{\alpha \in R^+} U_\alpha \to U$ is an isomorphism of varieties, for any order of the factors, and in the same way for $U^-$ and the roots $-R^+$.
3. The product map $U^- \times T \times U \to G$ is an open immersion. Its image $\Omega = U^- T U$ is the *big cell*, and it is dense in $G$.
4. The group $G$ is generated by $T$ and the root groups $U_\alpha$ for $\alpha \in R$.

For $G = GL_n$ with the diagonal torus $D_n$ and the upper triangular group $B_n$, the root group $U_{e_i - e_j}$ is the group of the matrices $1 + c E_{ij}$, and the big cell is the set of matrices $g$ whose leading principal minors $\det (g_{ab})_{a, b \le m}$ are all invertible, by the $LDU$ decomposition of linear algebra.

Fact 3 has a consequence that we use often. The big cell is isomorphic to $\mathbb{A}^N \times \mathbb{G}_m^r \times \mathbb{A}^N$, where $N = |R^+|$ and $r = \dim T$. If $k$ is infinite, its $k$-points are dense in it, so $G(k)$ is dense in $G$. To keep the proofs short we assume in Sections 4.1 to 4.4 that $k$ is infinite. The results hold over every field, and the reader reduces to this case by an extension of scalars, as in [Mil17], Chapter 22. A *representation* of $G$ is a representation in the sense of Definition 1.1.15. By Theorem 1.1.18 it is the union of its finite-dimensional subrepresentations, so we consider only finite-dimensional ones.

### Weights

**Definition 4.1.1 (weight).** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional representation of $G$. For $\mu \in X$, the *weight space* of $\mu$ is
$$V_\mu = \{ v \in V : t v = \mu(t) v \text{ for all } k\text{-algebras } R \text{ and all } t \in T(R) \},$$
where $v$ stands for $v \otimes 1 \in V \otimes R$. If $V_\mu \neq 0$, then $\mu$ is a *weight* of $V$, and $m_V(\mu) = \dim V_\mu$ is its *multiplicity*. A nonzero element of $V_\mu$ is a *weight vector* of weight $\mu$.

**Proposition 4.1.2 (weight decomposition).** Every finite-dimensional representation $V$ of $G$ is the direct sum $V = \bigoplus_{\mu \in X} V_\mu$ of its weight spaces. A linear map $f : V \to V'$ between representations that commutes with $T$ maps $V_\mu$ into $V'_\mu$.

*Proof.* The torus $T$ is split, so it is diagonalizable, and Theorem 1.4.5 applied to the restriction of $V$ to $T$ gives the decomposition. Its proof shows that $V_\mu$ is the set of $v$ with $\rho(v) = v \otimes \mu$ for the comodule map $\rho$ of $T$. A map $f$ that commutes with $T$ commutes with the comodule maps, so it maps $V_\mu$ into $V'_\mu$. $\square$

On the Lie algebra side, the differential of the action makes $V$ a module over $\mathfrak{g} = \mathrm{Lie}(G)$, and $H \in \mathfrak{t} = \mathrm{Lie}(T)$ acts on $V_\mu$ by the scalar $d\mu(H)$. Mathlib has the weight spaces of a module over a nilpotent Lie algebra as `LieModule.genWeightSpace`. In characteristic $0$ the map $\mu \mapsto d\mu$ is injective on $X$, so the weights of $G$ are determined by the weights of $\mathfrak{t}$. In characteristic $p$ this fails: the weights $\mu$ and $\mu + p \nu$ have the same differential.

**Proposition 4.1.3 (root groups move weights).** Let $V$ be a representation of $G$, let $\alpha \in R$ and let $v \in V_\mu$. There are vectors $v_j \in V_{\mu + j \alpha}$, for $j = 0, 1, \dots, m$, with $v_0 = v$, such that
$$u_\alpha(c) \, v = \sum_{j=0}^m c^j v_j$$
for every $k$-algebra $R$ and every $c \in R$. In particular $U$ maps $V_\mu$ into $V_\mu \oplus \bigoplus_{\nu > \mu} V_\nu$, and $U^-$ maps $V_\mu$ into $V_\mu \oplus \bigoplus_{\nu < \mu} V_\nu$, for the order of Definition 4.1.8 below.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The map $c \mapsto u_\alpha(c) v$ is a morphism $\mathbb{G}_a \to V$, that is, a polynomial in $c$ with coefficients in $V$. So $u_\alpha(c) v = \sum_j c^j v_j$ for some $v_j \in V$, and $v_0 = u_\alpha(0) v = v$. Let $t \in T(R)$. By fact 1, $t \, u_\alpha(c) = u_\alpha(\alpha(t) c) \, t$. We apply both sides to $v$:
$$\sum_j c^j \, t v_j = u_\alpha(\alpha(t) c) \, \mu(t) v = \sum_j \mu(t) \alpha(t)^j c^j v_j.$$
This holds for all $c$, in the algebra $R[c]$, so $t v_j = (\mu + j \alpha)(t) \, v_j$, that is, $v_j \in V_{\mu + j\alpha}$. The last statement follows from fact 2, because $U$ is a product of root groups $U_\alpha$ with $\alpha > 0$, and each of them adds nonnegative multiples of positive roots to the weights. $\square$

Differentiating the formula at $c = 0$ shows that the root vector $X_\alpha = du_\alpha(1) \in \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$ maps $V_\mu$ into $V_{\mu + \alpha}$. This is the familiar rule $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha V_\mu \subset V_{\mu + \alpha}$ of Chapter 2.

**Proposition 4.1.4 (the Weyl group permutes the weights).** Let $V$ be a representation of $G$, let $n \in N_G(T)(k)$ and let $w$ be its image in $W$. Then $n V_\mu = V_{w \mu}$ for every $\mu \in X$. In particular $m_V(w \mu) = m_V(\mu)$, and the set of weights of $V$ is stable under $W$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The action of $W$ on $X$ is $(w \mu)(t) = \mu(n^{-1} t n)$. Let $v \in V_\mu$ and $t \in T(R)$. Then $t (n v) = n (n^{-1} t n) v = \mu(n^{-1} t n) \, n v = (w\mu)(t) \, n v$. So $n V_\mu \subset V_{w\mu}$, and the same argument for $n^{-1}$ gives the reverse inclusion. $\square$

**Definition 4.1.5 (character).** Let $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ be the group ring of $X$. We write its elements as finite sums $\sum_\mu c_\mu e^\mu$, with $e^\mu e^\nu = e^{\mu + \nu}$. The *character* of a finite-dimensional representation $V$ is
$$\mathrm{ch}\, V = \sum_{\mu \in X} m_V(\mu) \, e^\mu \in \mathbb{Z}[X].$$
The Weyl group acts on $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ by $w(e^\mu) = e^{w\mu}$, and $\mathrm{ch}\, V$ lies in the ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]^W$ of invariants, by Proposition 4.1.4.

The element $e^\mu$ is the function $t \mapsto \mu(t)$ on $T$, and $\mathbb{Z}[X] \otimes k = \mathcal{O}(T)$. Then $\mathrm{ch}\, V$ is the function $t \mapsto \mathrm{tr}(t \mid V)$ on $T$. For a compact group the character is the trace function, and Section 4.6 uses it in this form.

**Proposition 4.1.6 (properties of characters).** Let $V$ and $V'$ be finite-dimensional representations of $G$.
1. If $0 \to V' \to V \to V'' \to 0$ is exact, then $\mathrm{ch}\, V = \mathrm{ch}\, V' + \mathrm{ch}\, V''$.
2. $\mathrm{ch}(V \otimes V') = \mathrm{ch}\, V \cdot \mathrm{ch}\, V'$.
3. $\mathrm{ch}(V^*)$ is the image of $\mathrm{ch}\, V$ under the involution $e^\mu \mapsto e^{-\mu}$.
4. $\dim V$ is the image of $\mathrm{ch}\, V$ under the augmentation $e^\mu \mapsto 1$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Part 1 holds because an exact sequence of representations is exact on each weight space, by Proposition 4.1.2. For part 2, $V_\mu \otimes V'_\nu$ lies in $(V \otimes V')_{\mu + \nu}$, and these spaces add up to $V \otimes V'$, so $(V \otimes V')_\lambda = \bigoplus_{\mu + \nu = \lambda} V_\mu \otimes V'_\nu$. For part 3, the dual basis of a basis of weight vectors of $V$ consists of weight vectors of $V^*$, with the opposite weights. Part 4 is the equality $\dim V = \sum_\mu \dim V_\mu$. $\square$

**Example 4.1.7 (the characters of some representations of $GL_n$).** Let $G = GL_n$ and $T = D_n$, so that $X = \mathbb{Z}^n$ with basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$. We write $x_i = e^{e_i}$, so that $\mathbb{Z}[X] = \mathbb{Z}[x_1^{\pm 1}, \dots, x_n^{\pm 1}]$ and $W = S_n$ permutes the variables. The invariants $\mathbb{Z}[X]^W$ are the symmetric Laurent polynomials. On the standard representation $V = k^n$, the basis vector $e_i$ has weight $e_i$, so $\mathrm{ch}\, V = x_1 + \dots + x_n$. The vectors $e_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{i_m}$ with $i_1 < \dots < i_m$ form a basis of weight vectors of $\wedge^m V$, and the monomials $e_{i_1} \cdots e_{i_m}$ with $i_1 \le \dots \le i_m$ form a basis of weight vectors of $\mathrm{Sym}^m V$. So
$$\mathrm{ch} \wedge^m V = e_m(x_1, \dots, x_n), \qquad \mathrm{ch}\, \mathrm{Sym}^m V = h_m(x_1, \dots, x_n),$$
the elementary and the complete symmetric polynomials of degree $m$. The determinant has character $x_1 \cdots x_n$. The adjoint representation on $M_n$ has character $\sum_{i,j} x_i x_j^{-1} = n + \sum_{i \neq j} x_i x_j^{-1}$, as in Example 1.4.14: its weights are $0$, with multiplicity $n$, and the roots, with multiplicity $1$.

### Dominant weights

**Definition 4.1.8 (the order and the dominant weights).** For $\lambda, \mu \in X$ we write $\mu \le \lambda$ if $\lambda - \mu$ is a sum of positive roots, that is, if $\lambda - \mu = \sum_{\alpha \in \Delta} c_\alpha \alpha$ with all $c_\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$. A weight $\lambda \in X$ is *dominant* if $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \ge 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$, and *strictly dominant* if $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle > 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$. The dominant weights form a monoid $X^+$.

The relation $\le$ is a partial order, because the simple roots are linearly independent. It depends on $B$. Two weights are comparable only if their difference lies in the root lattice $\mathbb{Z} R$. For a dominant weight $\lambda$ and a positive root $\alpha$ we also have $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \ge 0$, because $\alpha^\vee$ is a nonnegative combination of the simple coroots.

**Lemma 4.1.9 (dominant representatives).** Every $W$-orbit in $X$ contains exactly one dominant weight. If $\lambda$ is dominant, then $w \lambda \le \lambda$ for every $w \in W$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Let $\mu \in X$. The orbit $W \mu$ is finite, so it has an element $\lambda$ that is maximal for $\le$. For $\alpha \in \Delta$ we have $s_\alpha \lambda = \lambda - \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha$. If $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle < 0$, then $s_\alpha \lambda > \lambda$, which contradicts the maximality. So $\lambda$ is dominant. For the second statement we use induction on the length $\ell(w)$, the length of a shortest word for $w$ in the simple reflections. If $\ell(w) > 0$, write $w = w' s_\alpha$ with $\alpha \in \Delta$ and $\ell(w') = \ell(w) - 1$. The theory of root systems shows that then $w'(\alpha) > 0$ ([Hum72], Section 10). So
$$w \lambda = w'(\lambda - \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha) = w' \lambda - \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \, w'(\alpha) \le w' \lambda \le \lambda,$$
by the induction hypothesis and because $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \ge 0$. This also gives the uniqueness. If $\lambda$ and $\lambda' = w \lambda$ are dominant, then $\lambda' \le \lambda$ and $\lambda = w^{-1} \lambda' \le \lambda'$, so $\lambda = \lambda'$. $\square$

The same proof works for any $W$-stable subgroup $X'$ of $X \otimes \mathbb{Q}$ that contains the roots and satisfies $\langle \mu, \alpha^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}$ for $\mu \in X'$ and $\alpha \in R$. Section 4.3 uses it for $X' = X + \mathbb{Z} \rho$, with the weight $\rho$ defined at the end of this section. The finiteness of the set of dominant weights below a given one is used several times below.

**Lemma 4.1.10 (finiteness below a dominant weight).** Let $\lambda \in X^+$. The set of dominant weights $\mu$ with $\mu \le \lambda$ is finite.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Choose a positive definite inner product $( \ , \ )$ on $X \otimes \mathbb{R}$ that is invariant under $W$, for example the average over $W$ of any inner product. For $\alpha \in \Delta$ the reflection $s_\alpha$ is then orthogonal, so $\langle \mu, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2 (\mu, \alpha)/(\alpha, \alpha)$, and $\mu$ is dominant if and only if $(\mu, \alpha) \ge 0$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$. Let $\mu \le \lambda$ be dominant and put $\pi = \lambda - \mu$, a nonnegative combination of simple roots. Then
$$(\lambda, \lambda) - (\mu, \mu) = (\lambda + \mu, \pi) \ge 0,$$
because $\lambda + \mu$ is dominant. So $\mu$ lies in the coset $\lambda + \mathbb{Z} R$ and in the ball of radius $(\lambda, \lambda)^{1/2}$. A lattice coset meets a ball in a finite set. $\square$

**Example 4.1.11 (dominant weights of $GL_n$, $SL_n$ and $PGL_n$).** For $GL_n$ with $B_n$, the simple roots are $\alpha_i = e_i - e_{i+1}$ and the simple coroots are $\alpha_i^\vee = e_i^\vee - e_{i+1}^\vee$. So $\lambda = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n) \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ is dominant if and only if
$$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n.$$
The Weyl group $S_n$ permutes the coordinates, and the dominant element of an orbit is the decreasing rearrangement. The order is $\mu \le \lambda$ if and only if $\sum_{i \le m} \mu_i \le \sum_{i \le m} \lambda_i$ for all $m$, with equality for $m = n$. This is the *dominance order* of the theory of partitions. For $SL_n$, the lattice is $X = \mathbb{Z}^n / \mathbb{Z}(1, \dots, 1)$ by Example 1.4.9, and the dominant weights are the classes of the nonincreasing sequences. The classes of $\varpi_i = e_1 + \dots + e_i$, for $1 \le i \le n - 1$, are the *fundamental weights*: they satisfy $\langle \varpi_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle = \delta_{ij}$, and $X^+ = \bigoplus_i \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \varpi_i$ is a free monoid. For $PGL_n$, the lattice is $\{ \lambda \in \mathbb{Z}^n : \sum \lambda_i = 0 \}$, and the dominant weights are the nonincreasing sequences with sum $0$. For $n = 2$ we have $X^+(SL_2) = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \varpi$ with $\alpha = 2\varpi$, and $X^+(PGL_2) = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \alpha$. For $PGL_3$ the monoid $X^+$ is not free (Exercise 1).

For a semisimple group $G$, the lattice $X$ lies between the root lattice $\mathbb{Z} R$ and the weight lattice $P = \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{Q} R : \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ for all } \alpha \in R \}$. The group is simply connected if $X = P$, and adjoint if $X = \mathbb{Z} R$ (Chapter 3). In the simply connected case the fundamental weights $\varpi_\alpha$, defined by $\langle \varpi_\alpha, \beta^\vee \rangle = \delta_{\alpha \beta}$ for $\alpha, \beta \in \Delta$, lie in $X$, and $X^+$ is the free monoid on them.

For every reductive $G$ we put $\rho = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha \in R^+} \alpha \in X \otimes \mathbb{Q}$. For $\alpha \in \Delta$ the reflection $s_\alpha$ permutes the positive roots other than $\alpha$ and maps $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$ ([Hum72], Section 10). So $s_\alpha \rho = \rho - \alpha$, and $\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 1$ for every simple root $\alpha$. Since every coroot is an integral combination of the simple coroots, $\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $\alpha \in R$, and $w \rho - \rho \in \mathbb{Z} R$ for all $w \in W$. In the semisimple case $\rho$ is the sum of the fundamental weights, so it lies in $P$, but it need not lie in $X$. For $SL_2$ it is $\varpi$. For $PGL_2$ it is $\frac{1}{2}\alpha$, which is not in $X(PGL_2)$. For $GL_n$ it is $\frac{1}{2}(n - 1, n - 3, \dots, 1 - n)$, which lies in $X$ only when $n$ is odd.
