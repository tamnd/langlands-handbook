## The theorem of the highest weight

This section proves that the irreducible representations of $G$ correspond to the dominant weights. An irreducible representation has a unique line fixed by $U$, and the weight $\lambda$ of this line is dominant and lies above all the other weights. To go back from $\lambda$ to the representation we use the induced module $H^0(\lambda)$, a space of functions on $G$, and Frobenius reciprocity. In characteristic $0$ every representation is a direct sum of irreducible ones, and it is determined by its character. We follow [Mil17], Chapter 22, and we keep the notation and the assumptions of Section 4.1. The example to keep in mind is $SL_2$, where $H^0(n)$ is the space of binary forms of degree $n$.

### Highest weight vectors

**Definition 4.2.1 (highest weight vector).** Let $V$ be a representation of $G$. A *highest weight vector* of weight $\lambda$ in $V$ is a weight vector $v \in V_\lambda$ that is fixed by $U$, that is, a vector that spans a line stable under $B$ on which $B$ acts by $\lambda$.

**Lemma 4.2.2 (highest weight vectors exist).** Every nonzero finite-dimensional representation $V$ of $G$ contains a highest weight vector.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The group $U$ is unipotent, so the subspace $V^U$ of fixed vectors is nonzero by Definition 1.5.1. It is stable under $T$, because $T$ normalizes $U$: for $u \in U$, $t \in T$ and $v \in V^U$ we have $u t v = t (t^{-1} u t) v = t v$. By Proposition 4.1.2, $V^U$ is the sum of its intersections with the weight spaces $V_\mu$, and a nonzero element of one of them is a highest weight vector. $\square$

**Proposition 4.2.3 (the subrepresentation generated by a highest weight vector).** Let $v \in V$ be a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda$, and let $W$ be the subrepresentation of $V$ generated by $v$. Then $W$ is spanned by the vectors $u v$ for $u \in U^-(k)$. Every weight $\mu$ of $W$ satisfies $\mu \le \lambda$, and $W_\lambda = k v$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Since $k$ is infinite, $G(k)$ is dense in $G$, so $W$ is the span of the vectors $g v$ for $g \in G(k)$. Let $W'$ be the span of the vectors $u v$ for $u \in U^-(k)$. The map $G \to V$, $g \mapsto g v$, is a morphism, and the set of $g$ with $g v \in W'$ is closed. It contains the set $\Omega(k) = U^-(k) T(k) U(k)$, because $u t u' v = \lambda(t) \, u v$ for $u \in U^-(k)$, $t \in T(k)$ and $u' \in U(k)$. This set is dense in $G$ by fact 3 of Section 4.1. So $g v \in W'$ for all $g \in G(k)$, and $W = W'$. By Proposition 4.1.3, each $u \in U^-(k)$ maps $v$ to $v$ plus a sum of weight vectors of weights $\mu < \lambda$. So the weights of $W$ are at most $\lambda$, and $W_\lambda$ is spanned by $v$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2.4 (irreducible representations have a highest weight).** Let $V$ be an irreducible finite-dimensional representation of $G$.
1. The space $V^U$ is a line, spanned by a weight vector $v$. Its weight $\lambda$ is the *highest weight* of $V$.
2. Every weight $\mu$ of $V$ satisfies $\mu \le \lambda$, and $m_V(\lambda) = 1$.
3. The weight $\lambda$ is dominant.
4. The weight $w_0 \lambda$ is the lowest weight of $V$: every weight $\mu$ satisfies $w_0 \lambda \le \mu$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* By Lemma 4.2.2 there is a highest weight vector $v$, of some weight $\lambda$. It generates $V$, since $V$ is irreducible. Proposition 4.2.3 gives part 2. Let $v' \in V^U$. As in the proof of Lemma 4.2.2, its components in the weight spaces are fixed by $U$. A nonzero component of weight $\mu$ is a highest weight vector, and it generates $V$, so $\lambda \le \mu$ by Proposition 4.2.3 applied to it, and $\mu \le \lambda$ by part 2. Thus $\mu = \lambda$, and $v' \in V_\lambda = k v$. This proves part 1. For part 3, let $\alpha \in \Delta$. By Proposition 4.1.4 the weight $s_\alpha \lambda = \lambda - \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha$ is a weight of $V$, so it is at most $\lambda$, and $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \ge 0$. For part 4, let $\mu$ be a weight. Then $w_0 \mu$ is a weight, so $w_0 \mu \le \lambda$. The element $w_0$ maps the positive roots to negative roots, so it reverses the order, and $\mu = w_0 (w_0 \mu) \ge w_0 \lambda$. The weight $w_0 \lambda$ occurs by Proposition 4.1.4. $\square$

By symmetry, the line $V^{U^-}$ is the weight space $V_{w_0 \lambda}$. In the language of Lie algebras, a highest weight vector is a weight vector killed by all the root vectors $X_\alpha$ with $\alpha > 0$, and Theorem 4.2.4 is the analogue of the theorem on standard cyclic modules in Chapter 2. The proof here uses the group: the density of the big cell replaces the theorem of Poincaré, Birkhoff and Witt.

**Corollary 4.2.5 (an irreducible representation from a highest weight vector).** Let $v \in V$ be a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda$, and let $W$ be the subrepresentation generated by $v$. Then $W$ has a unique maximal proper subrepresentation $W'$, and $W / W'$ is irreducible with highest weight $\lambda$. In particular, an irreducible representation with highest weight $\lambda$ exists as soon as some representation contains a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Let $M \subset W$ be a subrepresentation. Then $M$ is the sum of its weight spaces $M_\mu = M \cap W_\mu$. If $M_\lambda \neq 0$, then $v \in M$ because $W_\lambda = k v$, and $M = W$. So every proper subrepresentation has $M_\lambda = 0$, and the sum $W'$ of all proper subrepresentations also has $W'_\lambda = 0$. Thus $W'$ is the unique maximal proper subrepresentation, and $W / W'$ is irreducible. The image of $v$ in $W / W'$ is nonzero, fixed by $U$ and of weight $\lambda$, so it spans $(W/W')^U$ by Theorem 4.2.4. $\square$

### The induced module

**Definition 4.2.6 (the induced module $H^0(\lambda)$).** Let $\lambda \in X$. We extend $\lambda$ to a character of $B^-$ by the quotient map $B^- \to B^-/U^- = T$. The *induced module* is
$$H^0(\lambda) = \{ f \in \mathcal{O}(G) : f(x b) = \lambda(b)^{-1} f(x) \text{ for all } k\text{-algebras } R, \ x \in G(R), \ b \in B^-(R) \},$$
with the action of $G$ by left translation, $(g f)(x) = f(g^{-1} x)$. We write $k_\lambda$ for the line $k$ on which $B^-$ acts by $\lambda$.

The space $H^0(\lambda)$ is a subrepresentation of $\mathcal{O}(G)$ for the action by left translation, because left and right translations commute. In geometric terms, $H^0(\lambda)$ is the space of global sections of a line bundle $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$ on the flag variety $G / B^-$. This variety is projective by Theorem 1.6.6, so $H^0(\lambda)$ is finite-dimensional ([Mil17], Chapter 22). The notation $H^0$ comes from this interpretation. The higher cohomology groups $H^i(G/B^-, \mathcal{L}(\lambda))$ are the subject of the theorem of Borel, Weil and Bott, which we do not need.

**Lemma 4.2.7 (Frobenius reciprocity).** For every representation $V$ of $G$, the map $\varphi \mapsto \mathrm{ev} \circ \varphi$, where $\mathrm{ev}(f) = f(e)$, is a bijection
$$\mathrm{Hom}_G(V, H^0(\lambda)) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{B^-}(V, k_\lambda).$$

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* First, $\mathrm{ev}$ is a map of representations of $B^-$ from $H^0(\lambda)$ to $k_\lambda$: for $b \in B^-(R)$ we have $(b f)(e) = f(b^{-1}) = \lambda(b^{-1})^{-1} f(e) = \lambda(b) f(e)$. Conversely, let $\psi : V \to k_\lambda$ be a map of representations of $B^-$. For $v \in V$, put $f_v(x) = \psi(x^{-1} v)$. This is a regular function on $G$, a matrix coefficient of $V$. It lies in $H^0(\lambda)$, because $f_v(x b) = \psi(b^{-1} x^{-1} v) = \lambda(b)^{-1} \psi(x^{-1} v)$. The map $v \mapsto f_v$ commutes with $G$, because $f_{g v}(x) = \psi(x^{-1} g v) = f_v(g^{-1} x)$. We have $f_v(e) = \psi(v)$, so $\psi \mapsto (v \mapsto f_v)$ is a right inverse of the map of the lemma. It is also a left inverse: if $\psi = \mathrm{ev} \circ \varphi$, then $f_v(x) = \varphi(x^{-1} v)(e) = (x^{-1} \varphi(v))(e) = \varphi(v)(x)$. $\square$

**Proposition 4.2.8 (the structure of $H^0(\lambda)$).** Let $\lambda \in X$.
1. The space $H^0(\lambda)^U$ has dimension at most $1$. If it is nonzero, it is spanned by a weight vector of weight $\lambda$.
2. If $H^0(\lambda) \neq 0$, then every nonzero subrepresentation of $H^0(\lambda)$ contains $H^0(\lambda)^U$. So $H^0(\lambda)$ has a unique irreducible subrepresentation $L(\lambda)$, and $L(\lambda)$ has highest weight $\lambda$.
3. If $H^0(\lambda) \neq 0$, then $\lambda$ is dominant.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Let $n_0 \in N_G(T)(k)$ represent $w_0$. Conjugation by $n_0$ maps $U^-$ to $U$ and $U$ to $U^-$, so it maps the big cell $U^- T U$ onto $U T U^- = U B^-$. Thus $U B^-$ is open and dense in $G$. Let $f \in H^0(\lambda)^U$. For $u \in U(R)$ and $b \in B^-(R)$ we have
$$f(u b) = (u^{-1} f)(b) = f(b) = \lambda(b)^{-1} f(e).$$
Since $G$ is smooth and connected, a regular function on $G$ is determined by its restriction to a dense open subset. So $f$ is determined by $f(e)$, and $\dim H^0(\lambda)^U \le 1$. For $t \in T(R)$ the function $t f$ is again fixed by $U$, since $T$ normalizes $U$, and $(t f)(e) = f(t^{-1}) = \lambda(t) f(e)$. So $t f = \lambda(t) f$. This proves part 1. For part 2, let $M \neq 0$ be a subrepresentation. By Theorem 1.1.18 we may assume that $M$ is finite-dimensional. Then $M^U \neq 0$ by Lemma 4.2.2, and $M^U \subset H^0(\lambda)^U$, which is a line. So $M$ contains this line. The intersection of all nonzero subrepresentations therefore contains $H^0(\lambda)^U$, so it is the unique irreducible subrepresentation $L(\lambda)$. It contains a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda$, and it has highest weight $\lambda$ by Theorem 4.2.4. Part 3 follows from part 3 of Theorem 4.2.4. $\square$

### The classification

**Theorem 4.2.9 (classification by the highest weight).** For each dominant weight $\lambda$, the induced module $H^0(\lambda)$ is nonzero, and its socle $L(\lambda)$ is an irreducible representation with highest weight $\lambda$. Every irreducible finite-dimensional representation of $G$ is isomorphic to $L(\lambda)$ for exactly one $\lambda \in X^+$, namely its highest weight. For $\lambda \notin X^+$ we have $H^0(\lambda) = 0$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Let $V$ be irreducible with highest weight $\lambda$, which is dominant by Theorem 4.2.4. Let $V' = \bigoplus_{\mu \neq \lambda} V_\mu$. It is stable under $T$. By Proposition 4.1.3, $U^-$ maps $V_\mu$ into the sum of $V_\mu$ and of weight spaces of weights below $\mu$. If $\mu \neq \lambda$, then $\mu < \lambda$, and no weight below $\mu$ equals $\lambda$. So $V'$ is stable under $B^- = T U^-$, and the quotient $V / V'$ is the line $k_\lambda$. By Lemma 4.2.7 the quotient map gives a nonzero map $V \to H^0(\lambda)$ of representations of $G$. It is injective because $V$ is irreducible, and its image is an irreducible subrepresentation, so it is $L(\lambda)$ by Proposition 4.2.8. Thus $V \cong L(\lambda)$, and in particular $H^0(\lambda) \neq 0$ when $\lambda$ is the highest weight of an irreducible representation. The highest weight of $L(\lambda)$ is $\lambda$, so different weights give nonisomorphic representations. Part 3 of Proposition 4.2.8 gives the last statement.

It remains to show that $H^0(\lambda) \neq 0$ for every dominant $\lambda$. We give the main steps. Define a function $f_\lambda$ on the open set $U B^- = U T U^-$ by $f_\lambda(u t u') = \lambda(t)^{-1}$. The proof of Proposition 4.2.8 shows that $H^0(\lambda) \neq 0$ if and only if $f_\lambda$ extends to a regular function on $G$. The Bruhat decomposition of Chapter 3, in the form $G = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} B w B^-$, shows that the complement of $U B^-$ is the union of the closures $D_\alpha$ of the sets $B s_\alpha B^-$ for $\alpha \in \Delta$, which are irreducible of codimension $1$. Since $G$ is smooth, the rational function $f_\lambda$ is regular as soon as it has no pole along any $D_\alpha$. The order of $f_\lambda$ along $D_\alpha$ can be computed in the subgroup of semisimple rank $1$ generated by $T$, $U_\alpha$ and $U_{-\alpha}$, and it equals $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle$. For $SL_2$ this is the computation of Example 4.2.10. So $f_\lambda$ is regular if $\lambda$ is dominant. The details are in [Mil17], Chapter 22, and in Jantzen, *Representations of Algebraic Groups*, Part II, Chapter 2. $\square$

**Example 4.2.10 (the induced modules of $SL_2$).** Let $G = SL_2$, with $T$ diagonal, $B$ upper triangular and $B^-$ lower triangular. Write $x = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ for the universal point and $\lambda = n \varpi$, so that $\lambda(\mathrm{diag}(s, s^{-1})) = s^n$. For $b' = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} s & 0 \\ r & s^{-1} \end{smallmatrix}\right) \in B^-$, the product $x b'$ has first column $s (a, c) + r (b, d)$ and second column $s^{-1}(b, d)$. A function $f \in \mathcal{O}(SL_2)$ that is invariant under right translation by the lower unitriangular matrices depends only on the second column $(b, d)$, which defines the quotient map $SL_2 \to \mathbb{A}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, and $\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{A}^2 \setminus \{0\}) = k[b, d]$. The condition $f(x b') = s^{-n} f(x)$ then says that $f$ is homogeneous of degree $n$. So
$$H^0(n \varpi) = k[b, d]_n,$$
the space of binary forms of degree $n$ in the second column. It is $0$ for $n < 0$ and has dimension $n + 1$ for $n \ge 0$. The torus element $t = \mathrm{diag}(s, s^{-1})$ maps $b^i d^{n-i}$ to $s^{n - 2i} b^i d^{n-i}$, because $t^{-1} x$ has second column $(s^{-1} b, s d)$. So the weights are $n, n - 2, \dots, -n$, each with multiplicity $1$, and $d^n$ is the highest weight vector. Here $U B^-$ is the set $d \neq 0$, its complement is the divisor $d = 0$, and the function $f_\lambda$ of the proof of Theorem 4.2.9 is $d^n$, which has order $n = \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ along this divisor. The representation $H^0(n\varpi)$ is the space of polynomial functions of degree $n$ on $k^2$. Since $k^2$ is self-dual as a representation of $SL_2$, it is isomorphic to $\mathrm{Sym}^n(k^2)$. Section 4.4 shows that it is irreducible in characteristic $0$, and that it is not irreducible for $n = p$ in characteristic $p$.

### Characteristic zero

**Theorem 4.2.11 (complete reducibility).** Let $k$ have characteristic $0$. Every finite-dimensional representation of $G$ is a direct sum of irreducible representations. In particular $H^0(\lambda) = L(\lambda)$ for every $\lambda \in X^+$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Outline of the proof.* In characteristic $0$, a subspace of a representation of the connected group $G$ is a subrepresentation if and only if it is stable under the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. The Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{z} \oplus [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$, where $\mathfrak{z}$ is the Lie algebra of the central torus and $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ is semisimple. The theorem of Weyl from Chapter 2 ([Hum72], Section 6) says that every finite-dimensional module over a semisimple Lie algebra in characteristic $0$ is semisimple, and $\mathfrak{z}$ acts on $V$ by commuting semisimple endomorphisms, which preserve the isotypic components of $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$. So $V$ is a semisimple $\mathfrak{g}$-module, and hence a semisimple representation of $G$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ there is a second proof by the unitary trick of Corollary 4.6.11. Since $H^0(\lambda)$ is semisimple and has a simple socle, it is irreducible. See [Mil17], Chapter 22. $\square$

**Corollary 4.2.12 (characters determine representations).** Let $k$ have characteristic $0$. For $\mu \in X^+$ let $m_\mu = \sum_{\nu \in W \mu} e^\nu$ be the orbit sum. Then
$$\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda) = m_\lambda + \sum_{\mu \in X^+, \ \mu < \lambda} c_{\lambda \mu} \, m_\mu$$
with integers $c_{\lambda\mu} \ge 0$. The characters $\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda)$ for $\lambda \in X^+$ form a basis of the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}[X]^W$. Two finite-dimensional representations of $G$ are isomorphic if and only if they have the same character.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* By Lemma 4.1.9 the orbit sums $m_\mu$, for $\mu \in X^+$, form a basis of $\mathbb{Z}[X]^W$. The formula for $\mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda)$ follows from Theorem 4.2.4 and Proposition 4.1.4: the character is $W$-invariant, $\lambda$ has multiplicity $1$, and the other dominant weights are below $\lambda$. By Lemma 4.1.10 the sum is finite, and the matrix that expresses the characters in the basis $m_\mu$ is unitriangular for the order $\le$, on each of the finite sets $\{\mu \in X^+ : \mu \le \lambda\}$. So it is invertible over $\mathbb{Z}$, and the characters form a basis. By Theorem 4.2.11 a representation $V$ is isomorphic to $\bigoplus_\lambda L(\lambda)^{n_\lambda}$, and $\mathrm{ch}\, V = \sum_\lambda n_\lambda \, \mathrm{ch}\, L(\lambda)$ determines the numbers $n_\lambda$. $\square$

**Proposition 4.2.13 (duals and tensor products).** Let $\lambda, \mu \in X^+$.
1. $L(\lambda)^* \cong L(-w_0 \lambda)$.
2. In $L(\lambda) \otimes L(\mu)$ the vector $v_\lambda \otimes v_\mu$ is a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda + \mu$, the weight $\lambda + \mu$ has multiplicity $1$, and all other weights are below $\lambda + \mu$. If $k$ has characteristic $0$, then $L(\lambda + \mu)$ is a direct summand of $L(\lambda) \otimes L(\mu)$ with multiplicity $1$, and the other irreducible summands have highest weights $\nu < \lambda + \mu$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The dual of an irreducible representation is irreducible, and its weights are the negatives of the weights of $L(\lambda)$. By part 4 of Theorem 4.2.4, the largest of them is $-w_0 \lambda$. This proves part 1. For part 2, $U$ fixes $v_\lambda \otimes v_\mu$, and its weight is $\lambda + \mu$. By Proposition 4.1.6, the weights of the tensor product are the sums $\lambda' + \mu'$ of weights of the two factors, so they are at most $\lambda + \mu$, with equality only for $\lambda' = \lambda$ and $\mu' = \mu$. In characteristic $0$ the tensor product is a direct sum of representations $L(\nu)$, and each $\nu$ is a weight of it. The summand $L(\lambda + \mu)$ occurs, by Corollary 4.2.5, and only once, because $\lambda + \mu$ has multiplicity $1$. $\square$

For $GL_n$, $w_0$ reverses the order of the coordinates, so $L(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)^* \cong L(-\lambda_n, \dots, -\lambda_1)$. For $SL_2$, $w_0 = -1$ on $X$, so every irreducible representation is self-dual. The same holds for every group in which $w_0 = -1$, such as $Sp_{2n}$ and $SO_{2n+1}$.

**Remark 4.2.14 (characteristic $p$).** In characteristic $p > 0$, Theorem 4.2.9 still classifies the irreducible representations, but $H^0(\lambda)$ is in general larger than $L(\lambda)$, and representations need not be semisimple. Kempf's vanishing theorem says that the higher cohomology of $\mathcal{L}(\lambda)$ on $G/B^-$ vanishes for dominant $\lambda$, and it implies that the character of $H^0(\lambda)$ is still given by the formula of Weyl in Section 4.3. The characters of the $L(\lambda)$ are much harder. For a semisimple simply connected group, Steinberg's tensor product theorem $L(\lambda_0 + p \lambda_1) \cong L(\lambda_0) \otimes L(\lambda_1)^{[1]}$, where $^{[1]}$ is the twist by the Frobenius map, reduces them to the *restricted* weights $\lambda_0$, those with $0 \le \langle \lambda_0, \alpha^\vee \rangle < p$ for all $\alpha \in \Delta$. The characters for restricted weights are the subject of the conjecture of Lusztig, see the Notes. The handbook does not use this theory, since the representations of $p$-adic groups in Volume 6 are on complex vector spaces.
