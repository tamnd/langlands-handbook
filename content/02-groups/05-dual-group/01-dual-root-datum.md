## The dual root datum

The axioms of a root datum do not change when we exchange the characters with the cocharacters and the roots with the coroots. So each root datum $\Psi$ has a dual $\Psi^\vee$, and $(\Psi^\vee)^\vee = \Psi$. This section proves the facts on this duality that the rest of the chapter uses. The Weyl groups of $\Psi$ and $\Psi^\vee$ are the same group, acting on dual lattices. A base of $\Psi$ gives a base of $\Psi^\vee$. The lattices between the root lattice and the weight lattice are exchanged, so that the centre of one group becomes the fundamental group of the other. The example to keep in mind is the rank one case: the root datum of $SL_2$ is $(\mathbb{Z}, \{\pm 2\}, \mathbb{Z}, \{\pm 1\})$, and its dual is the root datum of $PGL_2$. We follow [Spr98], Chapter 7, and [Bor79], Section 1.

### Root data

We recall the definition of Chapter 3. A *lattice* is a free abelian group of finite rank. A pairing $\langle \ , \ \rangle : X \times X^\vee \to \mathbb{Z}$ of lattices is *perfect* if it identifies each lattice with the group of homomorphisms from the other one to $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Definition 5.1.1 (root datum).** A *root datum* is a quadruple $\Psi = (X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$, where $X$ and $X^\vee$ are lattices with a perfect pairing $\langle \ , \ \rangle$, and $R \subset X$ and $R^\vee \subset X^\vee$ are finite subsets with a bijection $R \to R^\vee$, $\alpha \mapsto \alpha^\vee$. For $\alpha \in R$ define the maps
$$s_\alpha(x) = x - \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha \quad (x \in X), \qquad s_{\alpha^\vee}(y) = y - \langle \alpha, y \rangle \alpha^\vee \quad (y \in X^\vee).$$
The conditions are these: for each $\alpha \in R$,
1. $\langle \alpha, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2$;
2. $s_\alpha(R) = R$ and $s_{\alpha^\vee}(R^\vee) = R^\vee$.

The root datum is *reduced* if the only roots proportional to a root $\alpha$ are $\pm \alpha$. The *Weyl group* $W(\Psi)$ is the subgroup of $\mathrm{Aut}(X)$ generated by the maps $s_\alpha$. The *rank* of $\Psi$ is the rank of $X$. The root datum is *semisimple* if $R$ spans the vector space $X_{\mathbb{Q}} = X \otimes \mathbb{Q}$.

Mathlib indexes the roots and the coroots by one set $\iota$ and records the permutations of $\iota$ that the reflections induce. The finiteness of the index set and the freeness of the modules are separate hypotheses there. For a split reductive group $G$ with a split maximal torus $T$, Chapter 3 attaches the root datum $\Psi(G, T) = (X^*(T), R, X_*(T), R^\vee)$, where $R$ is the set of roots of $T$ on $\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ and $\alpha^\vee$ is the coroot of $\alpha$. This root datum is reduced.

Condition 1 gives $s_\alpha(\alpha) = -\alpha$, and $s_\alpha$ fixes the elements $x$ with $\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 0$. Also $s_\alpha^2 = 1$, since
$$s_\alpha(s_\alpha(x)) = x - \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha - \big( \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle - 2 \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \big) \alpha = x.$$
So $s_\alpha$ is a reflection of $X$, and in the same way $s_{\alpha^\vee}$ is a reflection of $X^\vee$. The two reflections are transposes of each other:
$$\langle s_\alpha(x), y \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle - \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \langle \alpha, y \rangle = \langle x, s_{\alpha^\vee}(y) \rangle. \tag{1}$$
The definition says nothing on the coroot of $s_\alpha(\beta)$. The next lemma shows that it is $s_{\alpha^\vee}(\beta^\vee)$. Some authors put this into the definition. Mathlib does so through the permutations of $\iota$.

**Lemma 5.1.2 (uniqueness of the coroot).** Let $\gamma \in X$, let $v \in R^\vee$ and let $u \in X^\vee$ with $\langle \gamma, u \rangle = \langle \gamma, v \rangle = 2$. Suppose that the two maps $a_u(y) = y - \langle \gamma, y \rangle u$ and $a_v(y) = y - \langle \gamma, y \rangle v$ of $X^\vee$ map $R^\vee$ onto $R^\vee$. Then $u = v$.

*Proof.* Put $d = v - u$, so that $\langle \gamma, d \rangle = 0$. For $y \in X^\vee$,
$$a_u(a_v(y)) = y - \langle \gamma, y \rangle v - \big( \langle \gamma, y \rangle - 2 \langle \gamma, y \rangle \big) u = y - \langle \gamma, y \rangle d.$$
Since $\langle \gamma, d \rangle = 0$, the $n$-th power of $a_u a_v$ is $y \mapsto y - n \langle \gamma, y \rangle d$. Apply it to $y = v$. The elements $v - 2 n d$, for $n \ge 0$, all lie in the finite set $R^\vee$. So $d = 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.1.3 (coroots of transformed roots).** Let $\Psi$ be a root datum and let $\alpha, \beta \in R$.
1. $(s_\alpha(\beta))^\vee = s_{\alpha^\vee}(\beta^\vee)$. In particular $(-\alpha)^\vee = -\alpha^\vee$.
2. If $c \alpha \in R$ for a rational number $c$, then $(c \alpha)^\vee = c^{-1} \alpha^\vee$.
3. For $w \in W(\Psi)$ put $w^\vee = (w^{-1})^t \in \mathrm{Aut}(X^\vee)$, the inverse of the transpose. Then $(w \alpha)^\vee = w^\vee(\alpha^\vee)$, and $s_\alpha^\vee = s_{\alpha^\vee}$.

*Proof.* For part 1, put $\gamma = s_\alpha(\beta)$, $v = \gamma^\vee$ and $u = s_{\alpha^\vee}(\beta^\vee)$. By (1), $\langle \gamma, u \rangle = \langle \beta, \beta^\vee \rangle = 2$, and $\langle \gamma, v \rangle = 2$ by condition 1. The map $a_v$ is $s_{\gamma^\vee}$, which preserves $R^\vee$. The map $a_u$ is the conjugate $s_{\alpha^\vee} s_{\beta^\vee} s_{\alpha^\vee}$. Indeed, by (1) and $s_{\alpha^\vee}^2 = 1$,
$$s_{\alpha^\vee} s_{\beta^\vee} s_{\alpha^\vee}(y) = y - \langle \beta, s_{\alpha^\vee}(y) \rangle \, s_{\alpha^\vee}(\beta^\vee) = y - \langle s_\alpha(\beta), y \rangle \, u.$$
So $a_u$ preserves $R^\vee$ as well, and Lemma 5.1.2 gives $u = v$. For $\beta = \alpha$ we get $(-\alpha)^\vee = s_{\alpha^\vee}(\alpha^\vee) = -\alpha^\vee$. For part 2, put $\gamma = c \alpha$, $v = (c\alpha)^\vee$ and $u = c^{-1} \alpha^\vee$. Then $\langle \gamma, u \rangle = 2$, $a_v = s_{(c\alpha)^\vee}$, and $a_u(y) = y - \langle c \alpha, y \rangle c^{-1} \alpha^\vee = s_{\alpha^\vee}(y)$. Lemma 5.1.2 applies again. For part 3, the map $w \mapsto w^\vee$ is a homomorphism, and $s_\alpha^\vee = s_{\alpha^\vee}$ by (1) and $s_\alpha^{-1} = s_\alpha$. Part 1 is the formula for $w = s_\alpha$, and the general case follows by induction on the length of a product of reflections. $\square$

### The dual root datum

**Definition 5.1.4 (dual root datum).** The *dual* of the root datum $\Psi = (X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$ is
$$\Psi^\vee = (X^\vee, R^\vee, X, R),$$
with the pairing $X^\vee \times X \to \mathbb{Z}$, $(y, x) \mapsto \langle x, y \rangle$, and the bijection $R^\vee \to R$, $\alpha^\vee \mapsto \alpha$.

Lean: `RootPairing.flip`, from Mathlib

**Proposition 5.1.5 (properties of the dual).** Let $\Psi$ be a root datum.
1. $\Psi^\vee$ is a root datum, and $(\Psi^\vee)^\vee = \Psi$.
2. $\Psi$ is reduced if and only if $\Psi^\vee$ is reduced.
3. The map $w \mapsto w^\vee = (w^{-1})^t$ is an isomorphism $W(\Psi) \to W(\Psi^\vee)$ that sends $s_\alpha$ to $s_{\alpha^\vee}$.
4. An isomorphism of root data $f : \Psi_1 \to \Psi_2$ is an isomorphism $f : X_1 \to X_2$ that maps $R_1$ onto $R_2$ and whose transpose $f^t : X_2^\vee \to X_1^\vee$ maps $R_2^\vee$ onto $R_1^\vee$. Then $f^t$ is an isomorphism $\Psi_2^\vee \to \Psi_1^\vee$, and $f \mapsto f^t$ is a contravariant equivalence of the groupoid of root data with itself.

Lean: `RootPairing.flip_flip`, from Mathlib, for (1), and `RootPairing.instFlipIsReduced` for (2)

*Proof.* In $\Psi^\vee$ the root $\alpha^\vee$ has the coroot $\alpha$. The reflection of $X^\vee$ that $\Psi^\vee$ attaches to $\alpha^\vee$ is $y \mapsto y - \langle \alpha, y \rangle \alpha^\vee$, which is $s_{\alpha^\vee}$, and the reflection of $X$ that it attaches to the coroot $\alpha$ is $s_\alpha$. So the conditions of Definition 5.1.1 for $\Psi^\vee$ are the conditions for $\Psi$, read in another order. The equality $(\Psi^\vee)^\vee = \Psi$ is clear. For part 2, suppose that $\alpha^\vee$ and $\beta^\vee = c \alpha^\vee$ are coroots with $c \ne \pm 1$. Apply Lemma 5.1.2 to the root datum $\Psi^\vee$, with $\gamma = \beta^\vee$, $v = \beta$ and $u = c^{-1} \alpha$. Then $\langle u, \gamma \rangle = \langle v, \gamma \rangle = 2$. The map $a_v$ is $s_\beta$, and $a_u(x) = x - \langle x, c \alpha^\vee \rangle c^{-1} \alpha = s_\alpha(x)$. Both preserve $R$, so $\beta = c^{-1} \alpha$, and $\Psi$ is not reduced. The converse follows from part 1. Part 3 follows from Corollary 5.1.3, since the group $W(\Psi^\vee)$ is generated by the $s_{\alpha^\vee}$. Part 4 is a translation of the definitions. $\square$

**Example 5.1.6 (rank one).** Let $X = X^\vee = \mathbb{Z}$, with the pairing $\langle a, b \rangle = ab$. There are three root data of rank one with roots.
1. $\Psi_{SL} = (\mathbb{Z}, \{\pm 2\}, \mathbb{Z}, \{\pm 1\})$, with $2^\vee = 1$. This is the root datum of $SL_2$ with its diagonal torus: the character $\mathrm{diag}(t, t^{-1}) \mapsto t$ generates $X$, the root is $t^2$, and the coroot is $s \mapsto \mathrm{diag}(s, s^{-1})$.
2. $\Psi_{PGL} = (\mathbb{Z}, \{\pm 1\}, \mathbb{Z}, \{\pm 2\})$, with $1^\vee = 2$. This is the root datum of $PGL_2$. Its torus is the image of $\mathrm{diag}(t_1, t_2)$, its character group is generated by the root $t_1 / t_2$, and the coroot $s \mapsto \mathrm{diag}(s, s^{-1})$ is twice the generator $s \mapsto \mathrm{diag}(s, 1)$ of the cocharacter group.
3. $\Psi_{GL} = (\mathbb{Z}^2, \{\pm(e_1 - e_2)\}, \mathbb{Z}^2, \{\pm(e_1^\vee - e_2^\vee)\})$. This is the root datum of $GL_2$ and has rank two.

By Definition 5.1.4, $\Psi_{SL}^\vee = \Psi_{PGL}$. The map $e_i \mapsto e_i^\vee$ is an isomorphism $\Psi_{GL} \to \Psi_{GL}^\vee$. The Weyl group in each case is $\{1, s\}$, with $s = -1$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ for the first two, and $s$ the exchange of the coordinates on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ for the third.

### Root lattices and weight lattices

Let $\Psi$ be a root datum. Put $V = X_{\mathbb{Q}}$ and $V^\vee = X^\vee_{\mathbb{Q}}$, and let $V_R$ and $V_R^\vee$ be the subspaces spanned by $R$ and by $R^\vee$. The dimension of $V_R$ is the *semisimple rank* of $\Psi$.

**Proposition 5.1.7 (the semisimple rank).** The spaces $V_R$ and $V_R^\vee$ have the same dimension, and the pairing restricts to a perfect pairing $V_R \times V_R^\vee \to \mathbb{Q}$. Let $V^1 = \{x \in V : \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 0 \text{ for all } \alpha \in R\}$. Then $V = V_R \oplus V^1$. In particular $\Psi$ is semisimple if and only if $\Psi^\vee$ is semisimple.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Define a symmetric bilinear form on $V$ by
$$B(x, x') = \sum_{\beta \in R} \langle x, \beta^\vee \rangle \langle x', \beta^\vee \rangle.$$
It is positive semidefinite, and $B(\alpha, \alpha) \ge \langle \alpha, \alpha^\vee \rangle^2 = 4$ for $\alpha \in R$. For $w \in W(\Psi)$, $\langle w x, \beta^\vee \rangle = \langle x, w^t \beta^\vee \rangle$, and $w^t = (w^{-1})^\vee$ permutes $R^\vee$ by Corollary 5.1.3. So $B$ is invariant under $W(\Psi)$. Fix $\alpha \in R$. If $\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 0$, then $s_\alpha(x) = x$, and $B(x, \alpha) = B(s_\alpha x, s_\alpha \alpha) = -B(x, \alpha)$, so $B(x, \alpha) = 0$. Thus the two linear forms $x \mapsto \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ and $x \mapsto 2 B(x, \alpha) / B(\alpha, \alpha)$ vanish on the same hyperplane, and they agree at $x = \alpha$. So
$$\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = \frac{2 B(x, \alpha)}{B(\alpha, \alpha)} \qquad \text{for all } x \in V. \tag{2}$$
Identify $V^\vee$ with the dual space of $V$. By (2), the map $b : V \to V^\vee$, $x \mapsto B(x, \cdot)$, sends $\alpha$ to $\tfrac12 B(\alpha, \alpha) \alpha^\vee$. So $b(V_R) = V_R^\vee$, and $\dim V_R^\vee \le \dim V_R$. The same argument for $\Psi^\vee$ gives the other inequality. So $b$ restricts to an isomorphism $V_R \to V_R^\vee$. Now $V^1$ is the annihilator of $V_R^\vee$, so $\dim V^1 = \dim V - \dim V_R$. If $x \in V_R \cap V^1$, then $b(x) = 0$, because $B(x, x') = \sum_\beta \langle x, \beta^\vee \rangle \langle x', \beta^\vee \rangle$ and each $\langle x, \beta^\vee \rangle$ vanishes. So $x = 0$, and $V = V_R \oplus V^1$. An element of $V_R$ that pairs to zero with $V_R^\vee$ lies in $V_R \cap V^1 = 0$, so the pairing on $V_R \times V_R^\vee$ is perfect. $\square$

The form $B$ makes $V_R$ a Euclidean space, and by (2) the reflections $s_\alpha$ are orthogonal reflections. Thus $R$ is a root system in $V_R$ in the sense of Chapter 2, and so is $R^\vee$ in $V_R^\vee$. Under the isomorphism $b$, the coroot $\alpha^\vee$ corresponds to $2 \alpha / B(\alpha, \alpha)$. So $R^\vee$ is the *dual root system* of $R$ in the sense of [Hum72], Section 9: the long roots of $R$ give the short coroots, and the short roots give the long coroots.

**Definition 5.1.8 (root lattice and weight lattice).** The *root lattice* is $Q = \mathbb{Z} R \subset X$, and the *weight lattice* is
$$P = \{ x \in V_R : \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ for all } \alpha \in R \}.$$
The *coroot lattice* $Q^\vee = \mathbb{Z} R^\vee$ and the *coweight lattice* $P^\vee \subset V_R^\vee$ are the same lattices for $\Psi^\vee$.

**Proposition 5.1.9 (the lattices of a semisimple root datum).** Let $\Psi$ be semisimple. Then $Q \subset X \subset P$ and $Q^\vee \subset X^\vee \subset P^\vee$, and all six lattices have the same rank. Under the pairing, each lattice in the chain $Q^\vee \subset X^\vee \subset P^\vee$ is the dual of the corresponding lattice in the chain $P \supset X \supset Q$. There are canonical isomorphisms of finite abelian groups
$$X^\vee / Q^\vee \cong \mathrm{Hom}(P / X, \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}), \qquad P^\vee / X^\vee \cong \mathrm{Hom}(X / Q, \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}).$$

*Proof.* Since $\Psi$ is semisimple, $V = V_R$, so $X \subset V_R$, and each $x \in X$ has $\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}$. So $X \subset P$. The inclusion $Q \subset X$ is clear. By Proposition 5.1.7, $\Psi^\vee$ is semisimple, and the same holds for the dual chain. For a lattice $L$ of full rank in $V$ let $L^* = \{ y \in V^\vee : \langle L, y \rangle \subset \mathbb{Z} \}$. By definition $P = (Q^\vee)^*$ and $P^\vee = Q^*$, and $X^* = X^\vee$ because the pairing is perfect. Since $L^{**} = L$, we also get $P^* = Q^\vee$. For full lattices $L \subset M$, the pairing gives an isomorphism $L^* / M^* \cong \mathrm{Hom}(M/L, \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})$: an element $y \in L^*$ defines the character $m \mapsto \langle m, y \rangle \bmod \mathbb{Z}$ of $M/L$, its kernel is $M^*$, and a count of orders (choose bases adapted to $L \subset M$) shows that every character arises. Apply this to $X \subset P$ and to $Q \subset X$. $\square$

Chapter 3 shows that a split reductive group $G$ with root datum $\Psi(G, T)$ is generated by $T$ and the root subgroups $U_\alpha$. The centre $Z(G)$ is contained in $T$, since $C_G(T) = T$. An element $t \in T$ acts on $U_\alpha \cong \mathbb{G}_a$ by multiplication by $\alpha(t)$. So $t$ is central if and only if $\alpha(t) = 1$ for all $\alpha \in R$. Thus $Z(G) = \bigcap_{\alpha \in R} \ker \alpha$, and by Corollary 1.4.6,
$$X^*(Z(G)) = X / Q.$$
The group $\pi_1(G) = X^\vee / Q^\vee$ is the *fundamental group* of $G$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it is the fundamental group of the topological space $G(\mathbb{C})$. A semisimple group $G$ is *simply connected* if $X^\vee = Q^\vee$, equivalently $X = P$. It is *adjoint* if $X = Q$, equivalently $Z(G) = 1$. Proposition 5.1.9 shows that duality exchanges the two notions: if $\Psi$ is the root datum of a semisimple group $G$, and $\Psi^\vee$ the root datum of $G'$, then
$$X^*(Z(G')) = X^\vee / Q^\vee = \pi_1(G), \qquad \pi_1(G') = X / Q = X^*(Z(G)).$$
So $G'$ is adjoint if $G$ is simply connected, and simply connected if $G$ is adjoint.

### Based root data

A *base* of $\Psi$ is a base $\Delta$ of the root system $R$ in $V_R$, that is, a subset that is a basis of $V_R$ such that each root is a combination of $\Delta$ with integer coefficients that are all $\ge 0$ or all $\le 0$. The roots with coefficients $\ge 0$ are the *positive roots* $R^+$. For a split reductive group, the Borel subgroups that contain $T$ correspond to the bases of $\Psi(G, T)$, by Chapter 3: $\Delta$ is the set of simple roots of $T$ on $\mathrm{Lie}(B)$.

**Proposition 5.1.10 (the dual base).** If $\Delta$ is a base of $\Psi$, then $\Delta^\vee = \{ \alpha^\vee : \alpha \in \Delta \}$ is a base of $\Psi^\vee$, and its positive roots are the coroots $\alpha^\vee$ with $\alpha \in R^+$.

*Proof.* We may assume that $\Psi$ is reduced. In general, remove from $R$ the roots $\alpha$ with $\alpha/2 \in R$, which changes neither $\Delta$ nor the chambers below. Use the isomorphism $b : V_R \to V_R^\vee$ of the proof of Proposition 5.1.7. It identifies $\alpha^\vee$ with $2\alpha / B(\alpha, \alpha)$, a positive multiple of $\alpha$. So $b^{-1}(R^\vee)$ is a root system with the same lines as $R$, and with the same reflections. By [Hum72], Section 10, the bases of a root system are the sets of roots that are positive on a Weyl chamber and orthogonal to its walls. The chambers and their walls depend only on the reflecting hyperplanes, which are the same for $R$ and $b^{-1}(R^\vee)$. Let $C$ be the chamber of $\Delta$. The roots of $b^{-1}(R^\vee)$ that are positive on $C$ and orthogonal to its walls are the positive multiples $2\alpha/B(\alpha, \alpha)$ of the elements $\alpha \in \Delta$. So $b^{-1}(\Delta^\vee)$ is a base, and the positive elements are the multiples of the positive roots. $\square$

Mathlib defines a base of a root pairing by conditions on the roots and on the coroots at the same time. So in Mathlib the dual base is a definition with no proof, and the content of Proposition 5.1.10 is that a base of $R$ in the sense of Chapter 2 satisfies the conditions on the coroots.

**Definition 5.1.11 (based root datum).** A *based root datum* is a quintuple, usually written as a quadruple $\Psi_0 = (X, \Delta, X^\vee, \Delta^\vee)$, which is a root datum with a base $\Delta$. Its *dual* is $\Psi_0^\vee = (X^\vee, \Delta^\vee, X, \Delta)$. An automorphism of $\Psi_0$ is an automorphism of $\Psi$ that maps $\Delta$ onto $\Delta$. The group of automorphisms is $\mathrm{Aut}(\Psi_0)$.

**Proposition 5.1.12 (automorphisms of the dual).** The map $f \mapsto f^\vee = (f^{-1})^t$ is an isomorphism of groups $\mathrm{Aut}(\Psi_0) \to \mathrm{Aut}(\Psi_0^\vee)$. The group $\mathrm{Aut}(\Psi_0)$ acts faithfully on $\Delta$ if $\Psi$ is semisimple, and it is then a subgroup of the group of automorphisms of the Dynkin diagram.

*Proof.* Let $f \in \mathrm{Aut}(\Psi)$. By Proposition 5.1.5, the transpose $f^t$ maps $R^\vee$ onto itself, and $f^t((f \alpha)^\vee) = \alpha^\vee$. Indeed, the coroot of $f\alpha$ is determined by Lemma 5.1.2, and $(f^{-1})^t(\alpha^\vee)$ has the defining properties. So $f^\vee(\alpha^\vee) = (f \alpha)^\vee$, and $f^\vee(\Delta^\vee) = \Delta^\vee$ if $f(\Delta) = \Delta$. The map is a homomorphism, and its inverse is the same construction for $\Psi^\vee$. If $\Psi$ is semisimple, $\Delta$ is a basis of $X_{\mathbb{Q}}$, so an automorphism that fixes each element of $\Delta$ is the identity. An automorphism preserves the numbers $\langle \alpha, \beta^\vee \rangle$, so it preserves the Dynkin diagram. $\square$

For a split reductive group, Section 5.2 identifies $\mathrm{Aut}(\Psi_0)$ with the group of outer automorphisms. For $SL_n$ with $n \ge 3$ it has order $2$, and its nontrivial element is $x \mapsto -w_0 x$, where $w_0$ is the longest element of the Weyl group.

**Proposition 5.1.13 (the Cartan matrix of the dual).** Let $\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r\}$ be a base of a reduced root datum $\Psi$, with Cartan matrix $A_{ij} = \langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle$. The Cartan matrix of $\Psi^\vee$ for the base $\Delta^\vee$ is the transpose of $A$. The Dynkin diagram of $\Psi^\vee$ is the Dynkin diagram of $\Psi$ with the arrows reversed.

*Proof.* In $\Psi^\vee$ the coroot of $\alpha_j^\vee$ is $\alpha_j$, so the Cartan matrix is $\langle \alpha_j, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle$ in the place $(i, j)$, that is, $A_{ji}$. The Dynkin diagram joins $i$ and $j$ by $A_{ij} A_{ji}$ edges, which does not change, and the arrow points to the shorter root. By the remark after Proposition 5.1.7, the shorter of $\alpha_i, \alpha_j$ has the longer coroot. $\square$

So the dual of a root system of type $B_n$ has type $C_n$, and the reverse holds. The types $A_n$, $D_n$, $E_6$, $E_7$ and $E_8$ have only one root length, and they are self-dual. The types $G_2$ and $F_4$ are also self-dual, but the isomorphism exchanges the long and the short roots.
