# Algebraic groups

A linear algebraic group is a group of matrices defined by polynomial equations, such as $SL_n$, the orthogonal groups and the symplectic groups. The modern definition looks at all the solutions of the equations at once. An algebraic group $G$ over a field $k$ is a functor that sends each $k$-algebra $R$ to the group $G(R)$ of solutions in $R$, and this functor is represented by a finitely generated $k$-algebra $\mathcal{O}(G)$. The group law becomes a comultiplication on $\mathcal{O}(G)$, so that $\mathcal{O}(G)$ is a Hopf algebra. This point of view has two advantages. It treats groups over number fields and $p$-adic fields in the same way as groups over $\mathbb{C}$, and it keeps the groups such as $\mu_p$ in characteristic $p$, which have only one point over each field but are not trivial.

The chapter builds the structure theory up to the Borel subgroups. Every algebraic group is a closed subgroup of some $GL_n$. Each element $g$ has a Jordan decomposition $g = g_s g_u$ into a semisimple part and a unipotent part, and homomorphisms preserve it. The groups whose elements are all semisimple and commute are the diagonalizable groups. They are classified by their character groups, and the connected ones are the tori. The groups whose elements are all unipotent are the unipotent groups, and Kolchin's theorem puts them into upper triangular form with ones on the diagonal. The theorem of Lie and Kolchin does the same for a connected solvable group, with an arbitrary diagonal. Borel's fixed point theorem then shows that the maximal connected solvable subgroups of a connected group are all conjugate. These are the Borel subgroups. For $GL_n$ they are the stabilizers of the full flags, and the quotient $G/B$ is the flag variety.

Section 1.1 defines affine groups, Hopf algebras and representations. Section 1.2 treats closed subgroups, homomorphisms, kernels, quotients and connected components. Section 1.3 proves the Jordan decomposition. Section 1.4 treats diagonalizable groups and tori, with their characters, cocharacters and the action of the Galois group. Section 1.5 treats unipotent and solvable groups and proves the theorems of Kolchin and of Lie and Kolchin. Section 1.6 proves the fixed point theorem and the conjugacy of Borel subgroups. The examples to keep in mind are $\mathbb{G}_m$, $GL_n$ with its diagonal torus and its upper triangular subgroup, and the real torus $SO_2$.

The chapter follows [Mil17], Chapters 1 to 17, for the language of group schemes, and [Spr98], Chapters 2 to 6, for the classical arguments over an algebraically closed field. The reader needs linear algebra, the Nullstellensatz and the first notions on varieties. Chapter 3 uses all of this chapter for the structure of reductive groups.
