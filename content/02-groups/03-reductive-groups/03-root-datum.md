## Root data

This section defines root data, shows that a root datum contains a root system in the sense of Chapter 2, and proves that a split reductive group gives a reduced root datum. It then reads the center of the group off its root datum. The example to keep in mind is the pair $SL_2$ and $PGL_2$: the two groups have the same root system, and their root data are dual to each other. We follow [Spr98], Chapter 7, and [Mil17], Chapter 21.

### The axioms

**Definition 3.3.1 (root datum).** A *root datum* is a quadruple $\Psi = (X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$, where
1. $X$ and $X^\vee$ are free abelian groups of finite rank, with a perfect pairing $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : X \times X^\vee \to \mathbb{Z}$;
2. $R \subset X$ and $R^\vee \subset X^\vee$ are finite subsets, with a bijection $R \to R^\vee$, $\alpha \mapsto \alpha^\vee$;

such that for each $\alpha \in R$:
- (RD1) $\langle \alpha, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2$;
- (RD2) $s_\alpha(R) = R$ and $s_{\alpha^\vee}(R^\vee) = R^\vee$, where $s_\alpha(x) = x - \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha$ for $x \in X$ and $s_{\alpha^\vee}(y) = y - \langle \alpha, y \rangle \alpha^\vee$ for $y \in X^\vee$.

The root datum is *reduced* if, for each $\alpha \in R$, the only elements of $R \cap \mathbb{Q}\alpha$ are $\alpha$ and $-\alpha$. The *rank* of $\Psi$ is the rank of $X$.

Lean: `RootPairing` and `RootDatum`, from Mathlib

In Mathlib, `RootPairing ι R M N` is a perfect pairing of two modules over a commutative ring with roots and coroots indexed by a type `ι` and permutations of `ι` that record the action of the reflections. The type `RootDatum ι X₁ X₂` is the case of the ring $\mathbb{Z}$. Mathlib does not include the finite generation of $X$ and $X^\vee$ in the definition, and the user adds it as a separate hypothesis. The reduced case is the class `RootPairing.IsReduced`.

**Lemma 3.3.2 (reflections).** Let $\alpha \in R$. Then $s_\alpha(\alpha) = -\alpha$, $s_\alpha^2 = 1$, and $\langle s_\alpha x, y \rangle = \langle x, s_{\alpha^\vee} y \rangle$ for $x \in X$ and $y \in X^\vee$. In particular $R = -R$.

Lean: `RootPairing.reflection`, from Mathlib, with its basic lemmas

*Proof.* By (RD1), $s_\alpha(\alpha) = \alpha - 2\alpha = -\alpha$. Then
$$s_\alpha^2(x) = s_\alpha(x) - \langle s_\alpha(x), \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha = x - \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha - (\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle - 2 \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle) \alpha = x.$$
Both $\langle s_\alpha x, y \rangle$ and $\langle x, s_{\alpha^\vee} y \rangle$ are equal to $\langle x, y \rangle - \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle \langle \alpha, y \rangle$. Finally $-\alpha = s_\alpha(\alpha) \in R$ by (RD2). $\square$

So $s_\alpha$ is a reflection of $X$: an involution that fixes the subgroup $\{x : \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 0\}$ of corank 1 and sends $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$. The map $s_{\alpha^\vee}$ is its transpose.

**Definition 3.3.3 (Weyl group, dual root datum, lattices).** Let $\Psi = (X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$ be a root datum.
1. The *Weyl group* $W(\Psi)$ is the subgroup of $\mathrm{Aut}(X)$ generated by the reflections $s_\alpha$, $\alpha \in R$.
2. The *dual root datum* is $\Psi^\vee = (X^\vee, R^\vee, X, R)$, with the bijection $\alpha^\vee \mapsto \alpha$.
3. The *root lattice* is $Q = \mathbb{Z} R \subset X$, and the *coroot lattice* is $Q^\vee = \mathbb{Z} R^\vee \subset X^\vee$.
4. The root datum is *semisimple* if $Q$ has finite index in $X$. A semisimple root datum is *adjoint* if $Q = X$ and *simply connected* if $Q^\vee = X^\vee$.

Lean: `RootPairing.weylGroup` and `RootPairing.flip`, from Mathlib

The axioms are symmetric in $(X, R)$ and $(X^\vee, R^\vee)$, so $\Psi^\vee$ is a root datum, and $(\Psi^\vee)^\vee = \Psi$. By Lemma 3.3.2, $s_\alpha \mapsto s_{\alpha^\vee}$ is the inverse transpose, so $W(\Psi^\vee)$ is isomorphic to $W(\Psi)$. The dual of an adjoint root datum is simply connected, and conversely. The dual root datum is the starting point of Chapter 5: the dual group of $G$ is the complex reductive group whose root datum is the dual of the root datum of $G$.

### The root system of a root datum

The next proposition connects root data with the root systems of Chapter 2.

**Proposition 3.3.4 (the root system of a root datum).** Let $\Psi = (X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$ be a root datum. Let $V \subset X_\mathbb{R} = X \otimes \mathbb{R}$ be the span of $R$, $V^\vee \subset X^\vee_\mathbb{R}$ the span of $R^\vee$, and $K = \{x \in X_\mathbb{R} : \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 0 \text{ for all } \alpha \in R\}$.
1. The form $(x, y) = \sum_{\beta \in R} \langle x, \beta^\vee \rangle \langle y, \beta^\vee \rangle$ on $X_\mathbb{R}$ is invariant under $W(\Psi)$, and $\langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2(x, \alpha)/(\alpha, \alpha)$ for $x \in X_\mathbb{R}$ and $\alpha \in R$.
2. $\dim V = \dim V^\vee$, $X_\mathbb{R} = V \oplus K$, and the form is positive definite on $V$.
3. If $\Psi$ is reduced, $R$ is a root system in $V$, and each $s_\alpha$ acts on $V$ as the orthogonal reflection in $\alpha$.
4. The group $W(\Psi)$ is finite. It acts trivially on $K$, and restriction to $V$ is an isomorphism of $W(\Psi)$ onto the Weyl group of the root system $R$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* For 1, let $\alpha \in R$. By Lemma 3.3.2, $\langle s_\alpha x, \beta^\vee \rangle = \langle x, s_{\alpha^\vee} \beta^\vee \rangle$, and $s_{\alpha^\vee}$ permutes $R^\vee$ by (RD2). So $(s_\alpha x, s_\alpha y) = (x, y)$. Then $(s_\alpha x, \alpha) = (s_\alpha x, -s_\alpha \alpha) = -(x, \alpha)$. On the other hand $(s_\alpha x, \alpha) = (x, \alpha) - \langle x, \alpha^\vee \rangle (\alpha, \alpha)$. Since $(\alpha, \alpha) \ge \langle \alpha, \alpha^\vee \rangle^2 = 4$, the formula follows.

For 2, define $\phi : X_\mathbb{R} \to X^\vee_\mathbb{R}$ by $\phi(x) = \sum_\beta \langle x, \beta^\vee \rangle \beta^\vee$, so that $\langle y, \phi(x) \rangle = (x, y)$. By 1, $\langle y, \phi(\alpha) \rangle = \frac{1}{2}(\alpha, \alpha) \langle y, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ for all $y$, so $\phi(\alpha) = \frac{1}{2} (\alpha, \alpha) \alpha^\vee$. Thus $\phi(V) = V^\vee$ and $\dim V^\vee \le \dim V$. The same argument for $\Psi^\vee$ gives $\dim V \le \dim V^\vee$. So $\phi$ restricts to an isomorphism $V \to V^\vee$. If $v \in V \cap K$, then $\phi(v) = 0$, so $v = 0$. The subspace $K$ is the annihilator of $V^\vee$, so $\dim K = \dim X_\mathbb{R} - \dim V$, and $X_\mathbb{R} = V \oplus K$. For $v \in V$ nonzero, $\phi(v) \ne 0$, so some $\langle v, \beta^\vee \rangle \ne 0$, and $(v, v) > 0$.

For 3, the set $R$ is finite, spans $V$ and does not contain 0. By 1, $s_\alpha(x) = x - 2\frac{(x, \alpha)}{(\alpha, \alpha)} \alpha$, the orthogonal reflection in $\alpha$, and it maps $R$ to $R$. The numbers $2(\beta, \alpha)/(\alpha, \alpha) = \langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ are integers. With the reducedness, these are the axioms of a root system in Chapter 2.

For 4, each $s_\alpha$ is the identity on $K$ and preserves $V$. An element $w \in W(\Psi)$ that fixes every root is the identity on $V$ and on $K$, so $w = 1$. Thus $W(\Psi)$ embeds into the finite group of permutations of $R$. Restriction to $V$ is injective for the same reason, and its image is the group generated by the orthogonal reflections $s_\alpha$, which is the Weyl group of $R$. $\square$

A root datum is semisimple if and only if $K = 0$, that is, if and only if $R$ spans $X_\mathbb{Q}$. If $\Psi$ is not reduced, $R$ is a root system in the wider sense of Bourbaki, in which $\alpha$ and $2\alpha$ may both be roots. Such root systems appear as relative root systems of nonsplit groups, but not as root systems of split groups.

### The root datum of a split reductive group

**Theorem 3.3.5 (the root datum of $(G, T)$).** Let $(G, T)$ be a split reductive group, with roots $R$ and coroots $R^\vee$ as in Theorem 3.2.5. Then
$$\Psi(G, T) = (X^*(T), R, X_*(T), R^\vee),$$
with the bijection $\alpha \mapsto \alpha^\vee$, is a reduced root datum.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* The pairing between $X^*(T)$ and $X_*(T)$ is perfect, and $\langle \alpha, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2$ by Theorem 3.2.5. The reflection $s_\alpha$ is induced by $n_\alpha \in N_G(T)(k)$, and $N_G(T)(k)$ permutes the roots, so $s_\alpha(R) = R$. Let $\beta \in R$. Composing $\varphi_\beta$ with conjugation by $n_\alpha$ gives a homomorphism $SL_2 \to G$ with the properties of Theorem 3.2.5.3 for the root $s_\alpha(\beta)$. By the uniqueness of the coroot, $(s_\alpha \beta)^\vee = n_\alpha \beta^\vee n_\alpha^{-1} = s_{\alpha^\vee}(\beta^\vee)$, so $s_{\alpha^\vee}(R^\vee) = R^\vee$. The map $\alpha \mapsto \alpha^\vee$ is injective. Indeed, if $\alpha^\vee = \beta^\vee = c$, then $s_\alpha s_\beta(x) = x + \langle x, c \rangle (\alpha - \beta)$, and since $\langle \alpha - \beta, c \rangle = 0$, this map has infinite order unless $\alpha = \beta$. But $s_\alpha s_\beta$ lies in the image of the finite group $N_G(T)/T$. The root datum is reduced by Theorem 3.2.5.1. $\square$

We call a root datum *of* $G$ any root datum $\Psi(G, T)$. Up to isomorphism it does not depend on $T$. Over $\bar k$ this follows from the conjugacy of maximal tori, and over $k$ from a theorem of Borel and Tits: two maximal split tori of $G$ are conjugate under $G(k)$ ([Spr98], Chapter 15).

**Example 3.3.6 (tori and groups of rank one).** The root datum of a split torus $T$ is $(X^*(T), \emptyset, X_*(T), \emptyset)$. By Examples 3.2.3 and 3.2.6,
$$\Psi(SL_2) = (\mathbb{Z}\chi, \{\pm 2\chi\}, \mathbb{Z}\chi^\vee, \{\pm \chi^\vee\}), \qquad \Psi(PGL_2) = (\mathbb{Z}\alpha, \{\pm\alpha\}, \mathbb{Z}\lambda, \{\pm 2\lambda\}),$$
with $\langle \chi, \chi^\vee \rangle = 1$ and $\langle \alpha, \lambda \rangle = 1$. The first is simply connected and not adjoint, and the second is adjoint and not simply connected. The map $\chi^\vee \mapsto \alpha$, $\chi \mapsto \lambda$ is an isomorphism $\Psi(SL_2)^\vee \cong \Psi(PGL_2)$. So the dual group of $SL_2$ is $PGL_2(\mathbb{C})$ and the dual group of $PGL_2$ is $SL_2(\mathbb{C})$, a fact that Chapter 5 and Volume 6 use often.

### The center

**Proposition 3.3.7 (the center).** Let $(G, T)$ be a split reductive group with root datum $(X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$. Then $Z(G) = \bigcap_{\alpha \in R} \ker \alpha$. It is a diagonalizable group with character group $X / \mathbb{Z}R$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* An element of $Z(G)$ centralizes $T$, so $Z(G) \subset C_G(T) = T$ by Theorem 3.1.10. An element $t \in T$ is central if and only if conjugation by $t$ is trivial on $T$ and on each $U_\alpha$, because these subgroups generate $G$ by Theorem 3.2.5.5. Conjugation by $t$ sends $u_\alpha(x)$ to $u_\alpha(\alpha(t) x)$, so it is trivial on $U_\alpha$ if and only if $\alpha(t) = 1$. The same argument works for points with values in any $k$-algebra, so $Z(G) = \bigcap_\alpha \ker \alpha$ as group schemes. By the duality between diagonalizable groups and finitely generated abelian groups (Chapter 1), the closed subgroup $\bigcap_{\chi \in M} \ker \chi$ of $T$ has character group $X/M$ for each subgroup $M \subset X$. Take $M = \mathbb{Z}R$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.3.8 (semisimple and adjoint groups).** A split reductive group $G$ is semisimple if and only if its root datum is semisimple. It is semisimple with trivial center if and only if its root datum is adjoint.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* By Theorem 3.1.6, $G$ is semisimple if and only if its largest central torus is trivial, that is, if and only if $Z(G)$ is finite. By Proposition 3.3.7 this holds if and only if $X/\mathbb{Z}R$ is finite. The center is trivial if and only if $X = \mathbb{Z}R$. $\square$

We call a split semisimple group *adjoint* or *simply connected* if its root datum is adjoint or simply connected. The *fundamental group* of a split semisimple group is $\pi_1(G) = X^\vee / \mathbb{Z}R^\vee$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it is the topological fundamental group of $G(\mathbb{C})$, which explains the name. By Proposition 3.3.7 applied to the dual root datum, $\pi_1(G)$ is the character group of the center of the dual group. This exchange of the center and the fundamental group is the first trace of duality in the Langlands program.

**Example 3.3.9 (three groups with the root system $A_1$ in rank 2).** Consider the root data of $GL_2$, of $SL_2 \times \mathbb{G}_m$ and of $PGL_2 \times \mathbb{G}_m$. All three have rank 2 and the root system $\{\pm\alpha\}$ of type $A_1$. For $GL_2$, $X = \mathbb{Z}e_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z}e_2$, $\alpha = e_1 - e_2$ and $\alpha^\vee = e_1^* - e_2^*$. Both $\alpha$ and $\alpha^\vee$ are primitive, that is, not divisible by an integer $m \ge 2$. For $SL_2 \times \mathbb{G}_m$ the root $\alpha = 2\chi$ is divisible by 2. For $PGL_2 \times \mathbb{G}_m$ the coroot $\alpha^\vee = 2\lambda$ is divisible by 2. Divisibility is preserved by isomorphisms, so the three root data are pairwise non-isomorphic. Their centers are $\mathbb{G}_m$, $\mu_2 \times \mathbb{G}_m$ and $\mathbb{G}_m$, by Proposition 3.3.7. Exercise 3 shows that these are all the reduced root data of rank 2 with a root system of type $A_1$, and that $GL_2 \cong (SL_2 \times \mathbb{G}_m)/\mu_2$.
