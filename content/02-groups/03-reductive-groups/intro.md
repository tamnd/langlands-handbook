# Reductive groups

A reductive group is a connected algebraic group with no connected unipotent normal subgroup other than the trivial one. The groups $GL_n$, $SL_n$, $PGL_n$, the symplectic groups and the special orthogonal groups are reductive, and so is every torus. These are the groups of the Langlands program. An automorphic representation is a representation of $G(\mathbb{A}_F)$ for a reductive group $G$ over a number field $F$, and the local correspondence of Volume 6 is a statement about the representations of $G(K)$ for a reductive group $G$ over a local field $K$.

The structure of a reductive group is controlled by a small amount of linear algebra. Fix a split maximal torus $T$ of $G$. The group $T$ acts on the Lie algebra of $G$, and the nonzero weights of this action are the roots. Each root $\alpha$ comes with a copy of $SL_2$ or $PGL_2$ in $G$, and the diagonal torus of that copy gives a cocharacter $\alpha^\vee$ of $T$, the coroot. The four objects (the character group of $T$, the roots, the cocharacter group of $T$, the coroots) form the root datum of $(G, T)$. The main theorem of the chapter says that the root datum determines the split group up to isomorphism, and that each reduced root datum comes from a split group. The theorem was proved by Chevalley over an algebraically closed field and extended by Demazure and Grothendieck to all bases.

The root datum is the object the Langlands program needs. Exchange the characters with the cocharacters and the roots with the coroots. The result is again a root datum, and the reductive group over $\mathbb{C}$ with this root datum is the dual group $G^\vee$ of Chapter 5. So $GL_n$ is its own dual, and $SL_n$ and $PGL_n$ are dual to each other. For a group that is not split, the Galois group of the field acts on the based root datum, and this action, with the dual group, gives the L-group.

Section 3.1 defines reductive and semisimple groups and gives their first properties. Section 3.2 constructs the roots, the root subgroups and the coroots of a split reductive group. Section 3.3 defines root data, shows that a split reductive group has one, and reads the center off the root datum. Section 3.4 relates the Borel subgroups that contain $T$ to the positive systems of roots, and it identifies the Weyl group $N_G(T)/T$ with the Weyl group of the root datum. Section 3.5 proves the Bruhat decomposition, first for $GL_n$ by row reduction and then for a group with a Tits system. Section 3.6 states the isomorphism and existence theorems and describes the automorphism group of a split group. Section 3.7 computes the root data of the classical groups. Section 3.8 treats forms of a split group, quasi-split groups and the action of the Galois group on the based root datum.

The chapter follows Milne, *Algebraic Groups: The Theory of Group Schemes of Finite Type over a Field*, Cambridge Studies in Advanced Mathematics 170 (Cambridge, 2017), Chapters 19 to 23. The handbook cites it as [Mil17]. A second source is Springer, *Linear Algebraic Groups*, second edition (Birkhäuser, 1998), Chapters 7 to 10 and 16, cited as [Spr98]. Springer works over an algebraically closed field first and then over an arbitrary field, and his proofs of the isomorphism and existence theorems are complete. For the Galois action on the based root datum we follow Borel, "Automorphic L-functions", in the proceedings of the Corvallis conference (Proc. Sympos. Pure Math. 33, part 2, 1979), Section 1, cited as [Bor79]. The reader needs Chapter 1 (tori, solvable groups, Borel subgroups) and the part of Chapter 2 on root systems and Weyl groups.

The notation is the following. The letter $k$ denotes a field, $\bar k$ an algebraic closure and $k_s$ the separable closure of $k$ in $\bar k$, with Galois group $\Gamma = \mathrm{Gal}(k_s / k)$. An algebraic group over $k$ is an affine group scheme of finite type over $k$, as in Chapter 1. For a torus $T$ we write $X^*(T) = \mathrm{Hom}(T_{k_s}, \mathbb{G}_m)$ for its character group and $X_*(T) = \mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{G}_m, T_{k_s})$ for its cocharacter group. We write both groups additively. The pairing $\langle \chi, \lambda \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}$ is defined by $\chi(\lambda(t)) = t^{\langle \chi, \lambda \rangle}$. It is a perfect pairing.
