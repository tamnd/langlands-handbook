## Borel subgroups and the Weyl group

This section relates the Borel subgroups of $G$ that contain $T$ to the positive systems of the root system, and it proves that the Weyl group $N_G(T)/T$ is the Weyl group of the root datum. It ends with the big cell. The example to keep in mind is $GL_n$, where the Borel subgroups that contain the diagonal torus are the stabilizers of the flags of coordinate subspaces, and they correspond to the orderings of the standard basis. We follow [Mil17], Chapter 21, and [Spr98], Chapters 7 and 8.

Throughout the section, $(G, T)$ is a split reductive group with root datum $\Psi = (X, R, X^\vee, R^\vee)$.

### The Weyl group

**Definition 3.4.1 (Weyl group of $(G, T)$).** The *Weyl group* of $(G, T)$ is the group $W(G, T) = N_G(T)(\bar k) / T(\bar k)$.

The group $N_G(T)(\bar k)$ acts on $T$ by conjugation, and so on $X$ and on $X^\vee$. By Theorem 3.1.10, $C_G(T) = T$, so the action of $W(G, T)$ on $X$ is faithful. The group $W(G, T)$ is finite, because the automorphism group of $T$ is discrete and $N_G(T)^0$ centralizes $T$ (Chapter 1). By Theorem 3.2.5, the elements $n_\alpha \in N_G(T)(k)$ act on $X$ by the reflections $s_\alpha$. So $W(\Psi) \subset W(G, T)$, where we view both as subgroups of $\mathrm{Aut}(X)$. Theorem 3.4.5 below proves equality.

### Positive systems and Borel subgroups

We recall the language of Chapter 2. A cocharacter $\lambda \in X^\vee$ is *regular* if $\langle \alpha, \lambda \rangle \ne 0$ for all $\alpha \in R$.

**Definition 3.4.2 (positive system and base).** A *positive system* of $R$ is a subset of the form $R^+ = \{\alpha \in R : \langle \alpha, \lambda \rangle > 0\}$ for a regular cocharacter $\lambda$. A root $\alpha \in R^+$ is *simple* if it is not the sum of two elements of $R^+$. The set $\Delta$ of simple roots is the *base* of $R^+$.

By Chapter 2, applied to the root system $R$ of Proposition 3.3.4, each root is a combination of the elements of $\Delta$ with integer coefficients that are all $\ge 0$ or all $\le 0$, and $\Delta$ is a basis of the span of $R$. The Weyl group acts simply transitively on the set of positive systems and is generated by the reflections $s_\alpha$ with $\alpha \in \Delta$. Chapter 2 uses regular elements of $X^\vee_\mathbb{R}$, but a small change makes such an element rational, and a multiple of it then lies in $X^\vee$. So the two definitions agree.

For a cocharacter $\lambda$ of $T$, let $P(\lambda)$ be the subgroup of $G$ of the elements $g$ for which the limit of $\lambda(t) g \lambda(t)^{-1}$ as $t \to 0$ exists, that is, for which the morphism $t \mapsto \lambda(t) g \lambda(t)^{-1}$ from $\mathbb{G}_m$ to $G$ extends to $\mathbb{A}^1$. This construction is the dynamic method of [Mil17], Chapter 13.

**Theorem 3.4.3 (Borel subgroups containing $T$).** Let $(G, T)$ be a split reductive group.
1. For a Borel subgroup $B \supset T$ of $G_{\bar k}$, the set $R(B) = \{\alpha \in R : \mathfrak{g}_\alpha \subset \mathrm{Lie}(B)\}$ is a positive system, and $\mathrm{Lie}(B) = \mathfrak{t} \oplus \bigoplus_{\alpha \in R(B)} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$.
2. The map $B \mapsto R(B)$ is a bijection from the set of Borel subgroups of $G_{\bar k}$ that contain $T$ onto the set of positive systems of $R$. The inverse sends $R^+$ to the subgroup generated by $T$ and the $U_\alpha$ with $\alpha \in R^+$. If $R^+$ is defined by the regular cocharacter $\lambda$, this subgroup is $P(\lambda)$. In particular each such $B$ is defined over $k$.
3. Let $U$ be the unipotent radical of $B$. Then $B = T \ltimes U$, and for each ordering of $R(B)$ the product map $\prod_{\alpha \in R(B)} U_\alpha \to U$ is an isomorphism of varieties.

*Proof sketch.* Let $\lambda$ be a regular cocharacter and $R^+$ its positive system. By the dynamic method, $P(\lambda)$ is a smooth connected subgroup with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{t} \oplus \bigoplus_{\langle \alpha, \lambda \rangle > 0} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$. It is the semidirect product of the centralizer of $\lambda$, which is $T$ because $\lambda$ is regular, and of the unipotent group $U(\lambda)$ of the $g$ with limit 1. So $P(\lambda)$ is connected and solvable, and $G/P(\lambda)$ is projective ([Mil17], Chapter 13). Thus $P(\lambda)$ is a Borel subgroup. Conversely, let $B \supset T$ be a Borel subgroup. If $\alpha$ and $-\alpha$ were both in $R(B)$, then $B$ would contain $U_\alpha$ and $U_{-\alpha}$, hence the image of $\varphi_\alpha$, which is not solvable. So $R(B) \cap -R(B) = \emptyset$. All Borel subgroups are conjugate (Chapter 1), so they have the same dimension $r + |R|/2$, and $R(B) \cup -R(B) = R$. It remains to show that $R(B)$ is the positive system of a regular cocharacter, and to prove part 3. For these steps see [Mil17], Chapter 21, and [Spr98], Chapter 8. $\square$

**Corollary 3.4.4 (the Weyl group permutes the Borel subgroups).** The group $W(G, T)$ acts simply transitively on the set of Borel subgroups of $G_{\bar k}$ that contain $T$.

*Proof.* We work over $\bar k$. Let $B$ and $B'$ be Borel subgroups that contain $T$. By the conjugacy of Borel subgroups, $B' = g B g^{-1}$ for some $g \in G(\bar k)$. Then $T$ and $g T g^{-1}$ are maximal tori of $B'$, so they are conjugate under $B'(\bar k)$: $g T g^{-1} = b T b^{-1}$ with $b \in B'(\bar k)$. Put $n = b^{-1} g$. Then $n$ normalizes $T$, and $n B n^{-1} = b^{-1} B' b = B'$. This proves transitivity. Let $n \in N_G(T)(\bar k)$ with $n B n^{-1} = B$. By the normalizer theorem (Chapter 1), $N_G(B) = B$, so $n \in N_B(T)$. In the connected solvable group $B$ the normalizer and the centralizer of a maximal torus are equal (Chapter 1), so $n \in C_B(T) = T$. Thus the stabilizer of $B$ in $W(G, T)$ is trivial. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4.5 (the two Weyl groups).** The action of $N_G(T)$ on $X$ identifies $W(G, T)$ with $W(\Psi)$. Every element of $W(G, T)$ has a representative in $N_G(T)(k)$, namely a product of elements $n_\alpha$ with $\alpha \in \Delta$.

*Proof.* The map $B \mapsto R(B)$ of Theorem 3.4.3 is compatible with the actions of $N_G(T)(\bar k)$, because $\mathrm{Lie}(n B n^{-1}) = \mathrm{Ad}(n) \mathrm{Lie}(B)$. By Corollary 3.4.4 and Theorem 3.4.3, $W(G, T)$ acts simply transitively on the set of positive systems, so $|W(G, T)|$ is the number of positive systems. By Chapter 2, $W(\Psi)$ also acts simply transitively on this set. So the subgroup $W(\Psi)$ of $W(G, T)$ has the same order, and the two groups are equal. The group $W(\Psi)$ is generated by the $s_\alpha$ with $\alpha \in \Delta$, and $s_\alpha$ is the image of $n_\alpha \in N_G(T)(k)$. $\square$

The group $N_G(T)(k)$ is an extension of $W$ by $T(k)$, but in general the extension does not split. For $SL_2$ the element $n = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has $n^2 = -1$, and when $-1 \ne 1$ in $k$ no element of $nT(k)$ has order 2, because $(nt)^2 = n t n^{-1} \cdot n^2 \cdot t = t^{-1} \cdot (-1) \cdot t = -1$ for $t \in T(k)$.

**Example 3.4.6 (Borel subgroups of $GL_n$ containing $T$).** Let $G = GL_n$ and $T$ the diagonal torus. The normalizer of $T$ is the group of monomial matrices, those with one nonzero entry in each row and each column, and $W(G, T) = S_n$, acting on $X = \mathbb{Z}^n$ by permuting the $e_i$. The reflection $s_{e_i - e_j}$ is the transposition $(i \, j)$. Let $\lambda(t) = \mathrm{diag}(t^{n-1}, t^{n-2}, \dots, t, 1)$. Then $\lambda(t) g \lambda(t)^{-1}$ has the entries $t^{j - i} g_{ij}$, and the limit as $t \to 0$ exists if and only if $g_{ij} = 0$ for $i > j$. So $P(\lambda)$ is the group $B_n$ of upper triangular matrices, with positive system $R^+ = \{e_i - e_j : i < j\}$ and base $\Delta = \{e_1 - e_2, \dots, e_{n-1} - e_n\}$. For $\sigma \in S_n$, the Borel subgroup $\sigma B_n \sigma^{-1}$ is the stabilizer of the flag
$$\langle e_{\sigma(1)} \rangle \subset \langle e_{\sigma(1)}, e_{\sigma(2)} \rangle \subset \dots \subset \langle e_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, e_{\sigma(n)} \rangle,$$
and its positive system is $\{e_{\sigma(i)} - e_{\sigma(j)} : i < j\}$. So there are $n!$ Borel subgroups that contain $T$, one for each ordering of the standard basis.

### Length and the big cell

**Definition 3.4.7 (length and the longest element).** Fix a positive system $R^+$ with base $\Delta$. The *length* $\ell(w)$ of $w \in W$ is the number of $\alpha \in R^+$ with $w\alpha \in -R^+$. The *longest element* $w_0$ is the unique element with $w_0 R^+ = -R^+$.

By Chapter 2, $\ell(w)$ is also the smallest number of simple reflections whose product is $w$, and $\ell(w_0) = |R^+|$. For $GL_n$, $\ell(\sigma)$ is the number of inversions of the permutation $\sigma$, and $w_0$ is the permutation $i \mapsto n + 1 - i$, with $\ell(w_0) = n(n-1)/2$. The Borel subgroup $B^- = w_0 B w_0^{-1}$ has the positive system $-R^+$. It is the *opposite* Borel subgroup. For $GL_n$ it is the group of lower triangular matrices.

**Proposition 3.4.8 (the big cell).** Let $B = T U$ be a Borel subgroup containing $T$, with positive system $R^+$, and let $B^- = T U^-$ be the opposite Borel subgroup. Then $B \cap B^- = T$, and the product map
$$U^- \times T \times U \to G$$
is an open immersion. Its image $\Omega$ is a dense open subset of $G$, the *big cell*. In particular $\dim U = |R^+|$, $\dim G = r + 2|R^+|$ and $\dim G/B = |R^+|$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof sketch.* The intersection $B \cap B^-$ is a closed subgroup that contains $T$, and its Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{t}$ by Theorem 3.4.3, so $(B \cap B^-)^0 = T$. That $B \cap B^- = T$ and that the product map is an open immersion are proved in [Mil17], Chapter 21, and [Spr98], Chapter 8. The differential of the product map at the identity is the isomorphism $\mathrm{Lie}(U^-) \oplus \mathfrak{t} \oplus \mathrm{Lie}(U) \to \mathfrak{g}$, so the image contains an open neighbourhood of 1. $\square$

**Example 3.4.9 (the big cell of $GL_n$).** For $g \in GL_n(k)$ let $d_m(g)$ be the determinant of the upper left $m \times m$ block of $g$. We claim that $g \in U^- T U$ if and only if $d_m(g) \ne 0$ for $1 \le m \le n$, and that the decomposition is then unique, with $t = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, d_2/d_1, \dots, d_n/d_{n-1})$. This is the $LDU$ decomposition of numerical linear algebra.

If $g = u^- t u$, then the upper left $m \times m$ block of $g$ is the product of the corresponding blocks of $u^-$, $t$ and $u$, because $u^-$ is lower triangular and $u$ is upper triangular. So $d_m(g) = t_1 \cdots t_m \ne 0$. Conversely, assume that all $d_m(g) \ne 0$, and argue by induction on $n$. Write $g = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} A & b \\ c & d \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $A$ of size $n-1$. By induction $A = L D V$ with $L \in U^-$, $D$ diagonal and $V \in U$. Then
$$g = \begin{pmatrix} L & 0 \\ c V^{-1} D^{-1} & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} D & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} V & D^{-1} L^{-1} b \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad s = d - c A^{-1} b,$$
as one checks by multiplying out, and $s = \det g / \det A \ne 0$. For uniqueness, $U^- \cap B = 1$, since a lower unitriangular matrix that is upper triangular is the identity. If $u_1^- t_1 u_1 = u_2^- t_2 u_2$, then $(u_2^-)^{-1} u_1^- = t_2 u_2 u_1^{-1} t_1^{-1}$ lies in $U^- \cap B$, so $u_1^- = u_2^-$, and then $t_1 = t_2$ and $u_1 = u_2$. Thus the big cell of $GL_n$ is the open set $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_{n-1} \ne 0$. Its complement is the union of the $n - 1$ hypersurfaces $d_m = 0$, which correspond to the simple roots.
