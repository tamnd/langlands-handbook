## The case n = 1 and the tori

For $n = 1$ the local Langlands correspondence is local class field theory. This section recalls the reciprocity map from Volume 3, shows that it gives a bijection $\mathcal{A}_1(K) \to \mathcal{G}_1(K)$, and checks that the bijection matches the conductors and the local factors. It then treats split tori, the first groups other than $GL_n$ for which the correspondence is a theorem. The last subsection proves the case $n = 1$ in full: it identifies $\mathcal{A}_1(K)$ with the smooth characters by Schur's lemma, checks each condition of Theorem 3.1.2, and treats the trivial case $n = 0$ that the Lean statement also asks for. It follows the Lean file `Langlands/Local/GL1.lean`, which proves all of this except one input from Tate's thesis. The example to keep in mind is the quadratic character $\kappa_{E/K}$ of $K^\times$ attached to a quadratic extension $E/K$. It corresponds to the character of $W_K$ with kernel $W_E$, and it appears in all the later sections.

### Local reciprocity

**Theorem 3.2.1 (local class field theory).** There is a unique isomorphism of topological groups $\mathrm{art}_K : K^\times \to W_K^{ab}$ with these properties.
1. For each uniformizer $\varpi$ of $K$, $\mathrm{art}_K(\varpi)$ is the class of a geometric Frobenius element.
2. For each finite abelian extension $L/K$ inside a fixed algebraic closure, the composite of $\mathrm{art}_K$ with the restriction $W_K^{ab} \to \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$ is surjective, and its kernel is the norm group $N_{L/K}(L^\times)$.

This is Volume 3, Theorem 2.3.3, where the map is called $\mathrm{rec}_K$. The proof is in Volume 3, Chapters 2 and 3, with Lubin-Tate theory for the existence, and also in [Ser67] and [Mil20], Chapter I. The Lean statement `Langlands.LocalReciprocityGL1` says that for each nonarchimedean local field $K$ there is exactly one topological isomorphism $K^\times \to W_K^{ab}$ with the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap`, which are properties 1 and 2. The Lean project proves it as the theorem `Langlands.localReciprocityGL1`. The image $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ is the image of the inertia group $I_K$ in $W_K^{ab}$, and $\mathrm{art}_K(1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m)$ is the image of the upper numbering ramification group $I_K^m$ for $m \ge 1$, see [Ser79].

**Corollary 3.2.2 (the correspondence for $GL_1$).** The map $\chi \mapsto \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$ is a bijection from the set of smooth characters of $K^\times$ to $\mathcal{G}_1(K)$. It satisfies conditions 1 to 4 of Theorem 3.1.2 for $n = 1$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.wdOfChar`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.charOfWD`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.wdEquiv`. The bijection is `wdEquiv`. It sends $\chi$ to the class of $(\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}, 0)$, which is `wdOfChar`, and its inverse sends $(r, N)$ to $\det r \circ \mathrm{art}_K$, which is `charOfWD`. The conditions are Theorem 3.2.11 below.

*Proof.* A Weil-Deligne representation $(r, N)$ of dimension 1 has $N = 0$, because a nilpotent endomorphism of a line is 0. It is Frobenius semisimple, because each element of $GL_1(\mathbb{C})$ is semisimple. So $\mathcal{G}_1(K)$ is the set of continuous characters of $W_K$. Each of them factors through $W_K^{ab}$, because $\mathbb{C}^\times$ is commutative and the closure of the commutator subgroup is in the kernel. So $\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ \mathrm{art}_K$ is a bijection from $\mathcal{G}_1(K)$ to the continuous characters of $K^\times$. It remains to see that each continuous character $\chi$ of $K^\times$ is smooth, that is, has open kernel. Let $U = \{z \in \mathbb{C}^\times : |z - 1| < 1\}$. The only subgroup $H$ of $\mathbb{C}^\times$ inside $U$ is $\{1\}$. Indeed, let $z \in H$. If $|z| \ne 1$, then $z$ or $z^{-1}$ has absolute value greater than 1, and its powers, which are in $H$, leave every bounded set. If $z = e^{i\theta}$ with $0 < |\theta| \le \pi$, then some multiple $m\theta$ lies in $[\pi/3, 5\pi/3]$ modulo $2\pi$, because the multiples move by steps of length $|\theta|$, and then $|z^m - 1| \ge 1$. Since $\chi$ is continuous, $\chi^{-1}(U)$ contains one of the open subgroups $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$, and $\chi(1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m)$ is a subgroup in $U$. So $\chi$ is trivial on $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$. Conversely a smooth character is continuous. Here we identify $\mathcal{A}_1(K)$ with the set of smooth characters of $K^\times$, which Corollary 3.2.10 below justifies. Conditions 1 and 3 hold by construction, and condition 4 holds since $(\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1})^{-1} = \chi^{-1} \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$. Condition 2 is condition 1, because the central character of $\chi$ is $\chi$. $\square$

This is Volume 3, Theorem 4.2.3, written from the Galois side there. The last sentence of the proof is the Lean theorem `Langlands.charRep_hasCentralChar`: the central character of the representation $\chi \circ \det$ of $GL_1(K)$ is $\chi$.

### Conductors and local factors

Let $\chi$ be a smooth character of $K^\times$ and $\varphi = \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$. The *conductor exponent* $a(\chi)$ is 0 if $\chi$ is unramified, that is trivial on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$, and otherwise the least $m \ge 1$ with $\chi$ trivial on $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$. The Artin conductor $a(\varphi)$ is defined in Volume 3, Definition 4.2.1.

**Proposition 3.2.3 (the factors match for $n = 1$).** Let $\chi$ and $\varphi$ be as above.
1. $a(\chi) = a(\varphi)$.
2. $L(s, \chi) = L(s, \varphi)$, where the left side is Tate's factor and the right side is the factor of Chapter 2.
3. $\varepsilon(s, \chi, \psi) = \varepsilon(s, \varphi, \psi)$ for each nontrivial character $\psi$ of $K$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.lFactor_wdOfChar_eq`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.charLFactor_eq_lFactor_of_iff`. These two theorems give part 2, with a hypothesis that Remark 3.2.12 explains. Tate's factor is `Langlands.LocalGL1.charLFactor`. Parts 1 and 3 are not stated in Lean.

*Proof.* Part 1 is Volume 3, Theorem 4.2.3, from the description of the images of the ramification groups after Theorem 3.2.1. For part 2, the space of $\varphi$ is the line $\mathbb{C}$, and $N = 0$. If $\chi$ is unramified, then $\varphi$ is trivial on $I_K$, because $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ is the image of $I_K$. So $V^{I_K} = V$, and $\Phi$ acts on it by $\varphi(\Phi) = \chi(\varpi)$, because $\mathrm{art}_K(\varpi)$ is the class of $\Phi$ by property 1 of Theorem 3.2.1. Thus $L(s, \varphi) = (1 - \chi(\varpi) q^{-s})^{-1}$, which is Tate's factor. If $\chi$ is ramified, then $\varphi$ is not trivial on $I_K$, so $V^{I_K} = 0$ and both factors are 1. Part 3 holds by definition: the theorem of Langlands and Deligne, Volume 3, Theorem 4.3.8, defines the $\varepsilon$-factors of characters of $W_K$ as Tate's factors of the corresponding characters of $K^\times$. $\square$

Volume 3 writes $L(\chi, s)$ and $\varepsilon(\chi, s, \psi)$. We put $s$ first, as is usual for $GL_n$. The proof of part 2 is where the geometric normalization matters, see Volume 3, Remark 4.3.7. With the arithmetic normalization, $\varphi(\Phi)$ would be $\chi(\varpi)^{-1}$, and the two $L$-factors would differ.

**Example 3.2.4 (quadratic characters).** Let $E/K$ be a separable quadratic extension, and let $\kappa_{E/K}$ be the character of $K^\times$ with kernel $N_{E/K}(E^\times)$. By Theorem 3.2.1 the norm group has index 2, so $\kappa_{E/K}$ is a character of order 2, and $\kappa_{E/K} \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$ is the character of $W_K$ with kernel $W_E$. If $E/K$ is unramified, the norm group is $\mathcal{O}_K^\times \varpi^{2\mathbb{Z}}$, so $\kappa_{E/K}(x) = (-1)^{v_K(x)}$. This is the unramified quadratic character, and $L(s, \kappa_{E/K}) = (1 + q^{-s})^{-1}$. If $p \ne 2$ and $E = K(\sqrt{\varpi})$, the norm group contains $-\varpi = N(\sqrt{\varpi})$ and the squares of $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$. So $\kappa_{E/K}$ is the character with $\kappa_{E/K}(-\varpi) = 1$ whose restriction to $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ is the Legendre symbol of the residue field, and $a(\kappa_{E/K}) = 1$. For $p \ne 2$ there are exactly three quadratic extensions, because $K^\times / K^{\times 2}$ has order 4, and the three characters $\kappa_{E/K}$ are the nontrivial characters of $K^\times / K^{\times 2}$.

**Example 3.2.5 (the change of normalization).** Let $\mathrm{art}'_K(x) = \mathrm{art}_K(x^{-1})$ be the arithmetic normalization of Volume 3, Remark 2.3.4. The bijection $\chi \mapsto \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K'^{-1}$ sends $\chi$ to $\chi^{-1} \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$, which is $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi^\vee)$. So the arithmetic normalization replaces $\mathrm{rec}_1$ by $\chi \mapsto \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi)^\vee$. Exercise 1 extends this to all $n$.

### Tori

Let $T$ be a torus over $K$, split over a finite Galois extension $L$, with character group $X^*(T)$ and dual torus $\hat T = X^*(T) \otimes \mathbb{C}^\times$. The group $W_K$ acts on $\hat T$ through $\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$. Langlands proved that the characters of $T(K)$ correspond to $H^1(W_K, \hat T)$, the classes of continuous cocycles, see Volume 3, Theorem 4.5.6. For a split torus the action is trivial, and $H^1$ is the group of continuous homomorphisms $W_K \to \hat T$.

**Theorem 3.2.6 (split tori).** Let $T = \mathbb{G}_m^n$, so that $T(K) = (K^\times)^n$ and $\hat T = (\mathbb{C}^\times)^n$. For each continuous character $\chi$ of $(K^\times)^n$ there is exactly one continuous homomorphism $\varphi : W_K \to (\mathbb{C}^\times)^n$ with
$$\chi(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n \varphi_i(w_i) \quad \text{whenever } [w_i] = \mathrm{art}_K(x_i) \text{ in } W_K^{ab} \text{ for each } i,$$
and each continuous $\varphi$ arises from exactly one $\chi$.

*Proof.* Let $\iota_i : K^\times \to (K^\times)^n$ be the inclusion of the $i$-th factor. A continuous character $\chi$ of $(K^\times)^n$ is the product of the characters $\chi_i = \chi \circ \iota_i$, since $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_i \iota_i(x_i)$. Put $\varphi_i = \chi_i \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$, composed with $W_K \to W_K^{ab}$. This $\varphi_i$ is continuous, because $\mathrm{art}_K$ is a homeomorphism, and it satisfies the displayed relation. If $\varphi'$ also satisfies it, take $x = \iota_i(x_i)$ and $w_j = 1$ for $j \ne i$. Then $\varphi'_i(w_i) = \chi_i(x_i) = \varphi_i(w_i)$ for each $w_i$, since each $w_i$ has the form $\mathrm{art}_K(x_i)$ in $W_K^{ab}$. So $\varphi' = \varphi$. Conversely, a continuous $\varphi$ has components $\varphi_i$, each of which factors through $W_K^{ab}$, and $\chi(x) = \prod_i \varphi_i(\mathrm{art}_K(x_i))$ is a continuous character of $(K^\times)^n$ that corresponds to $\varphi$. $\square$

The proof uses only that $\mathrm{art}_K$ is an isomorphism of topological groups. This is also how the Lean project proves it: the theorem `Langlands.localLanglandsTorusSplit` builds the two maps from the bijection of Corollary 3.2.2 coordinate by coordinate, and it does not use properties 1 and 2 of Theorem 3.2.1. The Lean statement takes $\mathrm{art}_K$ with the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap` as a hypothesis, and it is stated for continuous characters, which are smooth by the proof of Corollary 3.2.2.

**Remark 3.2.7 (induced tori).** Let $T = \mathrm{Res}_{L/K} \mathbb{G}_m$ for a finite separable extension $L/K$, so $T(K) = L^\times$. Then $\hat T$ is the induced module of $\mathbb{C}^\times$ from $W_L$ to $W_K$, and Shapiro's lemma gives $H^1(W_K, \hat T) = H^1(W_L, \mathbb{C}^\times)$, which is the group of characters of $W_L$. Local class field theory for $L$ then gives the correspondence, see Volume 3, Theorem 4.5.4. The Lean project states this as `Langlands.LocalLanglandsTorusInduced`, with the classes in $H^1(W_K, \mathrm{Coind}_{W_L}^{W_K} \mathbb{C}^\times)$, and proves it as `Langlands.localLanglandsTorusInduced`. A general torus is a quotient of a product of induced tori, and Langlands deduced the general case from this one, Volume 3, Theorem 4.5.6. The Lean project does not have the general case.

**Remark 3.2.8 (what the Lean statements leave out).** `Langlands.LocalReciprocityGL1` is the full theorem of local class field theory in the form of Theorem 3.2.1, with the norm groups of all finite abelian extensions. It leaves out the local existence theorem, which says that each open subgroup of finite index of $K^\times$ is a norm group, and the ramification properties used in Proposition 3.2.3. In particular the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap` do not include the equality $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) = I_K^{ab}$, where $I_K^{ab}$ is the image of $I_K$ in $W_K^{ab}$. The local factors of characters are in the file `Langlands/Local/GL1.lean`, see the next subsection. `Langlands.LocalLanglandsTorusSplit` is Theorem 3.2.6 as stated, and it does not mention local factors either.

### The case n = 1 in full

This subsection proves Theorem 3.1.2 for $n = 1$, with each of its conditions, in the order of the Lean file `Langlands/Local/GL1.lean`. Corollary 3.2.2 used two facts without proof: that each irreducible representation of $GL_1(K)$ is a character, and that the factors of Tate's thesis are the factors of Godement and Jacquet for $n = 1$. We prove both here. The Lean statement `Langlands.LocalLanglandsGL 1` of Remark 3.1.9 asks for bijections $\mathrm{rec}_m$ for each $m \le 1$, so it also asks for $m = 0$, and we treat this case too.

**Lemma 3.2.9 (Schur's lemma for a commutative group).** Let $G$ be a commutative $\ell$-group, and let $(\pi, V)$ be an irreducible smooth representation of $G$. Suppose that $\pi$ is admissible, or that $G$ is countable at infinity. Then $\dim V = 1$, and there is a unique character $\theta : G \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ with open kernel such that $\pi(g) v = \theta(g) v$ for all $g \in G$ and $v \in V$. So $\pi$ is isomorphic to the representation of $\theta$ on $\mathbb{C}$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.exists_iso_charRep`, for an admissible representation of a commutative topological group. The two steps of the proof are `Langlands.LocalGL1.finiteDimensional_of_comm` and `Langlands.LocalGL1.exists_smul_of_comm`. The case of a group countable at infinity is not in Lean.

*Proof.* First we show that each $\pi(g)$ is a scalar. Suppose that $G$ is countable at infinity. Since $G$ is commutative, $\pi(g)$ commutes with each $\pi(h)$, so $\pi(g)$ is an endomorphism of $\pi$. By Theorem 1.1.7, which is the argument of Jacquet for a space of countable dimension, $\pi(g)$ is a scalar. Now suppose that $\pi$ is admissible. Choose $v \ne 0$ in $V$ and a compact open subgroup $J$ that fixes $v$. For $w \in V^J$, $g \in G$ and $u \in J$ we have $\pi(u) \pi(g) w = \pi(g) \pi(u) w = \pi(g) w$, so $V^J$ is stable under $G$. It contains $v$, so $V^J = V$ by irreducibility, and $V$ has finite dimension by admissibility. Let $g \in G$. The endomorphism $\pi(g)$ of the finite dimensional space $V \ne 0$ has an eigenvalue $c$. Its eigenspace is not zero, and it is stable under $G$, because each $\pi(h)$ commutes with $\pi(g)$. So the eigenspace is $V$, and $\pi(g) = c$.

In both cases write $\pi(g) = \theta(g)$. Then $\theta(gh) = \theta(g) \theta(h)$ and $\theta(1) = 1$, so $\theta$ is a homomorphism to $\mathbb{C}^\times$. Each line in $V$ is stable under $G$, so $V$ is a line by irreducibility. The kernel of $\theta$ is the stabilizer of any $v \ne 0$, which is open because $\pi$ is smooth. The character $\theta$ is unique, because it is determined by the action on one vector $v \ne 0$. $\square$

The group $GL_1(K)$ is commutative, and it is countable at infinity. So both forms of the lemma apply to it. The admissible form is enough for the Lean statement, because the Lean type `Langlands.IrrSmooth` consists of the classes of irreducible admissible representations, as in Definition 1.1.9. By Theorem 1.1.11 this is no restriction.

**Corollary 3.2.10 (the irreducible representations of $GL_1(K)$).** The map $\chi \mapsto [\chi \circ \det]$ is a bijection from the set of smooth characters of $K^\times$ to $\mathcal{A}_1(K) = \mathrm{Irr}(GL_1(K))$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.exists_iso_detChar`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.irrEquiv`. The theorem `exists_iso_detChar` says that each irreducible admissible representation of $GL_1(K)$ is isomorphic to $\chi \circ \det$ for a smooth character $\chi$, and `irrEquiv` is the inverse bijection $\mathrm{Irr}(GL_1(K)) \to \{\text{smooth characters}\}$.

*Proof.* The representation $\chi \circ \det$ has dimension 1, so it is irreducible, and it is smooth because $\det$ is continuous and $\ker \chi$ is open. Let $\iota : K^\times \to GL_1(K)$ send $a$ to the matrix $(a)$. It is an isomorphism of topological groups with inverse $\det$. Let $\pi$ be irreducible and admissible. By Lemma 3.2.9, $\pi \cong \theta$ for a character $\theta$ of $GL_1(K)$ with open kernel. Put $\chi = \theta \circ \iota$. Then $\chi$ is smooth, since $\iota$ is continuous, and $\chi \circ \det = \theta \circ \iota \circ \det = \theta$. So the map is surjective. If $\chi \circ \det \cong \chi' \circ \det$, an isomorphism of lines commutes with the scalars $\chi(\det g)$ and $\chi'(\det g)$, so $\chi = \chi'$. $\square$

From now on we identify $\mathcal{A}_1(K)$ with the smooth characters of $K^\times$, and we write $\chi$ for the class of $\chi \circ \det$. With Corollary 3.2.2 we get a bijection
$$\mathrm{rec}_1 : \mathcal{A}_1(K) \to \mathcal{G}_1(K), \qquad \chi \mapsto (\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}, 0).$$
In Lean it is the composite of `irrEquiv` and `wdEquiv`.

**Theorem 3.2.11 (the local Langlands correspondence for $GL_1$).** Let $\mathrm{art}_K$ be the map of Theorem 3.2.1. The bijection $\mathrm{rec}_1$ satisfies the conditions of Theorem 3.1.2 for $n = n' = 1$. Let $\chi, \chi'$ be smooth characters of $K^\times$, and put $\varphi_\chi = \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$.
1. $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi) = \varphi_\chi$.
2. $\det \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi) = \mathrm{rec}_1(\omega_\chi)$, where $\omega_\chi = \chi$ is the central character.
3. $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi \chi') = \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi) \otimes \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi')$, that is, $\mathrm{rec}_1$ commutes with the twists.
4. $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi^\vee) = \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi)^\vee$, and $\chi^\vee = \chi^{-1}$.
5. $L(s, \chi \times \chi') = L(s, \varphi_\chi \otimes \varphi_{\chi'})$.
6. $\varepsilon(s, \chi \times \chi', \psi) = \varepsilon(s, \varphi_\chi \otimes \varphi_{\chi'}, \psi)$ for each nontrivial character $\psi$ of $K$.

Also $\mathcal{A}_1(K) = \mathcal{A}^0_1(K) = \mathcal{A}^2_1(K)$ and $\mathcal{G}_1(K) = \mathcal{G}^0_1(K) = \mathcal{G}^2_1(K)$, and the conductor of $\chi$ is the Artin conductor of $\varphi_\chi$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_gl1`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_central`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_twist`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_dual`. These are the bijection and parts 1 to 4. The Lean theorems hold for each topological isomorphism $K^\times \to W_K^{ab}$, and they do not use the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap`. Part 5 for $\chi' = 1$ is the named proposition `Langlands.LocalGL1.GL1LFactor`, see Theorem 3.2.14 and Remark 3.2.15. Part 5 for other $\chi'$, part 6 and the last sentence are not in Lean.

Parts 5 and 6 are condition 5 of Theorem 3.1.2. We list the $L$-factors and the $\varepsilon$-factors apart, since they need different inputs and since the Lean statement has only the $L$-factors. So the theorem has six parts, and the condition $\mathrm{rec}_1(\mathcal{A}^0_1) = \mathcal{G}^0_1$ and the conductors come in addition.

*Proof.* Part 1 is the definition. For the other parts we use the Lean form of each condition, which is a statement on representatives of the classes, see Remark 1.1.10.

Part 2. The center of $GL_1(K)$ is the whole group, and the scalar $a \cdot 1$ is the matrix $(a)$ with $\det (a) = a$. So $\chi \circ \det$ acts on $a \cdot 1$ by $\chi(a)$, and $\omega_\chi = \chi$. This is `Langlands.charRep_hasCentralChar`. Let $(r, N)$ represent $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi)$. For a matrix of size 1, $\det r(w) = r(w)$. Two Weil-Deligne representations of dimension 1 are isomorphic only if they have the same $r$, since conjugation in $GL_1(\mathbb{C})$ is trivial. So $\det r = \varphi_\chi = \mathrm{rec}_1(\omega_\chi)$. In the Lean form: if $\pi$ has central character $\omega$, then $\omega$ and $\det r$ correspond through $\mathrm{art}_K$, that is, $\omega(x) = \det r(w)$ whenever $w$ maps to $\mathrm{art}_K(x)$ in $W_K^{ab}$.

Part 3. For each $g \in GL_1(K)$ we have $(\chi \circ \det)(g) \cdot (\chi' \circ \det)(g) = (\chi \chi')(\det g)$, so the twist of $\chi$ by $\chi' \circ \det$ is $\chi \chi'$. On the other side, $\varphi_{\chi \chi'} = \varphi_\chi \varphi_{\chi'}$, which is the twist of $(\varphi_\chi, 0)$ by $\varphi_{\chi'}$. In the Lean form, $\varphi_{\chi'}$ is any character $\varphi$ of $W_K$ that corresponds to $\chi'$ through $\mathrm{art}_K$. Such a $\varphi$ is equal to $\varphi_{\chi'}$, because each $w \in W_K$ maps to $\mathrm{art}_K(x)$ for some $x$.

Part 4. The contragredient of a line $V$ with the character $\chi \circ \det$ is the line $V^*$, and $(\pi^\vee(g) \lambda)(v) = \lambda(\pi(g^{-1}) v) = \chi(\det g)^{-1} \lambda(v)$. So $\chi^\vee = \chi^{-1}$. The dual of $(\varphi, 0)$ is $({}^t \varphi^{-1}, -{}^t 0) = (\varphi^{-1}, 0)$, and $\varphi_{\chi^{-1}} = \varphi_\chi^{-1}$.

Part 5. By Section 3.1, the factor $L(s, \chi \times \chi')$ for $n' = 1$ is the Godement-Jacquet factor of $\chi \circ \det \otimes \chi' \circ \det = (\chi \chi') \circ \det$. By parts 3 and 1, $\varphi_\chi \otimes \varphi_{\chi'} = \varphi_{\chi \chi'}$. So it suffices to prove $L_{GJ}(s, \mu) = L(s, \varphi_\mu)$ for each smooth character $\mu$, where $L_{GJ}$ is the factor of Godement and Jacquet of Section 2.5. For $n = 1$ we have $M_1(K) = K$, the space of $\mu \circ \det$ is a line, and each matrix coefficient is $f(x) = c \, \mu(x)$ with $c \in \mathbb{C}$. The zeta integral of Section 2.5 for $n = 1$ is
$$Z(\Phi, s, f) = c \int_{K^\times} \Phi(x) \mu(x) |x|^s \, d^\times g = c \, c' \, Z(\Phi, \mu, s),$$
where $Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$ is the zeta integral of Volume 3, Definition 5.1.8, for $\Phi \in \mathcal{S}(K)$, and $c' \ne 0$ is the ratio of the measure $d^\times g$ of Section 2.5 to Tate's measure $d^\times x$. The Lean integral `Langlands.gjZeta` is $\int_K \Phi(x) f(x) |x|^{s - 1} \, dx$ for an additive Haar measure $dx$, and $|x|^{-1} dx$ is a constant multiple of $d^\times x$. So the zeta integrals of $\mu \circ \det$ are the constant multiples of Tate's integrals $Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$. By Volume 3, Theorem 5.2.7, part 1, and the remark after Volume 3, Corollary 5.2.21, Tate's integrals span the space $L(\mu, s) \, \mathbb{C}[q^{-s}, q^s]$, where $L(\mu, s)$ is the factor of Volume 3, Definition 5.2.2. For a ramified $\mu$ this is Volume 3, Corollary 5.2.21, and for an unramified $\mu$ the integrals of the functions $1_{\mathfrak{p}^k}$ give the generator. So $L_{GJ}(s, \mu)$, which is the generator of this space of the form $1 / P(q^{-s})$ with $P(0) = 1$, is Tate's factor $L(\mu, s)$. By Proposition 3.2.3, part 2, Tate's factor is $L(s, \varphi_\mu)$.

Part 6. In the same way, the factor $\varepsilon(s, \chi \times \chi', \psi)$ is the Godement-Jacquet factor of $\mu = \chi \chi'$. For $n = 1$ the Fourier transform on $M_1(K) = K$ is the transform for $\psi$ of Volume 3, Section 5.1, and $f^\vee(x) = f(x^{-1}) = c \, \mu^{-1}(x)$. The constants $c$ and $c'$ are the same on both sides. So the functional equation of Theorem 2.5.3, part 3, reads $Z(\hat \Phi, \mu^{-1}, 1 - s) = \gamma(s, \mu, \psi) Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$. This is Tate's local functional equation, Volume 3, Theorem 5.2.7, part 2. It determines $\gamma(s, \mu, \psi)$, because some $Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$ is not zero. The $L$-factors of both sides are Tate's factors by part 5, so the Godement-Jacquet factor $\varepsilon(s, \mu, \psi)$ is Tate's factor $\varepsilon(\mu, s, \psi)$. Propositions 5.2.3 and 5.2.4 of Volume 3 compute it. By Proposition 3.2.3, part 3, it is $\varepsilon(s, \varphi_\mu, \psi)$.

The last sentence. The group $GL_1(K)$ has no proper parabolic subgroup, so each irreducible representation is supercuspidal, and in particular essentially square integrable. Each Weil-Deligne representation of dimension 1 has $N = 0$ and is irreducible. The conductor exponent of $\chi$ is the exponent $a(\chi)$ in $\varepsilon(\chi, s, \psi) = \varepsilon(\chi, 1/2, \psi) q^{a(\chi)(1/2 - s)}$ for $\psi$ of conductor $\mathcal{O}_K$, by Volume 3, Theorem 5.2.7, part 3. It is $a(\varphi_\chi)$ by Proposition 3.2.3, part 1. $\square$

**Remark 3.2.12 (the hypothesis in the Lean comparison of $L$-factors).** The proof of part 2 of Proposition 3.2.3 has two steps, and the Lean file proves them apart. Let $\chi$ be smooth, $\varphi = \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$ and $\varpi$ a uniformizer. The first step is `Langlands.LocalGL1.lFactor_wdOfChar_eq`: $L(s, (\varphi, 0)) = (1 - \chi(\varpi) q^{-s})^{-1}$ if $\varphi$ is trivial on $I_K$, and $L(s, (\varphi, 0)) = 1$ otherwise. The proof uses only property 1 of Theorem 3.2.1. The space $V^{I_K}_N$ is $\mathbb{C}$ if $\varphi$ is trivial on $I_K$, and $0$ otherwise. In the first case $\Phi$ acts on it by $\varphi(\Phi)$. By property 1 there is a geometric Frobenius $\Phi'$ with $\mathrm{art}_K(\varpi) = [\Phi']$. Both $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$ map to the inverse of the Frobenius of $\bar k / k$, so $\Phi'^{-1} \Phi \in I_K$, and $\varphi(\Phi) = \varphi(\Phi') = \chi(\varpi)$. The second step is `Langlands.LocalGL1.charLFactor_eq_lFactor_of_iff`: Tate's factor $L(s, \chi)$ is $L(s, (\varphi, 0))$, under the hypothesis that $\chi$ is trivial on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ if and only if $\varphi$ is trivial on $I_K$. The Lean theorem needs this hypothesis because `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap` does not state that $\mathrm{art}_K$ maps $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ onto $I_K^{ab}$. In the mathematics the equality $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) = I_K^{ab}$ is part of local class field theory. It is proved in the proof of Volume 3, Theorem 2.3.3, from Volume 3, Proposition 2.1.8, and it is the statement on unramified characters of Volume 3, Corollary 2.3.6. One inclusion follows from property 1 alone, see Exercise 12. The other inclusion uses property 2 for all finite abelian extensions and a compactness argument.

**Proposition 3.2.13 (the case $n = 0$).** The group $GL_0(K)$ is trivial, and $\mathcal{A}_0(K)$ and $\mathcal{G}_0(K)$ each have exactly one element. The unique bijection $\mathrm{rec}_0 : \mathcal{A}_0(K) \to \mathcal{G}_0(K)$ satisfies conditions 2 to 4 of Theorem 3.1.2 and the condition on the standard $L$-factor, both factors being equal to 1.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec0`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.isLanglandsMap_rec0`

*Proof.* A representation of the trivial group is irreducible only if it is a line, by Lemma 3.2.9, so $\mathcal{A}_0(K)$ consists of the trivial representation. A Weil-Deligne representation of dimension 0 is the zero space, so $\mathcal{G}_0(K)$ has one element. Conditions 3 and 4 hold because both sides have one element. For condition 2, let $\pi$ have central character $\omega$. Each scalar $a \cdot 1$ is the identity of $GL_0(K)$, so $\omega(a) = 1$ for all $a$. The determinant of an endomorphism of the zero space is 1, so $\det r = 1$, and it corresponds to $\omega = 1$. For the $L$-factors, $L(s, (r, N)) = \det(1 - q^{-s} \Phi \mid 0)^{-1} = 1$. The space $M_0(K)$ has one point $0$, and the zeta integral of $\Phi$ and of the matrix coefficient $\lambda(\pi(g) v)$ is $\Phi(0) \lambda(v) \, \mathrm{vol}(M_0(K))$. The volume is not zero. So the zeta integrals are the constants, and the Godement-Jacquet factor is 1. $\square$

**Remark 3.2.15 (`GL1LFactor` is Tate's local theory).** The statement `GL1LFactor` is Tate's local theory in the form of Godement and Jacquet. By the proof of part 5 of Theorem 3.2.11 it consists of two facts. The first is that the zeta integrals of $\chi \circ \det$ on $M_1(K)$ span $L(\chi, s) \, \mathbb{C}[q^{-s}, q^s]$, which is Volume 3, Theorem 5.2.7, part 1, and Volume 3, Corollary 5.2.21. The second is that Tate's factor $L(\chi, s)$ is $L(s, (\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}, 0))$, which needs $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) = I_K^{ab}$, see Remark 3.2.12. The Lean project does not prove `GL1LFactor` yet, so it proves `LocalLanglandsGL 1` only up to this one proposition. The reason is that the Lean files on Tate's thesis in `Langlands/Tate` do not apply. They work over the completion $K_v$ of a number field at a finite place, and not over an arbitrary nonarchimedean local field with the class `IsNonarchimedeanLocalField`, which also includes the fields $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$. They also treat only the standard test functions $1_{\mathcal{O}_v}$ and $1_{U^{(n)}}$, while `Langlands.IsGJLFactor` asks for every Schwartz-Bruhat function on $M_1(K)$, see Volume 3, Remark 5.2.12. Finally the $\varepsilon$-factors are not in the Lean statement, so part 6 of Theorem 3.2.11 has no Lean form.
