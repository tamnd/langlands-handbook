## The case n = 1 and the tori

For $n = 1$ the local Langlands correspondence is local class field theory. This section recalls the reciprocity map from Volume 3, shows that it gives a bijection $\mathcal{A}_1(K) \to \mathcal{G}_1(K)$, and checks that the bijection matches the conductors and the local factors. It then treats split tori, the first groups other than $GL_n$ for which the correspondence is a theorem. The last subsection proves the case $n = 1$ in full: it identifies $\mathcal{A}_1(K)$ with the smooth characters by Schur's lemma, checks each condition of Theorem 3.1.2, and treats the trivial case $n = 0$ that the Lean statement also asks for. It follows the Lean files `Langlands/Local/GL1.lean` and `Langlands/Local/GL1LFactor.lean`, which together prove the Lean form of all of this with no hypothesis. The analytic input is Tate's computation of the zeta integral of a character, and the last subsection writes it out in full. It also explains why the first Lean form of this input was false: it allowed the zero representation. The example to keep in mind is the quadratic character $\kappa_{E/K}$ of $K^\times$ attached to a quadratic extension $E/K$. It corresponds to the character of $W_K$ with kernel $W_E$, and it appears in all the later sections.

### Local reciprocity

**Theorem 3.2.1 (local class field theory).** There is a unique isomorphism of topological groups $\mathrm{art}_K : K^\times \to W_K^{ab}$ with these properties.
1. For each uniformizer $\varpi$ of $K$, $\mathrm{art}_K(\varpi)$ is the class of a geometric Frobenius element.
2. For each finite abelian extension $L/K$ inside a fixed algebraic closure, the composite of $\mathrm{art}_K$ with the restriction $W_K^{ab} \to \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$ is surjective, and its kernel is the norm group $N_{L/K}(L^\times)$.

This is Volume 3, Theorem 2.3.3, where the map is called $\mathrm{rec}_K$. The proof is in Volume 3, Chapters 2 and 3, with Lubin-Tate theory for the existence, and also in [Ser67] and [Mil20], Chapter I. The Lean statement `Langlands.LocalReciprocityGL1` says that for each nonarchimedean local field $K$ there is exactly one topological isomorphism $K^\times \to W_K^{ab}$ with the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap`, which are properties 1 and 2. The Lean project proves it as the theorem `Langlands.localReciprocityGL1`. The image $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ is the image of the inertia group $I_K$ in $W_K^{ab}$, and $\mathrm{art}_K(1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m)$ is the image of the upper numbering ramification group $I_K^m$ for $m \ge 1$, see [Ser79].

**Corollary 3.2.2 (the correspondence for $GL_1$).** The map $\chi \mapsto \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$ is a bijection from the set of smooth characters of $K^\times$ to $\mathcal{G}_1(K)$. It satisfies conditions 1 to 4 of Theorem 3.1.2 for $n = 1$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.wdOfChar`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.charOfWD`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.wdEquiv`. The bijection is `wdEquiv`. It sends $\chi$ to the class of $(\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}, 0)$, which is `wdOfChar`, and its inverse sends $(r, N)$ to $\det r \circ \mathrm{art}_K$, which is `charOfWD`. The conditions are Theorem 3.2.11 below.

*Proof.* A Weil-Deligne representation $(r, N)$ of dimension 1 has $N = 0$, because a nilpotent endomorphism of a line is 0. It is Frobenius semisimple, because each element of $GL_1(\mathbb{C})$ is semisimple. So $\mathcal{G}_1(K)$ is the set of continuous characters of $W_K$. Each of them factors through $W_K^{ab}$, because $\mathbb{C}^\times$ is commutative and the closure of the commutator subgroup is in the kernel. So $\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ \mathrm{art}_K$ is a bijection from $\mathcal{G}_1(K)$ to the continuous characters of $K^\times$. It remains to see that each continuous character $\chi$ of $K^\times$ is smooth, that is, has open kernel. Let $U = \{z \in \mathbb{C}^\times : |z - 1| < 1\}$. The only subgroup $H$ of $\mathbb{C}^\times$ inside $U$ is $\{1\}$. Indeed, let $z \in H$. If $|z| \ne 1$, then $z$ or $z^{-1}$ has absolute value greater than 1, and its powers, which are in $H$, leave every bounded set. If $z = e^{i\theta}$ with $0 < |\theta| \le \pi$, then some multiple $m\theta$ lies in $[\pi/3, 5\pi/3]$ modulo $2\pi$, because the multiples move by steps of length $|\theta|$, and then $|z^m - 1| \ge 1$. Since $\chi$ is continuous, $\chi^{-1}(U)$ contains one of the open subgroups $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$, and $\chi(1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m)$ is a subgroup in $U$. So $\chi$ is trivial on $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$. Conversely a smooth character is continuous. Here we identify $\mathcal{A}_1(K)$ with the set of smooth characters of $K^\times$, which Corollary 3.2.10 below justifies. Conditions 1 and 3 hold by construction, and condition 4 holds since $(\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1})^{-1} = \chi^{-1} \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$. Condition 2 is condition 1, because the central character of $\chi$ is $\chi$. $\square$

This is Volume 3, Theorem 4.2.3, written from the Galois side there. The last sentence of the proof is the Lean theorem `Langlands.charRep_hasCentralChar`: the central character of the representation $\chi \circ \det$ of $GL_1(K)$ is $\chi$.

### Conductors and local factors

Let $\chi$ be a smooth character of $K^\times$ and $\varphi = \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$. The *conductor exponent* $a(\chi)$ is 0 if $\chi$ is unramified, that is trivial on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$, and otherwise the least $m \ge 1$ with $\chi$ trivial on $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$. The Artin conductor $a(\varphi)$ is defined in Volume 3, Definition 4.2.1.

**Proposition 3.2.3 (the factors match for $n = 1$).** Let $\chi$ and $\varphi$ be as above.
1. $a(\chi) = a(\varphi)$.
2. $L(s, \chi) = L(s, \varphi)$, where the left side is Tate's factor and the right side is the factor of Chapter 2.
3. $\varepsilon(s, \chi, \psi) = \varepsilon(s, \varphi, \psi)$ for each nontrivial character $\psi$ of $K$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.lFactor_wdOfChar_eq`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.charLFactor_eq_lFactor_of_iff`. These two theorems give part 2, with a hypothesis that Remark 3.2.12 explains. Tate's factor is `Langlands.LocalGL1.charLFactor`. Parts 1 and 3 are not stated in Lean.

*Proof.* Part 1 is Volume 3, Theorem 4.2.3, from the description of the images of the ramification groups after Theorem 3.2.1. For part 2, the space of $\varphi$ is the line $\mathbb{C}$, and $N = 0$. If $\chi$ is unramified, then $\varphi$ is trivial on $I_K$, because $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ is the image of $I_K$. So $V^{I_K} = V$, and $\Phi$ acts on it by $\varphi(\Phi) = \chi(\varpi)$, because $\mathrm{art}_K(\varpi)$ is the class of $\Phi$ by property 1 of Theorem 3.2.1. Thus $L(s, \varphi) = (1 - \chi(\varpi) q^{-s})^{-1}$, which is Tate's factor. If $\chi$ is ramified, then $\varphi$ is not trivial on $I_K$, so $V^{I_K} = 0$ and both factors are 1. Part 3 holds by definition: the theorem of Langlands and Deligne, Volume 3, Theorem 4.3.8, defines the $\varepsilon$-factors of characters of $W_K$ as Tate's factors of the corresponding characters of $K^\times$. $\square$

Volume 3 writes $L(\chi, s)$ and $\varepsilon(\chi, s, \psi)$. We put $s$ first, as is usual for $GL_n$. The proof of part 2 is where the geometric normalization matters, see Volume 3, Remark 4.3.7. With the arithmetic normalization, $\varphi(\Phi)$ would be $\chi(\varpi)^{-1}$, and the two $L$-factors would differ.

**Example 3.2.4 (quadratic characters).** Let $E/K$ be a separable quadratic extension, and let $\kappa_{E/K}$ be the character of $K^\times$ with kernel $N_{E/K}(E^\times)$. By Theorem 3.2.1 the norm group has index 2, so $\kappa_{E/K}$ is a character of order 2, and $\kappa_{E/K} \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$ is the character of $W_K$ with kernel $W_E$. If $E/K$ is unramified, the norm group is $\mathcal{O}_K^\times \varpi^{2\mathbb{Z}}$, so $\kappa_{E/K}(x) = (-1)^{v_K(x)}$. This is the unramified quadratic character, and $L(s, \kappa_{E/K}) = (1 + q^{-s})^{-1}$. If $p \ne 2$ and $E = K(\sqrt{\varpi})$, the norm group contains $-\varpi = N(\sqrt{\varpi})$ and the squares of $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$. So $\kappa_{E/K}$ is the character with $\kappa_{E/K}(-\varpi) = 1$ whose restriction to $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ is the Legendre symbol of the residue field, and $a(\kappa_{E/K}) = 1$. For $p \ne 2$ there are exactly three quadratic extensions, because $K^\times / K^{\times 2}$ has order 4, and the three characters $\kappa_{E/K}$ are the nontrivial characters of $K^\times / K^{\times 2}$.

**Example 3.2.5 (the change of normalization).** Let $\mathrm{art}'_K(x) = \mathrm{art}_K(x^{-1})$ be the arithmetic normalization of Volume 3, Remark 2.3.4. The bijection $\chi \mapsto \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K'^{-1}$ sends $\chi$ to $\chi^{-1} \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$, which is $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi^\vee)$. So the arithmetic normalization replaces $\mathrm{rec}_1$ by $\chi \mapsto \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi)^\vee$. Exercise 1 extends this to all $n$.

### Tori

Let $T$ be a torus over $K$, split over a finite Galois extension $L$, with character group $X^*(T)$ and dual torus $\hat T = X^*(T) \otimes \mathbb{C}^\times$. The group $W_K$ acts on $\hat T$ through $\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$. Langlands proved that the characters of $T(K)$ correspond to $H^1(W_K, \hat T)$, the classes of continuous cocycles, see Volume 3, Theorem 4.5.6. For a split torus the action is trivial, and $H^1$ is the group of continuous homomorphisms $W_K \to \hat T$.

**Theorem 3.2.6 (split tori).** Let $T = \mathbb{G}_m^n$, so that $T(K) = (K^\times)^n$ and $\hat T = (\mathbb{C}^\times)^n$. For each continuous character $\chi$ of $(K^\times)^n$ there is exactly one continuous homomorphism $\varphi : W_K \to (\mathbb{C}^\times)^n$ with
$$\chi(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n \varphi_i(w_i) \quad \text{whenever } [w_i] = \mathrm{art}_K(x_i) \text{ in } W_K^{ab} \text{ for each } i,$$
and each continuous $\varphi$ arises from exactly one $\chi$.

*Proof.* Let $\iota_i : K^\times \to (K^\times)^n$ be the inclusion of the $i$-th factor. A continuous character $\chi$ of $(K^\times)^n$ is the product of the characters $\chi_i = \chi \circ \iota_i$, since $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_i \iota_i(x_i)$. Put $\varphi_i = \chi_i \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$, composed with $W_K \to W_K^{ab}$. This $\varphi_i$ is continuous, because $\mathrm{art}_K$ is a homeomorphism, and it satisfies the displayed relation. If $\varphi'$ also satisfies it, take $x = \iota_i(x_i)$ and $w_j = 1$ for $j \ne i$. Then $\varphi'_i(w_i) = \chi_i(x_i) = \varphi_i(w_i)$ for each $w_i$, since each $w_i$ has the form $\mathrm{art}_K(x_i)$ in $W_K^{ab}$. So $\varphi' = \varphi$. Conversely, a continuous $\varphi$ has components $\varphi_i$, each of which factors through $W_K^{ab}$, and $\chi(x) = \prod_i \varphi_i(\mathrm{art}_K(x_i))$ is a continuous character of $(K^\times)^n$ that corresponds to $\varphi$. $\square$

The proof uses only that $\mathrm{art}_K$ is an isomorphism of topological groups. This is also how the Lean project proves it: the theorem `Langlands.localLanglandsTorusSplit` builds the two maps from the bijection of Corollary 3.2.2 coordinate by coordinate, and it does not use properties 1 and 2 of Theorem 3.2.1. The Lean statement takes $\mathrm{art}_K$ with the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap` as a hypothesis, and it is stated for continuous characters, which are smooth by the proof of Corollary 3.2.2.

**Remark 3.2.7 (induced tori).** Let $T = \mathrm{Res}_{L/K} \mathbb{G}_m$ for a finite separable extension $L/K$, so $T(K) = L^\times$. Then $\hat T$ is the induced module of $\mathbb{C}^\times$ from $W_L$ to $W_K$, and Shapiro's lemma gives $H^1(W_K, \hat T) = H^1(W_L, \mathbb{C}^\times)$, which is the group of characters of $W_L$. Local class field theory for $L$ then gives the correspondence, see Volume 3, Theorem 4.5.4. The Lean project states this as `Langlands.LocalLanglandsTorusInduced`, with the classes in $H^1(W_K, \mathrm{Coind}_{W_L}^{W_K} \mathbb{C}^\times)$, and proves it as `Langlands.localLanglandsTorusInduced`. A general torus is a quotient of a product of induced tori, and Langlands deduced the general case from this one, Volume 3, Theorem 4.5.6. The Lean project does not have the general case.

**Remark 3.2.8 (what the Lean statements leave out).** `Langlands.LocalReciprocityGL1` is the full theorem of local class field theory in the form of Theorem 3.2.1, with the norm groups of all finite abelian extensions. It leaves out the local existence theorem, which says that each open subgroup of finite index of $K^\times$ is a norm group, and the ramification properties used in Proposition 3.2.3. In particular the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap` do not include the equality $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) = I_K^{ab}$, where $I_K^{ab}$ is the image of $I_K$ in $W_K^{ab}$. This equality follows from property 1 and the bijectivity of $\mathrm{art}_K$, see Remark 3.2.12. The local factors of characters are in the files `Langlands/Local/GL1.lean` and `Langlands/Local/GL1LFactor.lean`, see the next two subsections. `Langlands.LocalLanglandsTorusSplit` is Theorem 3.2.6 as stated, and it does not mention local factors either.

### The case n = 1 in full

This subsection proves Theorem 3.1.2 for $n = 1$, with each of its conditions, in the order of the Lean file `Langlands/Local/GL1.lean`. Corollary 3.2.2 used two facts without proof: that each irreducible representation of $GL_1(K)$ is a character, and that the factors of Tate's thesis are the factors of Godement and Jacquet for $n = 1$. We prove the first fact here. The second fact is Theorem 3.2.22, in the last subsection, and the proofs of this subsection use it as a black box. The Lean statement `Langlands.LocalLanglandsGL 1` of Remark 3.1.9 asks for bijections $\mathrm{rec}_m$ for each $m \le 1$, so it also asks for $m = 0$, and we treat this case too.

**Lemma 3.2.9 (Schur's lemma for a commutative group).** Let $G$ be a commutative $\ell$-group, and let $(\pi, V)$ be an irreducible smooth representation of $G$. Suppose that $\pi$ is admissible, or that $G$ is countable at infinity. Then $\dim V = 1$, and there is a unique character $\theta : G \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ with open kernel such that $\pi(g) v = \theta(g) v$ for all $g \in G$ and $v \in V$. So $\pi$ is isomorphic to the representation of $\theta$ on $\mathbb{C}$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.exists_iso_charRep`, for an admissible representation of a commutative topological group. The two steps of the proof are `Langlands.LocalGL1.finiteDimensional_of_comm` and `Langlands.LocalGL1.exists_smul_of_comm`. The case of a group countable at infinity is not in Lean.

*Proof.* First we show that each $\pi(g)$ is a scalar. Suppose that $G$ is countable at infinity. Since $G$ is commutative, $\pi(g)$ commutes with each $\pi(h)$, so $\pi(g)$ is an endomorphism of $\pi$. By Theorem 1.1.7, which is the argument of Jacquet for a space of countable dimension, $\pi(g)$ is a scalar. Now suppose that $\pi$ is admissible. Choose $v \ne 0$ in $V$ and a compact open subgroup $J$ that fixes $v$. For $w \in V^J$, $g \in G$ and $u \in J$ we have $\pi(u) \pi(g) w = \pi(g) \pi(u) w = \pi(g) w$, so $V^J$ is stable under $G$. It contains $v$, so $V^J = V$ by irreducibility, and $V$ has finite dimension by admissibility. Let $g \in G$. The endomorphism $\pi(g)$ of the finite dimensional space $V \ne 0$ has an eigenvalue $c$. Its eigenspace is not zero, and it is stable under $G$, because each $\pi(h)$ commutes with $\pi(g)$. So the eigenspace is $V$, and $\pi(g) = c$.

In both cases write $\pi(g) = \theta(g)$. Then $\theta(gh) = \theta(g) \theta(h)$ and $\theta(1) = 1$, so $\theta$ is a homomorphism to $\mathbb{C}^\times$. Each line in $V$ is stable under $G$, so $V$ is a line by irreducibility. The kernel of $\theta$ is the stabilizer of any $v \ne 0$, which is open because $\pi$ is smooth. The character $\theta$ is unique, because it is determined by the action on one vector $v \ne 0$. $\square$

The group $GL_1(K)$ is commutative, and it is countable at infinity. So both forms of the lemma apply to it. The admissible form is enough for the Lean statement, because the Lean type `Langlands.IrrSmooth` consists of the classes of irreducible admissible representations, as in Definition 1.1.9. By Theorem 1.1.11 this is no restriction.

**Corollary 3.2.10 (the irreducible representations of $GL_1(K)$).** The map $\chi \mapsto [\chi \circ \det]$ is a bijection from the set of smooth characters of $K^\times$ to $\mathcal{A}_1(K) = \mathrm{Irr}(GL_1(K))$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.exists_iso_detChar`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.irrEquiv`. The theorem `exists_iso_detChar` says that each irreducible admissible representation of $GL_1(K)$ is isomorphic to $\chi \circ \det$ for a smooth character $\chi$, and `irrEquiv` is the inverse bijection $\mathrm{Irr}(GL_1(K)) \to \{\text{smooth characters}\}$.

*Proof.* The representation $\chi \circ \det$ has dimension 1, so it is irreducible, and it is smooth because $\det$ is continuous and $\ker \chi$ is open. Let $\iota : K^\times \to GL_1(K)$ send $a$ to the matrix $(a)$. It is an isomorphism of topological groups with inverse $\det$. Let $\pi$ be irreducible and admissible. By Lemma 3.2.9, $\pi \cong \theta$ for a character $\theta$ of $GL_1(K)$ with open kernel. Put $\chi = \theta \circ \iota$. Then $\chi$ is smooth, since $\iota$ is continuous, and $\chi \circ \det = \theta \circ \iota \circ \det = \theta$. So the map is surjective. If $\chi \circ \det \cong \chi' \circ \det$, an isomorphism of lines commutes with the scalars $\chi(\det g)$ and $\chi'(\det g)$, so $\chi = \chi'$. $\square$

From now on we identify $\mathcal{A}_1(K)$ with the smooth characters of $K^\times$, and we write $\chi$ for the class of $\chi \circ \det$. With Corollary 3.2.2 we get a bijection
$$\mathrm{rec}_1 : \mathcal{A}_1(K) \to \mathcal{G}_1(K), \qquad \chi \mapsto (\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}, 0).$$
In Lean it is the composite of `irrEquiv` and `wdEquiv`.

**Theorem 3.2.11 (the local Langlands correspondence for $GL_1$).** Let $\mathrm{art}_K$ be the map of Theorem 3.2.1. The bijection $\mathrm{rec}_1$ satisfies the conditions of Theorem 3.1.2 for $n = n' = 1$. Let $\chi, \chi'$ be smooth characters of $K^\times$, and put $\varphi_\chi = \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$.
1. $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi) = \varphi_\chi$.
2. $\det \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi) = \mathrm{rec}_1(\omega_\chi)$, where $\omega_\chi = \chi$ is the central character.
3. $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi \chi') = \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi) \otimes \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi')$, that is, $\mathrm{rec}_1$ commutes with the twists.
4. $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi^\vee) = \mathrm{rec}_1(\chi)^\vee$, and $\chi^\vee = \chi^{-1}$.
5. $L(s, \chi \times \chi') = L(s, \varphi_\chi \otimes \varphi_{\chi'})$.
6. $\varepsilon(s, \chi \times \chi', \psi) = \varepsilon(s, \varphi_\chi \otimes \varphi_{\chi'}, \psi)$ for each nontrivial character $\psi$ of $K$.

Also $\mathcal{A}_1(K) = \mathcal{A}^0_1(K) = \mathcal{A}^2_1(K)$ and $\mathcal{G}_1(K) = \mathcal{G}^0_1(K) = \mathcal{G}^2_1(K)$, and the conductor of $\chi$ is the Artin conductor of $\varphi_\chi$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_gl1`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_central`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_twist`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.rec1_dual`. These are the bijection and parts 1 to 4. The Lean theorems hold for each topological isomorphism $K^\times \to W_K^{ab}$, and they do not use the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap`. Part 5 for $\chi' = 1$ is `Langlands.LocalGL1.isGJLFactor_wdOfChar`, see Theorem 3.2.22. Part 5 for other $\chi'$, part 6 and the last sentence are not in Lean.

Parts 5 and 6 are condition 5 of Theorem 3.1.2. We list the $L$-factors and the $\varepsilon$-factors apart, since they need different inputs and since the Lean statement has only the $L$-factors. So the theorem has six parts, and the condition $\mathrm{rec}_1(\mathcal{A}^0_1) = \mathcal{G}^0_1$ and the conductors come in addition.

*Proof.* Part 1 is the definition. For the other parts we use the Lean form of each condition, which is a statement on representatives of the classes, see Remark 1.1.10.

Part 2. The center of $GL_1(K)$ is the whole group, and the scalar $a \cdot 1$ is the matrix $(a)$ with $\det (a) = a$. So $\chi \circ \det$ acts on $a \cdot 1$ by $\chi(a)$, and $\omega_\chi = \chi$. This is `Langlands.charRep_hasCentralChar`. Let $(r, N)$ represent $\mathrm{rec}_1(\chi)$. For a matrix of size 1, $\det r(w) = r(w)$. Two Weil-Deligne representations of dimension 1 are isomorphic only if they have the same $r$, since conjugation in $GL_1(\mathbb{C})$ is trivial. So $\det r = \varphi_\chi = \mathrm{rec}_1(\omega_\chi)$. In the Lean form: if $\pi$ has central character $\omega$, then $\omega$ and $\det r$ correspond through $\mathrm{art}_K$, that is, $\omega(x) = \det r(w)$ whenever $w$ maps to $\mathrm{art}_K(x)$ in $W_K^{ab}$.

Part 3. For each $g \in GL_1(K)$ we have $(\chi \circ \det)(g) \cdot (\chi' \circ \det)(g) = (\chi \chi')(\det g)$, so the twist of $\chi$ by $\chi' \circ \det$ is $\chi \chi'$. On the other side, $\varphi_{\chi \chi'} = \varphi_\chi \varphi_{\chi'}$, which is the twist of $(\varphi_\chi, 0)$ by $\varphi_{\chi'}$. In the Lean form, $\varphi_{\chi'}$ is any character $\varphi$ of $W_K$ that corresponds to $\chi'$ through $\mathrm{art}_K$. Such a $\varphi$ is equal to $\varphi_{\chi'}$, because each $w \in W_K$ maps to $\mathrm{art}_K(x)$ for some $x$.

Part 4. The contragredient of a line $V$ with the character $\chi \circ \det$ is the line $V^*$, and $(\pi^\vee(g) \lambda)(v) = \lambda(\pi(g^{-1}) v) = \chi(\det g)^{-1} \lambda(v)$. So $\chi^\vee = \chi^{-1}$. The dual of $(\varphi, 0)$ is $({}^t \varphi^{-1}, -{}^t 0) = (\varphi^{-1}, 0)$, and $\varphi_{\chi^{-1}} = \varphi_\chi^{-1}$.

Part 5. By Section 3.1, the factor $L(s, \chi \times \chi')$ for $n' = 1$ is the Godement-Jacquet factor of $\chi \circ \det \otimes \chi' \circ \det = (\chi \chi') \circ \det$. By parts 3 and 1, $\varphi_\chi \otimes \varphi_{\chi'} = \varphi_{\chi \chi'}$. So it suffices to prove $L_{GJ}(s, \mu) = L(s, \varphi_\mu)$ for each smooth character $\mu$, where $L_{GJ}$ is the factor of Godement and Jacquet of Section 2.5. This is Theorem 3.2.22 below, and its proof has two steps. Proposition 3.2.21 shows that the zeta integrals of $\mu \circ \det$ are the multiples $\lambda(v) Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$ of Tate's integrals. Corollary 3.2.19 shows that Tate's integrals span $L(s, \mu) \, \mathbb{C}[q^{-s}, q^s]$, where $L(s, \mu)$ is Tate's factor of Volume 3, Definition 5.2.2. So $L_{GJ}(s, \mu)$, which is the generator of this space of the form $1 / P(q^{-s})$ with $P(0) = 1$, is Tate's factor. By Proposition 3.2.3, part 2, Tate's factor is $L(s, \varphi_\mu)$.

Part 6. In the same way, the factor $\varepsilon(s, \chi \times \chi', \psi)$ is the Godement-Jacquet factor of $\mu = \chi \chi'$. For $n = 1$ the Fourier transform on $M_1(K) = K$ is the transform for $\psi$ of Volume 3, Section 5.1. A matrix coefficient is $f(x) = c \, \mu(x)$ with $c = \lambda(v)$, and $f^\vee(x) = f(x^{-1}) = c \, \mu^{-1}(x)$. By Proposition 3.2.21 and Volume 3, Definition 5.1.5, $Z(\Phi, s, f) = c \, c' \, Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$, where $Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$ is the integral of Volume 3, Definition 5.1.8, and $c' \ne 0$ is the ratio of the measure $d^\times g$ of Section 2.5 to Tate's measure $d^\times x$. The same constants $c$ and $c'$ appear for $f^\vee$ and $\mu^{-1}$. So the functional equation of Theorem 2.5.3, part 3, reads $Z(\hat \Phi, \mu^{-1}, 1 - s) = \gamma(s, \mu, \psi) Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$. This is Tate's local functional equation, Volume 3, Theorem 5.2.7, part 2. It determines $\gamma(s, \mu, \psi)$, because some $Z(\Phi, \mu, s)$ is not zero. The $L$-factors of both sides are Tate's factors by part 5, so the Godement-Jacquet factor $\varepsilon(s, \mu, \psi)$ is Tate's factor $\varepsilon(\mu, s, \psi)$. Propositions 5.2.3 and 5.2.4 of Volume 3 compute it. By Proposition 3.2.3, part 3, it is $\varepsilon(s, \varphi_\mu, \psi)$.

The last sentence. The group $GL_1(K)$ has no proper parabolic subgroup, so each irreducible representation is supercuspidal, and in particular essentially square integrable. Each Weil-Deligne representation of dimension 1 has $N = 0$ and is irreducible. **Remark 3.2.12 (the hypothesis in the Lean comparison of $L$-factors).** The proof of part 2 of Proposition 3.2.3 has two steps, and the Lean file proves them apart. Let $\chi$ be smooth, $\varphi = \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}$ and $\varpi$ a uniformizer. By property 1 there is a geometric Frobenius $\Phi'$ with $\mathrm{art}_K(\varpi) = [\Phi']$. Both $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$ map to the inverse of the Frobenius of $\bar k / k$, so $\Phi'^{-1} \Phi \in I_K$, and $\varphi(\Phi) = \varphi(\Phi') = \chi(\varpi)$. The second step is `Langlands.LocalGL1.charLFactor_eq_lFactor_of_iff`: Tate's factor $L(s, \chi)$ is $L(s, (\varphi, 0))$, under the hypothesis that $\chi$ is trivial on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ if and only if $\varphi$ is trivial on $I_K$. The theorem `Langlands.LocalGL1.unramified_iff` proves the condition from property 1 and the bijectivity of $\mathrm{art}_K$, as follows. Let $\deg : W_K \to \mathbb{Z}$ be the map with $\deg w = n$ when $w$ acts on $\bar k$ as the $n$-th power of the geometric Frobenius, so that a geometric Frobenius has degree 1. Its kernel is $I_K$. This kernel is open, so it is closed, and it contains the commutator subgroup, so it contains its closure. Thus $\deg$ factors through $W_K^{ab}$, and the kernel of the induced map is $I_K^{ab}$. By property 1, $\deg \mathrm{art}_K(\varpi) = 1$. The element $u \varpi$ is also a uniformizer, so $\deg \mathrm{art}_K(u) + 1 = \deg \mathrm{art}_K(u \varpi) = 1$, and $\deg \mathrm{art}_K(u) = 0$. So $\deg \mathrm{art}_K(x) = k$. The claim gives $\mathrm{art}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) \subseteq I_K^{ab}$. Conversely, let $w \in I_K^{ab}$. The degree map on $W_K^{ab}$ is `Langlands.LocalGL1.degreeAb`, and the claim is `Langlands.LocalGL1.degreeAb_art`. So the hypothesis of `charLFactor_eq_lFactor_of_iff` always holds, and Tate's factor is $L(s, (\varphi, 0))$ for each map with the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap`. In the classical treatment the equality is part of local class field theory, and it is the statement on unramified characters of Volume 3, Corollary 2.3.6.

### Tate's local computation

This subsection computes the zeta integrals of a character of $K^\times$, and deduces that the $L$-factor of Godement and Jacquet of $\chi \circ \det$ is Tate's factor $L(s, \chi)$. The computation is in Tate's thesis of 1950, printed as [CF67], Chapter XV, Section 2.5. The book [BH06], Chapter 6, treats the case of $GL_1$ before the case of $GL_2$. We follow the Lean file `Langlands/Local/GL1LFactor.lean`. It does not sum geometric series. It proves two recursions over the shells $\varpi^k \mathcal{O}_K^\times$ instead, and the factor $1 - \chi(\varpi) q^{-s}$ comes out of a single change of variables.

Let $\varpi$ be a uniformizer, $q$ the order of the residue field $k$, $\mathrm{ord} : K^\times \to \mathbb{Z}$ the normalized valuation and $|y| = q^{-\mathrm{ord}(y)}$, with $|0| = 0$. For $N \in \mathbb{Z}$ let $B_N = \varpi^N \mathcal{O}_K$ be the ball $\{y : y = 0 \text{ or } \mathrm{ord}(y) \ge N\}$, and for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ let $S_k = \varpi^k \mathcal{O}_K^\times$ be the shell $\{y \ne 0 : \mathrm{ord}(y) = k\}$. The balls are compact and open, the shells are compact and open, and $B_N$ is the disjoint union of $B_{N+1}$ and $S_N$. Let $\mu$ be an additive Haar measure on $K$, and let $\chi$ be a smooth character of $K^\times$. Extend $\chi$ to $K$ by $\chi(0) = 0$. For a Schwartz-Bruhat function $\phi$ on $K$, that is, a locally constant function with compact support, put
$$Z(\phi, \chi, s) = \int_K \phi(y) \chi(y) |y|^{s - 1} \, d\mu(y).$$
The integrand is $0$ at $y = 0$, and a point has measure $0$. If $\mu$ is the self-dual measure $dx$ of Volume 3, Section 5.1, then $d^\times x = \frac{q}{q - 1} |x|^{-1} dx$ by Volume 3, Definition 5.1.5, so the integral of Volume 3, Definition 5.1.8, is $\frac{q}{q - 1} Z(\phi, \chi, s)$. The additive form is the one that the integral of Godement and Jacquet gives for $n = 1$, and it is the one in Lean. There $\mathrm{ord}$, $B_N$, $S_k$, the extension of $\chi$, the integrand and the integral are `Langlands.LocalGL1.ord`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.ordBall`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.ordShell`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.extChar`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.zetaFn` and `Langlands.LocalGL1.zeta`, and $\varpi$ is the uniformizer `Langlands.LocalGL1.unif`.

**Lemma 3.2.16 (the modulus of $K$).** Let $a \in K^\times$. Then $\mu(aE) = |a| \, \mu(E)$ for each Borel set $E \subseteq K$, and $\int_K g(ay) \, d\mu(y) = |a|^{-1} \int_K g \, d\mu$ for each integrable $g$. In particular $\mu(B_N) = q^{-N} \mu(\mathcal{O}_K)$ and $\mu(S_k) = q^{-k} \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$, with $\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) = (1 - q^{-1}) \mu(\mathcal{O}_K)$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.distribHaarChar_eq`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.measure_ordBall_zero`, `Langlands.LocalGL1.integral_comp_mul_left`

*Proof.* The measure $E \mapsto \mu(aE)$ is invariant under translation, since $a(E + b) = aE + ab$, and it is a Haar measure. By the uniqueness of Haar measure it is $\Delta(a) \mu$ for a number $\Delta(a) > 0$. Clearly $\Delta(ab) = \Delta(a) \Delta(b)$. The ball $\mathcal{O}_K$ is compact and open, so $0 < \mu(\mathcal{O}_K) < \infty$, and we can compute $\Delta$ on it. For a unit $u$ we have $u \mathcal{O}_K = \mathcal{O}_K$, so $\Delta(u) = 1$. The ball $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the disjoint union of the $q$ cosets $b + \varpi \mathcal{O}_K$, where $b$ runs over representatives of $k$. These cosets have the same measure, so $\mu(\mathcal{O}_K) = q \, \mu(\varpi \mathcal{O}_K)$ and $\Delta(\varpi) = q^{-1}$. Each $a \in K^\times$ is $u \varpi^{\mathrm{ord}(a)}$ with $u$ a unit, so $\Delta(a) = q^{-\mathrm{ord}(a)} = |a|$. The formula for the integrals holds for indicator functions, since $\int 1_E(ay) \, d\mu(y) = \mu(a^{-1} E)$. It follows for simple functions by linearity and for integrable functions by the usual limit argument. Finally $B_N = \varpi^N \mathcal{O}_K$, and $\mu(S_k) = \mu(B_k) - \mu(B_{k+1}) = q^{-k} (1 - q^{-1}) \mu(\mathcal{O}_K)$. $\square$

The function $\Delta$ is the modular character `MeasureTheory.distribHaarChar` of Mathlib, and `distribHaarChar_eq` is the formula $\Delta(a) = q^{-\mathrm{ord}(a)}$. The theorem `measure_ordBall_zero` is $\mu(\mathcal{O}_K) = q \, \mu(\varpi \mathcal{O}_K)$.

**Lemma 3.2.17 (the integrand on a shell).** Put $r(\chi, s) = \chi(\varpi) q^{1 - s}$. Let $y \in S_k$, and write $y = \varpi^k u$ with $u \in \mathcal{O}_K^\times$. Then
$$\chi(y) |y|^{s - 1} = r(\chi, s)^k \chi(u).$$
Suppose that $|r(\chi, s)| \le 1$. Then for each Schwartz-Bruhat function $\phi$ and each $N$ the function $\phi \chi |\cdot|^{s-1}$ is bounded on $B_N$, and it is integrable on $B_N$ and on $K$. The condition $|r(\chi, s)| \le 1$ holds for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > s_0(\chi) = 1 + \log_q |\chi(\varpi)|$.

*Proof.* We have $\chi(y) = \chi(\varpi)^k \chi(u)$ and $|y|^{s-1} = q^{-k(s-1)} = (q^{1-s})^k$, which gives the formula. The character $\chi$ is trivial on some $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$, so it takes finitely many values on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$, and $|\chi(u)| \le C$ for all units $u$. A Schwartz-Bruhat function takes finitely many values, so it is bounded. For $y \in B_N$ with $y \ne 0$ we have $k = \mathrm{ord}(y) \ge N$, so $|r|^k \le |r|^N$ when $|r| \le 1$. Thus the integrand is bounded on $B_N$, and $B_N$ has finite measure. The support of $\phi$ is compact and the balls $B_{-n}$ form an increasing open cover of $K$, so the support lies in some $B_{-M}$, and the integrand is integrable on $K$. Finally $|r(\chi, s)| = |\chi(\varpi)| q^{1 - \mathrm{Re}(s)}$, which is at most $1$ if and only if $\mathrm{Re}(s) \ge 1 + \log_q |\chi(\varpi)|$. $\square$

The half plane of the lemma is not the best one. On $S_k$ the integrand of $Z(1_{\mathcal{O}_K}, \chi, s)$ has absolute value $|r|^k$ times a bounded function, while $\mu(S_k) = q^{-k} \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$. So the integral converges absolutely if and only if $|\chi(\varpi) q^{-s}| < 1$, that is, $\mathrm{Re}(s) > \log_q |\chi(\varpi)|$. This is the half plane $\mathrm{Re}(s) > -\sigma(\chi)$ of Volume 3, Proposition 5.1.9. The Lean proof uses the bounded integrand and does not sum the volumes of the shells, and it loses $1$ in the abscissa. This costs nothing, because `Langlands.IsGJLFactor` asks for its identities only for $\mathrm{Re}(s)$ large.

From now on fix $s$ with $|r(\chi, s)| \le 1$, and write $X = q^{-s}$. Then $|\chi(\varpi) X| = |r(\chi, s)| / q < 1$. For a Schwartz-Bruhat function $\phi$ put
$$T_N(\phi) = \int_{B_N} \phi(y) \chi(y) |y|^{s-1} \, d\mu(y), \qquad c_k(\phi) = \int_{S_k} \phi(y) \chi(y) \, d\mu(y).$$
The integral $T_N(\phi)$ depends on $s$. The number $c_k(\phi)$ does not depend on $s$, and it is zero for all but finitely many $k$. In Lean $T_N$ is `Langlands.LocalGL1.ballZeta` and $c_k$ is `Langlands.LocalGL1.shellCoeff`. We write $1$ for the constant function $1$, so that $T_N(1) = Z(1_{B_N}, \chi, s)$.

**Theorem 3.2.18 (Tate's local computation).** Let $\chi$ be a smooth character of $K^\times$, and let $s$ satisfy $|\chi(\varpi) q^{1-s}| \le 1$.
1. For each Schwartz-Bruhat function $\phi$ and each $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, $T_m(\phi) = T_{m+1}(\phi) + c_m(\phi) (q^{1-s})^m$.
2. For each $N \in \mathbb{Z}$, $T_{N+1}(1) = \chi(\varpi) X \, T_N(1)$. So $T_N(1) = (\chi(\varpi) X)^N \, T_0(1)$ for $N \ge 0$.
3. $(1 - \chi(\varpi) X) \, T_0(1) = c_0(1)$, and $1 - \chi(\varpi) X \ne 0$. If $\chi$ is unramified, then $c_0(1) = \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ and
$$Z(1_{\mathcal{O}_K}, \chi, s) = \frac{\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)}{1 - \chi(\varpi) q^{-s}}.$$
If $\chi$ is ramified, then $c_0(1) = 0$ and $Z(1_{\mathcal{O}_K}, \chi, s) = 0$.
4. For each Schwartz-Bruhat function $\phi$ there are a polynomial $A \in \mathbb{C}[X]$ and integers $M, N \ge 0$, which do not depend on $s$, with
$$Z(\phi, \chi, s) = A(X) X^{-M} + \phi(0) \, (\chi(\varpi) X)^N \, Z(1_{\mathcal{O}_K}, \chi, s).$$

$$A(X) = \sum_{j=0}^{M+N-1} q^{j-M} c_{j-M}(\phi) \, X^j,$$

**Corollary 3.2.19 (the span of the zeta integrals).** Let $L(s, \chi) = (1 - \chi(\varpi) q^{-s})^{-1}$ if $\chi$ is unramified, and $L(s, \chi) = 1$ if $\chi$ is ramified. Let $s$ satisfy $|\chi(\varpi) q^{1-s}| \le 1$, and $X = q^{-s}$.

$$Z(\phi, \chi, s) = \bigl( A(X) (1 - \chi(\varpi) X) + \phi(0) \, \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) \, \chi(\varpi)^N X^{N+M} \bigr) X^{-M} L(s, \chi).$$
Part 2. For unramified $\chi$ this is Theorem 3.2.18, part 3. Let $\chi$ be ramified. The set $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ is compact and open, and $\chi^{-1}$ is constant on the cosets of some $1 + \mathfrak{p}_K^m$, so $\phi_\chi$ is locally constant with compact support. On $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ the integrand is $\chi(y)^{-1} \chi(y) |y|^{s-1} = 1$. So $Z(\phi_\chi, \chi, s) = \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$, which is $\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) L(s, \chi)$. In both cases $\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) \ne 0$, since $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ is open and not empty.

Part 3. By part 1 the span lies in $L(s, \chi) \, \mathbb{C}[X, X^{-1}]$, and by part 2 it contains $L(s, \chi)$. It remains to see that the span is stable under multiplication by $X$ and $X^{-1}$. Let $\phi' (y) = \phi(\varpi^{-1} y)$. By Lemma 3.2.16 with $a = \varpi$ and the formula in the proof of Theorem 3.2.18, part 2,
$$Z(\phi', \chi, s) = q^{-1} \int_K \phi(z) \chi(\varpi z) |\varpi z|^{s-1} \, d\mu(z) = \chi(\varpi) X \, Z(\phi, \chi, s).$$
So the span is stable under $X$, and in the same way under $X^{-1}$, with $\phi(\varpi y)$. $\square$

The function $L(s, \chi)$ does not depend on the choice of $\varpi$, because $\chi(\varpi)$ does not depend on it when $\chi$ is unramified. It is Tate's factor $L(\chi, s)$ of Volume 3, Definition 5.2.2. Part 1 gives the meromorphic continuation of each $Z(\phi, \chi, s)$ to the whole plane, as a rational function of $q^{-s}$, and part 3 shows that the poles of the zeta integrals are exactly the poles of $L(s, \chi)$. For ramified $\chi$ the theorem recovers Volume 3, Corollary 5.2.21, without Gauss sums: the zeta integrals are Laurent polynomials, and every Laurent polynomial is one.

**Example 3.2.20 (two computations).** Let $\kappa$ be the unramified quadratic character of Example 3.2.4, so that $\kappa(\varpi) = -1$ and $L(s, \kappa) = (1 + q^{-s})^{-1}$. Theorem 3.2.18 gives $Z(1_{\mathcal{O}_K}, \kappa, s) = \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) / (1 + q^{-s})$. Let $\phi = 1_{\varpi^{-1} \mathcal{O}_K}$. In part 4 we may take $N = 0$ and $M = 1$. The only shell coefficient is $c_{-1}(\phi) = \kappa(\varpi)^{-1} \mu(S_{-1}) = -q \, \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$, so $A(X) = q^{-1} c_{-1}(\phi) = -\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$, and
$$Z(\phi, \kappa, s) = -\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) X^{-1} + \frac{\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)}{1 + X} = -\frac{\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)}{X (1 + X)} = -q^s \, \frac{\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)}{1 + q^{-s}}.$$
This agrees with the proof of Corollary 3.2.19, part 3: $\phi(y) = 1_{\mathcal{O}_K}(\varpi y)$, so $Z(\phi, \kappa, s) = (\kappa(\varpi) X)^{-1} Z(1_{\mathcal{O}_K}, \kappa, s)$. For the normalization of Volume 3, let $\psi$ have conductor $\mathcal{O}_K$ and $\mu$ be the self-dual measure, so that $\mu(\mathcal{O}_K) = 1$ and $\mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) = 1 - q^{-1}$. For an unramified $\chi$, the integral of Volume 3, Definition 5.1.8, is then
$$\frac{q}{q-1} Z(1_{\mathcal{O}_K}, \chi, s) = \frac{q}{q-1} \cdot \frac{1 - q^{-1}}{1 - \chi(\varpi) q^{-s}} = L(s, \chi),$$
which is the first example of Volume 3, Section 5.1, for $d(\psi) = 0$.

**Proposition 3.2.21 (the zeta integral of Godement and Jacquet for $n = 1$).** Let $e : M_1(K) \to K$ be the map $x \mapsto x_{11}$, and let $\mu$ be the image under $e$ of the Haar measure $dx$ of $M_1(K)$. Let $(\pi, V)$ be a smooth representation of $GL_1(K)$ and $\chi$ a smooth character of $K^\times$ with $\pi(g) v = \chi(\det g) v$ for all $g$ and $v$. Let $v \in V$, $\lambda$ a linear form on $V$, $f(g) = \lambda(\pi(g) v)$ and $\Phi$ a Schwartz-Bruhat function on $M_1(K)$. Then
$$\int_{M_1(K)} \Phi(x) f(x) |\det x|^{s - 1} \, dx = \lambda(v) \, Z(\Phi \circ e^{-1}, \chi, s),$$
where the integrand on the left is $0$ off $GL_1(K)$.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.gjZeta_eq_zeta`, with `Langlands.LocalGL1.entryHomeomorph` for $e$

*Proof.* The map $e$ is an isomorphism of topological groups, so $\mu$ is a Haar measure on $K$. For $x \in GL_1(K)$ we have $\det x = x_{11} = e(x)$, and $f(x) = \chi(\det x) \lambda(v)$. So the integrand on the left is $\lambda(v) \, \Phi(x) \chi(e(x)) |e(x)|^{s-1}$ at $x \ne 0$, and it is $0$ at $x = 0$, as is the integrand of $Z$. The change of variables $y = e(x)$ gives the formula. $\square$

The left side is the Lean integral `Langlands.gjZeta` for $n = 1$, which is $\int \Phi(x) f(x) |\det x|^{s + (n-1)/2 - n} \, dx$ with the measure `Langlands.matrixHaar`. Since $|\det x|^{-n} dx$ is a Haar measure on $GL_n(K)$, it is a constant multiple of the integral of Definition 2.5.2. For $n = 1$ the exponent is $s - 1$, as in Tate's additive form.

**Theorem 3.2.22 (the $L$-factor of a character of $GL_1(K)$).** Let $\mathrm{art}_K$ have the properties of `Langlands.IsLocalArtinMap`, let $\chi$ be a smooth character of $K^\times$, and let $(\pi, V)$ be a smooth representation of $GL_1(K)$ with $V \ne 0$ and $\pi(g) v = \chi(\det g) v$ for all $g$ and $v$. Put $L(s) = L(s, (\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}, 0))$ and $X = q^{-s}$.
1. $L(s) = P(X)^{-1}$ for a polynomial $P$ with $P(0) = 1$.
2. For each Schwartz-Bruhat function $\Phi$ on $M_1(K)$, each $v \in V$ and each smooth linear form $\lambda$ on $V$, there are a polynomial $A$, an integer $k \ge 0$ and $s_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ with $Z(\Phi, s, f) = A(X) X^{-k} L(s)$ for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > s_0$, where $f(g) = \lambda(\pi(g) v)$.
3. $L(s)$ is a finite sum $\sum_i A_i(X) X^{-k} Z(\Phi_i, s, f_i)$ for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > s_0$, with polynomials $A_i$ and matrix coefficients $f_i$.

So $L(s)$ is the Godement-Jacquet factor of $\pi$ in the sense of `Langlands.IsGJLFactor`. In particular $L(s, \chi \circ \det) = L(s, \chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1})$ for the factor $L(s, \chi \circ \det)$ of Definition 2.5.4.

Lean: `Langlands.LocalGL1.isGJLFactor_wdOfChar`, with the statement `Langlands.LocalGL1.GL1LFactorNontrivial` and its proof `Langlands.LocalGL1.gl1LFactorNontrivial`

*Proof.* By `lFactor_wdOfChar_eq` and Remark 3.2.12, $L(s)$ is Tate's factor $L(s, \chi)$ of Corollary 3.2.19. This gives part 1, with $P = 1 - \chi(\varpi) X$ if $\chi$ is unramified and $P = 1$ otherwise. Let $\mu$ be as in Proposition 3.2.21. By that proposition, $Z(\Phi, s, f) = \lambda(v) Z(\Phi \circ e^{-1}, \chi, s)$, and $\Phi \circ e^{-1}$ is a Schwartz-Bruhat function on $K$. By Corollary 3.2.19, part 1, with $s_0 = s_0(\chi)$ of Lemma 3.2.17, this is $\lambda(v) B(X) X^{-k} L(s)$, which is part 2. For part 3, choose $v \ne 0$ in $V$, which exists because $V \ne 0$, and a linear form $\lambda$ with $\lambda(v) \ne 0$. The form $\lambda$ is smooth: an element $g$ of the open subgroup $\ker(\chi \circ \det)$ acts trivially on $V$, so it fixes $\lambda$. Let $\Phi = \phi_\chi \circ e$ with $\phi_\chi$ as in Corollary 3.2.19, part 2. Then
$$Z(\Phi, s, f) = \lambda(v) \, \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) \, L(s),$$
and $L(s)$ is $Z(\Phi, s, f)$ divided by the nonzero constant $\lambda(v) \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$. This is part 3 with one term, $k = 0$ and the constant polynomial $A_1 = (\lambda(v) \mu(\mathcal{O}_K^\times))^{-1}$. The last sentence follows from Theorem 2.5.3, part 2, since parts 1 to 3 say that $L(s)$ generates the span of the zeta integrals and has the form $P(q^{-s})^{-1}$ with $P(0) = 1$. $\square$

The theorem holds for every nonzero $\pi$ on which $GL_1(K)$ acts by $\chi \circ \det$, of any dimension, and not only for the irreducible ones. Such a $\pi$ is a direct sum of copies of the line $\chi$, and each zeta integral of $\pi$ is a zeta integral of $\chi$. The theorem is the one point where the proof uses that $V$ is not zero: in part 3 we need a vector $v$ and a form $\lambda$ with $\lambda(v) \ne 0$. In Lean, `Module.Projective.exists_dual_ne_zero` gives the form $\lambda$, and `isGJLFactor_wdOfChar` has the hypothesis `[Nontrivial π.V]`.

**Remark 3.2.23 (the zero representation).** The zero space $V = 0$, with the trivial action, is a smooth representation of $GL_1(K)$. It satisfies $\pi(g) v = \chi(\det g) v$ for every character $\chi$, since both sides are $0$. Each of its matrix coefficients is $0$, so each zeta integral is $0$, and the span of the zeta integrals is the zero space. This span is not a fractional ideal that contains $1$, and part 3 of Theorem 3.2.22 fails: it would give $L(s) = 0$ for $\mathrm{Re}(s)$ large, while $L(s) = P(q^{-s})^{-1}$ is never $0$. So the theory of Godement and Jacquet says nothing about the zero representation, and this is one reason why Theorem 2.5.3 assumes that $\pi$ is irreducible. An irreducible representation is not zero by definition, in the same way as $1$ is not a prime number. For a reducible $\pi \ne 0$ the span still makes sense, but it need not have the properties of Theorem 2.5.3. For $GL_1$ Theorem 3.2.22 covers every nonzero $\pi$ that acts by a character, so the hypothesis $V \ne 0$ is the only part of irreducibility that the proof uses.

This is why the first Lean formulation was false. The proposition `Langlands.LocalGL1.GL1LFactor` of `GL1.lean` asks that $L(s, (\chi \circ \mathrm{art}_K^{-1}, 0))$ be the Godement-Jacquet factor of each smooth $\pi$ on which $g$ acts by $\chi(\det g)$, and the zero representation is such a $\pi$. The theorem `Langlands.LocalGL1.not_gl1LFactor` proves the negation. It takes $K = \mathbb{Q}_2$, the map $\mathrm{art}_K$ of `Langlands.exists_isLocalArtinMap`, the trivial character $\chi = 1$ and $\pi = 0$. Then the third clause of `Langlands.IsGJLFactor` gives $L(s) = 0$ for real $s$ large, while $L(s) = (1 - 2^{-s})^{-1}$. The fix is the hypothesis `Nontrivial π.V`, which gives the statement `Langlands.LocalGL1.GL1LFactorNontrivial`, and Theorem 3.2.22 proves it. The implication `localLanglandsGL_one_of_gl1LFactor` of Theorem 3.2.14 was correct, but its hypothesis was false. In Lean, `not_gl1LFactor` is proved for the universe level $0$, which is the level of $\mathbb{Q}_2$.
