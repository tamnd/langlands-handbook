## The principal series of GL(2)

This section classifies the irreducible representations of $G = GL_2(K)$ that are not supercuspidal. The result is the classification of [BH06], Chapter 3: each irreducible representation of $G$ is an irreducible principal series, a character, a twist of the Steinberg representation, or a supercuspidal representation. Let $B$ be the upper triangular Borel subgroup, $T$ the diagonal torus, $N$ the upper unipotent group and $K_0 = GL_2(\mathcal{O}_K)$. For characters $\chi_1, \chi_2$ of $K^\times$ we write $\chi = \chi_1 \otimes \chi_2$ for the character $\mathrm{diag}(a, d) \mapsto \chi_1(a) \chi_2(d)$ of $T$, and $\chi^w = \chi_2 \otimes \chi_1$.

### The principal series

By Definition 1.3.1, the principal series $\chi_1 \times \chi_2 = \mathrm{Ind}_B^G \chi$ is the space of the locally constant functions $f : G \to \mathbb{C}$ with
$$f\left(\begin{pmatrix} a & x \\ 0 & d \end{pmatrix} g\right) = \chi_1(a) \chi_2(d) \left|\frac{a}{d}\right|^{1/2} f(g).$$
This is the space of `Langlands.principalSeries K χ₁ χ₂` in the Lean project.

Lean: `Langlands.principalSeries`, `Langlands.principalSeriesInd`, `Langlands.normPrincipalSeries`, `Langlands.normPrincipalSeries_eq`, `Langlands.normPrincipalSeries_iso`. The first is normalized as here, and the second is the unnormalized induction from `Langlands.GL2.borel`. The third is $\mathrm{Ind}_B^G((\chi_1 \otimes \chi_2) \delta_B^{1/2})$, built with `SmoothRep.ind` and `Langlands.GL2.sqrtModulus`. Lean proves that it is equal to `principalSeriesInd` of $\chi_1 |\cdot|^{1/2}$ and $\chi_2 |\cdot|^{-1/2}$, and that it is isomorphic to `principalSeries K χ₁ χ₂`. So the three forms describe the same representation $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$.

Its central character is $\chi_1 \chi_2$. By Proposition 1.3.4 it is admissible. A direct proof for $GL_2$ is short. By the Iwasawa decomposition $G = B K_0$ of Section 1.3, a function in $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is determined by its restriction to $K_0$. If it is fixed by an open normal subgroup $J$ of $K_0$, it is determined by its values on a set of representatives of the finite set $K_0 / J$. So the space of $J$-fixed vectors has dimension at most $[K_0 : J]$.

Lean: `Langlands.principalSeries_isAdmissible`, `Langlands.normPrincipalSeries_isAdmissible`, `Langlands.principalSeriesInd_isAdmissible`, `Langlands.principalSeriesInd_nontrivial`. The admissibility comes from `Langlands.SmoothRep.ind_isAdmissible` and `Langlands.GL2.iwasawa`, and the last theorem says that the principal series is not zero.

The contragredient of $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is $\chi_1^{-1} \times \chi_2^{-1}$, and $(\chi_1 \times \chi_2) \otimes \eta = \chi_1 \eta \times \chi_2 \eta$ for a character $\eta$ of $K^\times$. By Theorem 1.3.11, the case of $GL_2$ of Theorem 1.3.8, its Jacquet module sits in the exact sequence
$$0 \to \chi^w \to r_B(\chi_1 \times \chi_2) \to \chi \to 0. \tag{1}$$

Lean: `Langlands.principalSeriesJacquet_shortExact`, `Langlands.bigCellJacquet_iso`, `Langlands.finrank_principalSeriesJacquet`. These prove Theorem 1.3.11 for the unnormalized forms, in the file `Langlands/Smooth/JacquetPrincipal.lean`: the sequence (1), with the sub $(V_w)_N$ identified with the character `Langlands.bigCellChar`, and the dimension $2$ of $r_B(\chi_1 \times \chi_2)$. Section 1.3 explains how the characters agree with (1). Of the theorems below, only the irreducibility in part 1 of Theorem 1.5.1 is in Lean.

The two lines of (1) can be seen on functions. Let $V_w \subset \chi_1 \times \chi_2$ be the subspace of the functions that vanish at $1$, as in Proposition 1.3.10. The map $r_B(\chi_1 \times \chi_2) \to \chi$ is induced by $f \mapsto f(1)$, and by the proof of Theorem 1.3.11 the line $\chi^w$ is the image of $V_w$, detected by the linear form $\lambda(f) = \int_K f(w n(x)) \, dx$ on $V_w$. Sometimes the integral converges on all of $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$. For $f \in \chi_1 \times \chi_2$ and $|x|$ large, identity (4) of Section 1.3 gives
$$f(w n(x)) = \chi_1(-1) \, \chi_1^{-1} \chi_2(x) \, |x|^{-1} f(1).$$
Suppose that $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1}$ is unramified with $|\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1}(\varpi)| < 1$. On the shell $|x| = q^k$ the integral of $\chi_1^{-1} \chi_2(x) |x|^{-1}$ is $(1 - q^{-1}) (\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1}(\varpi))^k$, so the integral over $K$ converges absolutely for each $f$. Then $\Lambda(f) = \int_K f(w n(x)) \, dx$ is a linear form on all of $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$. The computation of the proof of Theorem 1.3.11 shows that it is invariant under $N$ and that $\Lambda(t \cdot f) = \chi^w \delta_B^{1/2}(t) \Lambda(f)$. So it factors through a map $r_B(\chi_1 \times \chi_2) \to \chi^w$, which is not zero on the image of $V_w$. It splits the sequence (1). The form $\Lambda$ is the value at $1$ of the intertwining operator of Proposition 1.5.2, $\Lambda(f) = M(\chi) f(1)$.

The character $\chi \circ \det$ of $G$, restricted to $T$ and normalized, gives the Jacquet module $r_B(\chi \circ \det) = \delta_B^{-1/2} \cdot (\chi \circ \det)|_T = \chi |\cdot|^{-1/2} \otimes \chi |\cdot|^{1/2}$. By Theorem 1.3.3, $\chi \circ \det$ is a subrepresentation of $\chi |\cdot|^{-1/2} \times \chi |\cdot|^{1/2}$. Indeed the function $g \mapsto \chi(\det g)$ satisfies the transformation law, since $\chi(ad) = \chi(a) |a|^{-1/2} \chi(d) |d|^{1/2} |a/d|^{1/2}$.

**Theorem 1.5.1 (reducibility of the principal series).** Let $\chi_1, \chi_2$ be characters of $K^\times$.
1. The representation $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is irreducible if and only if $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} \ne |\cdot|^{\pm 1}$.
2. If $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} = |\cdot|^{-1}$, write $\chi_1 = \eta |\cdot|^{-1/2}$ and $\chi_2 = \eta |\cdot|^{1/2}$. Then $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ has length two. Its unique irreducible subrepresentation is $\eta \circ \det$, and its quotient is infinite-dimensional.
3. If $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} = |\cdot|$, write $\chi_1 = \eta |\cdot|^{1/2}$ and $\chi_2 = \eta |\cdot|^{-1/2}$. Then $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ has length two. Its unique irreducible quotient is $\eta \circ \det$, and its subrepresentation is infinite-dimensional.

Lean: `Langlands.normPrincipalSeries_irreducible`, `Langlands.principalSeriesInd_isIrreducible`. These prove the "if" direction of part 1, in the file `Langlands/Smooth/IrreducibilityCriterion.lean`. The first has the hypotheses $\chi_1 \ne \chi_2 |\cdot|$ and $\chi_2 \ne \chi_1 |\cdot|$, which together say $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} \ne |\cdot|^{\pm 1}$. The second is the same result for the unnormalized induction. The Lean proof is the second proof below, not the proof with Whittaker functionals. The "only if" direction and parts 2 and 3 are not in Lean.

*Proof.* We use two facts on Whittaker functionals from Section 1.7. Fix a nontrivial character $\psi$ of $K$, viewed as a character of $N$. The functor $V \mapsto V_{N, \psi}$ of twisted coinvariants is exact, by the argument of Proposition 1.3.5. By Theorem 1.7.4, an irreducible representation $\sigma$ of $G$ has $\dim \sigma_{N, \psi} = 1$ if $\sigma$ is infinite-dimensional and $\sigma_{N, \psi} = 0$ if $\sigma$ is a character. By Propositions 1.3.9 and 1.3.10, the representation $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ contains the space $V_w$ of the functions supported on the open cell $B w N$, with quotient the character $\chi \delta_B^{1/2}$ of $B$ given by evaluation at $1$. The quotient has trivial twisted coinvariants, since $N$ acts trivially on it. By part 3 of Proposition 1.3.10, the functions on the open cell are the functions $f(b w n) = \chi \delta_B^{1/2}(b) \phi(n)$ with $\phi \in C_c^\infty(N)$, and their $(N, \psi)$-coinvariants are spanned by the image of $\phi \mapsto \int_N \phi(n) \psi^{-1}(n) \, dn$. So $\dim (\chi_1 \times \chi_2)_{N, \psi} = 1$.

Every irreducible subquotient of $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ has a nonzero Jacquet module, by Theorem 1.4.6. By the exactness of the Jacquet functor, Proposition 1.3.5, and by (1), $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ has length at most $2$. Suppose that it is reducible, with irreducible subrepresentation $U$ and irreducible quotient $Q$. By exactness of the twisted coinvariants, exactly one of $U$ and $Q$ has a one-dimensional space of twisted coinvariants, and the other has none. So one of $U$ and $Q$ is a character $\eta \circ \det$. If it is $U$, then by Theorem 1.3.3 its Jacquet module $\eta |\cdot|^{-1/2} \otimes \eta |\cdot|^{1/2}$ maps onto $\chi$, so $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} = |\cdot|^{-1}$. If it is $Q$, apply the same argument to the contragredient: $Q^\vee = \eta^{-1} \circ \det$ is a subrepresentation of $\chi_1^{-1} \times \chi_2^{-1}$, so $\chi_1^{-1} \chi_2 = |\cdot|^{-1}$. This proves that $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is irreducible when $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} \ne |\cdot|^{\pm 1}$.

Conversely, in case 2 the function $\eta \circ \det$ lies in $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$, as we saw above. The quotient is infinite-dimensional and irreducible, by the length bound. The subrepresentation $\eta \circ \det$ is the only irreducible one. Indeed, $r_B(\eta \circ \det) = \chi$, so by (1) an infinite-dimensional irreducible subrepresentation $U'$ has $r_B(U') = \chi^w$. Theorem 1.3.3 then gives a nonzero map $\chi^w \to \chi$, but $\chi^w \ne \chi$ since $\chi_1 \ne \chi_2$. In case 3, $\chi_1^{-1} \times \chi_2^{-1}$ is as in case 2 for $\eta^{-1}$, so it contains $\eta^{-1} \circ \det$ as its only irreducible subrepresentation. By Proposition 1.3.4, $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is its contragredient. By Proposition 1.1.6, the quotients of an admissible representation correspond to the subrepresentations of its contragredient. So $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ has $\eta \circ \det$ as its only irreducible quotient. $\square$

### A second proof of the irreducibility

The proof above uses the uniqueness of Whittaker functionals, which is a deep result. The "if" direction of part 1 has a more elementary proof. It uses only the big cell, Theorem 1.3.11 and the Fourier transform on $K$. The Lean project follows this proof. We write $V = \chi_1 \times \chi_2$, so that $f(bg) = \chi \delta_B^{1/2}(b) f(g)$ for $b \in B$. Let $V_w$ and $\phi_f(x) = f(w n(x))$ be as in Proposition 1.3.10. By part 3 of that proposition, $f \mapsto \phi_f$ is a bijection from $V_w$ onto $C_c^\infty(K)$.

**Step 1: the action on the big cell.** Let $f \in V_w$, $y \in K$ and $t = \mathrm{diag}(a, d) \in T$. Then $\phi_{n(y) f}(x) = \phi_f(x + y)$. Also $t^{-1} n(x) t = n(x d / a)$ and $w t = \mathrm{diag}(d, a) w$, so
$$\phi_{t f}(x) = f(w t \, n(x d/a)) = \chi^w \delta_B^{-1/2}(t) \, \phi_f(x d / a).$$
Thus $N$ acts on $C_c^\infty(K)$ by translations, and $T$ acts by dilations times a scalar. Let $W \subseteq V$ be a $G$-stable subspace and let $Y = \{\phi_f : f \in W \cap V_w\}$. Then $Y$ is a subspace of $C_c^\infty(K)$ that is stable under the translations $\phi \mapsto \phi(\cdot + y)$ and the dilations $\phi \mapsto \phi(\cdot \, a)$ for $a \in K^\times$.

**Step 2: a lemma on test functions.** *If $Y \subseteq C_c^\infty(K)$ is a nonzero subspace that is stable under translations and dilations, then $Y$ contains each $\phi \in C_c^\infty(K)$ with $\int_K \phi(x) \, dx = 0$.* Fix a nontrivial character $\psi$ of $K$, and let $\hat\phi(\xi) = \int_K \phi(x) \psi(x \xi) \, dx$. The Fourier transform is a bijection of $C_c^\infty(K)$ onto itself, by Tate's local theory. First, $Y$ is stable under convolution with each $g \in C_c^\infty(K)$. Indeed, choose $r$ so large that $\phi$ is invariant under $\mathfrak{p}^r$ and $g$ is constant on the cosets of $\mathfrak{p}^r$. Then $g$ is a finite sum $\sum_b c_b 1_{b + \mathfrak{p}^r}$, and $g * \phi = \mathrm{vol}(\mathfrak{p}^r) \sum_b c_b \, \phi(\cdot - b)$. Since $\widehat{g * \phi} = \hat g \hat\phi$, the space $\hat Y$ is stable under multiplication by each function of $C_c^\infty(K)$. Let $U$ be the set of the points $\xi$ with $h(\xi) \ne 0$ for some $h \in \hat Y$. If $h(\xi_0) \ne 0$, then $h$ is constant on a ball $\xi_0 + \mathfrak{p}^M$, and $1_{\xi_0 + \mathfrak{p}^M} = h \cdot h(\xi_0)^{-1} 1_{\xi_0 + \mathfrak{p}^M}$ is in $\hat Y$. A function of $C_c^\infty(U)$ is a finite combination of indicators of balls, each in such a ball, since two balls of $K$ are disjoint or one contains the other. So $\hat Y = C_c^\infty(U)$. The dilation $\phi \mapsto \phi(\cdot \, a)$ changes $\hat\phi$ to $|a|^{-1} \hat\phi(\cdot \, a^{-1})$, so $U$ is stable under $K^\times$. The set $U$ is open and not empty, so it contains a point other than $0$, and thus $U \supseteq K \setminus \{0\}$. Now let $\int_K \phi = 0$. Then $\hat\phi(0) = 0$, and the locally constant function $\hat\phi$ vanishes near $0$. So $\hat\phi \in C_c^\infty(K \setminus \{0\}) \subseteq \hat Y$, and $\phi \in Y$.

**Step 3: if $W \cap V_w \ne 0$, then $V(N) \subseteq W$.** Let $f \in V$ and $y \in K$, and let $g = n(y) f - f$. Then $g(1) = f(n(y)) - f(1) = 0$, so $g \in V_w$ and $\phi_g = \phi_f(\cdot + y) - \phi_f$, where $\phi_f(x) = f(w n(x))$ is locally constant but need not have compact support. By (4) of Section 1.3, $\phi_f(x)$ is a fixed character of $x$ times $|x|^{-1} f(1)$ for $|x|$ large. If $|x|$ is large, then $(x + y)/x$ is near $1$, so $\phi_f(x + y) = \phi_f(x)$. Take a ball $\mathfrak{p}^{-R}$ that contains $y$ and outside of which this holds. Then $\int_K \phi_g = \int_{\mathfrak{p}^{-R}} (\phi_f(x + y) - \phi_f(x)) \, dx = 0$, since $\mathfrak{p}^{-R} + y = \mathfrak{p}^{-R}$. By Step 2, $\phi_g \in Y$, so $g \in W$. The vectors $n f - f$ span $V(N)$, so $V(N) \subseteq W$.

**Step 4: $SL_2(K)$ acts trivially on $V / W$.** Let $S = \{g \in G : g v - v \in W \text{ for each } v \in V\}$. Since $W$ is $G$-stable, $S$ is a normal subgroup of $G$. For example, $h g h^{-1} v - v = h(g(h^{-1} v) - h^{-1} v)$. By Step 3, $N \subseteq S$. So $S$ contains $w N w^{-1}$, the lower unipotent group, and these two groups generate $SL_2(K)$ (Exercise 21).

**Step 5: the case $W \cap V_w \ne 0$.** Suppose also that $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} \ne |\cdot|$, and that $W \ne 0$. The line $(V_w)_N \subseteq V_N$ has the character $\chi^w \delta_B^{1/2}$ by Theorem 1.3.11. On $s = \mathrm{diag}(a, a^{-1})$ its value is $\beta = \chi_2 \chi_1^{-1}(a) |a|$. By hypothesis we can choose $a$ with $\beta \ne 1$. Let $f_1 \in V_w$ have nonzero image in $V_N$. Then $s f_1 - \beta f_1 \in V(N) \subseteq W$, and $s f_1 - f_1 \in W$ by Step 4. So $(\beta - 1) f_1 \in W$ and $f_1 \in W$. Since $W \ne 0$, there is $f_0 \in W$ with $f_0(g) \ne 0$ for some $g$. Then $g f_0 \in W$ and $(g f_0)(1) \ne 0$. By (1), $V_N$ has dimension $2$, and the images of $g f_0$ and $f_1$ span it, since evaluation at $1$ vanishes on $V_w$ but not on $g f_0$. So $V = W + V(N) = W$.

**Step 6: the case $W \cap V_w = 0$.** Suppose that $W \ne 0$. For $f \in W$ we have $n f - f \in W \cap V_w = 0$, so $N$ fixes each vector of $W$. The vectors of $W$ are then fixed by the normal subgroup generated by $N$, which contains $SL_2(K)$. Choose $f \in W$ with $f(1) \ne 0$, as in Step 5. For $s = \mathrm{diag}(a, a^{-1})$ we get $f(1) = (s f)(1) = f(s) = \chi \delta_B^{1/2}(s) f(1)$, so $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1}(a) |a| = 1$ for each $a$. That is, $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} = |\cdot|^{-1}$.

Now let $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} \ne |\cdot|^{\pm 1}$, and let $W \ne 0$ be a $G$-stable subspace. Step 6 shows that $W \cap V_w \ne 0$, and Step 5 then gives $W = V$. So $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is irreducible. Step 6 also shows the reducible case 2 directly: there $W$ is fixed by $SL_2(K)$, which is the form of the character $\eta \circ \det$.

The Lean proof of Step 2 does not use the Fourier transform. It works with the sums of the translates of a function over the classes of $\mathfrak{p}^m / \mathfrak{p}^{m + 1}$, and it compares the levels of invariance and of support. Lean: `Langlands.IsAffineStable.translate_sub_mem`, `Langlands.jacquetKer_le_of_inf_ne_bot`, `Langlands.eq_top_of_jacquetKer_le`, `Langlands.eq_of_inf_principalSeriesBigCell_eq_bot`. These are Steps 2, 3, 5 and 6, for the unnormalized induction. The group $S$ of Step 4 is `Langlands.relStab`.

The docstring of `Langlands.principalSeries` states these facts in the following form: $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is irreducible unless $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} = |\cdot|^{\pm 1}$; when $\chi_1 = \chi_2 |\cdot|$ its unique irreducible subrepresentation is $\mathrm{St} \otimes (\chi_2 |\cdot|^{1/2}) \circ \det$; when $\chi_1 = \chi_2 |\cdot|^{-1}$ it is $(\chi_2 |\cdot|^{-1/2}) \circ \det$. With $\eta = \chi_2 |\cdot|^{1/2}$ in case 3 and $\eta = \chi_2 |\cdot|^{-1/2}$ in case 2, this is Theorem 1.5.1 together with Definition 1.5.3 below. The docstring is a comment. The Lean project proves the irreducibility in part 1, but it does not state parts 2 and 3.

**Proposition 1.5.2 (the intertwining operator).** If $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is irreducible, then $\chi_1 \times \chi_2 \cong \chi_2 \times \chi_1$. In all cases the two representations have the same irreducible subquotients.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* By (1), the Jacquet module of $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ has a quotient $\chi$ and a sub $\chi^w$. If $\chi \ne \chi^w$, the sequence splits. Indeed $T(\mathcal{O}_K)$ is compact and acts semisimply, and $\mathrm{diag}(\varpi, 1)$ and $\mathrm{diag}(1, \varpi)$ act through their generalized eigenspaces, which separate two different characters. Then $\chi^w$ is also a quotient of $r_B(\chi_1 \times \chi_2)$. By Theorem 1.3.3 there is a nonzero map $\chi_1 \times \chi_2 \to \chi_2 \times \chi_1$. If $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is irreducible, the map is injective, and by comparing Jacquet modules, which both have length two, its image is everything. If $\chi = \chi^w$ there is nothing to prove. The statement on subquotients follows from the case analysis of Theorem 1.5.1. $\square$

The map of the proof can be written as an integral. For $\mathrm{Re}$ of $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1}$ large enough, $M(\chi) f(g) = \int_N f(w n g) \, dn$ converges and defines a nonzero intertwining operator $\chi_1 \times \chi_2 \to \chi_2 \times \chi_1$. It continues meromorphically in the unramified twists, and its normalization is controlled by the local factors of Chapter 2. On the spherical vector of an unramified principal series it acts by the scalar $L(0, \chi_1 \chi_2^{-1}) / L(1, \chi_1 \chi_2^{-1})$. This is the local form of the constant term computation of Volume 4 for Eisenstein series.

### The Steinberg representation

**Definition 1.5.3 (Steinberg representation).** The *Steinberg representation* $\mathrm{St}$ of $GL_2(K)$ is the irreducible quotient of $|\cdot|^{-1/2} \times |\cdot|^{1/2}$ by the trivial representation. Equivalently, it is the irreducible subrepresentation of $|\cdot|^{1/2} \times |\cdot|^{-1/2}$. For a character $\eta$ of $K^\times$ the *twisted Steinberg representation* is $\eta \mathrm{St} = \mathrm{St} \otimes (\eta \circ \det)$.

Lean: not stated yet

The two descriptions agree. By Theorem 1.5.1 the representation $|\cdot|^{1/2} \times |\cdot|^{-1/2}$ has the trivial representation as its quotient, and its sub is infinite-dimensional. The contragredient of the sub is a quotient of the contragredient $|\cdot|^{-1/2} \times |\cdot|^{1/2}$ and is infinite-dimensional. So it is the Steinberg quotient, and the sub is $\mathrm{St}^\vee$. But $\mathrm{St}^\vee \cong \mathrm{St} \otimes \omega_{\mathrm{St}}^{-1} = \mathrm{St}$, by the remark after Proposition 1.1.6.

The representation $|\cdot|^{-1/2} \times |\cdot|^{1/2}$ is the space of locally constant functions on $B \backslash G = \mathbb{P}^1(K)$. So $\mathrm{St}$ is the space of locally constant functions on $\mathbb{P}^1(K)$ modulo the constants. This is the representation of the introduction to this chapter.

**Proposition 1.5.4 (properties of the Steinberg representation).**
1. $\mathrm{St}$ has trivial central character, and $\eta \mathrm{St}$ has central character $\eta^2$.
2. $r_B(\mathrm{St}) = |\cdot|^{1/2} \otimes |\cdot|^{-1/2}$.
3. $\mathrm{St}^{K_0} = 0$. If $I$ is the Iwahori subgroup of the matrices in $K_0$ that are upper triangular modulo $\mathfrak{p}$, then $\dim \mathrm{St}^I = 1$.
4. The matrix coefficients of $\mathrm{St}$ are square integrable on $G / Z$, and they do not have compact support modulo $Z$.
5. $\eta \mathrm{St} \cong \eta' \mathrm{St}$ if and only if $\eta = \eta'$.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Part 1 is clear. Part 2 follows from (1) and from $r_B(1) = |\cdot|^{-1/2} \otimes |\cdot|^{1/2}$. For part 3, the space of $K_0$-fixed vectors of the functions on $\mathbb{P}^1(K)$ is the line of the constants, since $K_0$ acts transitively on $\mathbb{P}^1(K) = \mathbb{P}^1(\mathcal{O}_K)$; by Proposition 1.1.4 the quotient has no fixed vectors. The Iwahori group has two orbits on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathcal{O}_K)$, the points that reduce to $\infty$ and the others. So the $I$-fixed functions form a space of dimension two, and $\dim \mathrm{St}^I = 1$. Part 4 is a computation with the Cartan decomposition and the Iwahori-Hecke algebra. Let $c$ be a coefficient of $I$-fixed vectors with $c(1) \ne 0$. Modulo $Z$ we have $\mathrm{diag}(\varpi, 1) = s \omega$ and $\mathrm{diag}(1, \varpi) = \omega s$, with $s$ and $\omega$ as in Exercise 10. On $\mathrm{St}^I$ the operator $1_{I s I}$ acts by $-1$, and $1_{I \omega I}$ acts by $-1$, since $\omega$ exchanges the two $I$-orbits on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathcal{O}_K)$. So $c(\mathrm{diag}(\varpi^m, 1)) = q^{-|m|} c(1)$, and $c$ is bounded by a constant times $q^{-|m|}$ on $K_0 \mathrm{diag}(\varpi^m, 1) K_0$, which has volume $\sim q^{|m|}$. So $|c|^2$ is integrable on $G / Z$, and $c$ does not have compact support modulo $Z$. Since $\mathrm{St}$ is irreducible, each coefficient is a finite combination of left and right translates of $c$, so each coefficient is square integrable. For part 5, compare the Jacquet modules: $r_B(\eta \mathrm{St}) = \eta |\cdot|^{1/2} \otimes \eta |\cdot|^{-1/2}$ determines $\eta$. $\square$

An irreducible representation whose matrix coefficients are square integrable modulo the center, after a twist by a character, is *essentially square integrable*, or a *discrete series* representation up to twist. By part 4, the representations $\eta \mathrm{St}$ are essentially square integrable and not supercuspidal. The other essentially square integrable representations of $GL_2(K)$ are the supercuspidal ones. This is the notion of `Langlands.IsDiscreteSeries` of the Lean project, used in Section 3.5.

### The classification for GL(2)

**Theorem 1.5.5 (the irreducible representations of $GL_2$).** Each irreducible smooth representation of $GL_2(K)$ is isomorphic to exactly one of the following.
1. An irreducible principal series $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ with $\chi_1 \chi_2^{-1} \ne |\cdot|^{\pm 1}$. Here $\chi_1 \times \chi_2 \cong \chi_1' \times \chi_2'$ if and only if $\{\chi_1, \chi_2\} = \{\chi_1', \chi_2'\}$.
2. A character $\eta \circ \det$.
3. A twisted Steinberg representation $\eta \mathrm{St}$.
4. A supercuspidal representation.

Lean: not stated yet

*Proof.* Let $\pi$ be irreducible. If $\pi$ is not supercuspidal, then by Theorem 1.4.5 it embeds in some $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$, since the only proper parabolic subgroup up to conjugation is $B$. If $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ is irreducible, we are in case 1. Otherwise, by Theorem 1.5.1, $\pi$ is the irreducible sub of a reducible principal series, so $\pi$ is a character or a twisted Steinberg representation. The four cases are disjoint: supercuspidal representations have zero Jacquet module, the characters are the finite-dimensional ones, and the twisted Steinberg representations have one-dimensional Jacquet modules while the principal series have two-dimensional ones. The isomorphism classes within each case are separated by the Jacquet modules, by (1) and Proposition 1.5.4, and by Proposition 1.5.2. $\square$

**Example 1.5.6 (the unramified and Iwahori-spherical representations).** The principal series $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ has a nonzero $K_0$-fixed vector if and only if $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$ are unramified. The space $V^{K_0}$ is then spanned by the function $f_0$ of Example 1.2.7. By Corollary 1.2.6, the irreducible unramified representations are the irreducible $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ with $\chi_1, \chi_2$ unramified, and the unramified characters $\eta \circ \det$. The Steinberg twists $\eta \mathrm{St}$ with $\eta$ unramified are not unramified, but they have an Iwahori-fixed vector, by Proposition 1.5.4. Borel and Casselman proved that the irreducible representations with an Iwahori-fixed vector are exactly the irreducible subquotients of the unramified principal series. For an elliptic curve $E$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ with good reduction at $p$, the local component at $p$ of the automorphic representation of $E$ is an unramified principal series. At a prime of multiplicative reduction it is a twist $\eta \mathrm{St}$, with $\eta$ unramified, and $\eta(p) = \pm 1$ in the unitary normalization, the sign that distinguishes split from nonsplit reduction.

The unitary irreducible representations of $GL_2(K)$ are also known. The principal series $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ with $\chi_1, \chi_2$ unitary is unitary by Proposition 1.3.4. The representations $\chi |\cdot|^s \times \chi |\cdot|^{-s}$ with $\chi$ unitary and $0 < s < 1/2$ are unitary too, for a different inner product. They form the *complementary series*. The Ramanujan conjecture for a cusp form says that its local components are never in the complementary series. Volume 4 discusses this conjecture for holomorphic forms, where Deligne proved it.
